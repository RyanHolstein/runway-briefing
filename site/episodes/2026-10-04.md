Episode Title: Holiday Fares Spike, United Picks a Wi-Fi Fight With Delta, and Vegas Can't Catch a Break
Episode Summary: Thanksgiving and Christmas airfares are hitting decade highs as airlines quietly shrink their schedules — we break down why "controlled capacity" is code for higher prices. Plus, United takes a very public shot at Delta over inflight Wi-Fi, flight attendants push back on a "too sick to vacation" memo, and Las Vegas can't figure out why cheaper rooms aren't bringing people back.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Sunday, October 4th. Let's get into it.

So, holiday travel. If you haven't booked Thanksgiving or Christmas flights yet, sit down.

Hopper's data is out, and domestic round trip fares for Thanksgiving are averaging four-oh-two bucks. That's up 31% from last year. Christmas is up 23%, averaging $452. Both are tracking to be the priciest holiday travel in a decade.

Here's the thing — it's not really about demand. Demand's actually fine, which is exactly the problem. Airlines have trimmed about 3% of seats between late November and New Year's. Sounds tiny, right? But when a schedule's already running close to full, cutting 3% doesn't leave empty seats, it leaves expensive ones.

United's cutting December flying and says more is coming. Southwest literally cut its planned capacity growth in half. And United's own CFO said the quiet part out loud — they're not flying to chase market share anymore, they're flying to protect margins. Which, fair, it's a business. But it also means the industry has figured out that flying a slightly emptier plane for more money beats flying a full plane for less. Flighty's Katy Nastro put it well — the average traveler might just get priced out of the holidays entirely. And with jet fuel sitting at four-forty a gallon, nobody's in a rush to fix that math.

Speaking of airlines fighting over money — United and Delta are having a moment. United rolled out a new status match this week, and it's clearly aimed right at Delta. The pitch is basically, "come get real Wi-Fi."

This comes right after some leaked comments from Delta CEO Ed Bastian about why Delta passed on Starlink — apparently it got into some "it's complicated working with Elon Musk" territory. United saw blood in the water and named the promo "Switch to Better Wi-Fi." Subtle.

Look, United is actually ahead here — they're rolling out Starlink fleet-wide, done by 2027. Delta picked Amazon's Leo service, which, fine, but it's unproven in the air and lagging behind. American's also doing Starlink starting next year but they played it cooler, just pushing their standard status match. United's the one turning a Wi-Fi vendor decision into a rivalry. Honestly? Kind of a genius marketing move, even if it's a little petty.

Speaking of United being United — their flight attendants are not happy right now. A memo went out telling the nearly 30,000-person crew that if you're too sick to work, you're too sick for leisure travel on standby. Which sounds reasonable until you remember these are the same non-rev travel benefits flight attendants have used for literally decades.

The union's response was basically, "cute memo, but read the contract." Turns out there's nothing in there that actually bans sick flight attendants from flying standby, and there's a long-standing understanding that differs from what the memo says. United can still investigate individual cases, sure. But telling 30,000 people a blanket rule that isn't actually in their contract is a bold way to start a fight you're probably going to lose.

Last one — Las Vegas. Rooms are the cheapest they've been all year, average rate down to $150 a night. And visitors still dropped over 4% in August. That should not happen at the same time.

The airport numbers are the real tell — passenger traffic at Harry Reid was down over 9%, and domestic traffic specifically fell more than 10%. Part of that is Spirit's collapse finally showing up in the data, since Vegas was one of their biggest markets. So it's not really a calendar quirk or a soft month — it's a low-cost carrier disappearing and taking its passengers with it. When your cheapest flights vanish, cheap hotel rooms don't matter much.
