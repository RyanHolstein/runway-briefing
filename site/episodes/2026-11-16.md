Private Equity Grabs easyJet, Wizz Air's Profit Collapses, and Korean Air Inherits a Superjumbo Problem
Episode Summary: Apollo just bought easyJet for £5.7 billion, and we get into what private equity buyouts usually do to airlines. Plus, Wizz Air's profit fell off a cliff while Ryanair barely flinched — same fuel crisis, wildly different outcomes — and Korean Air is about to have more A380s than it probably wants. Also, an update on Friday's ground stop that makes it look even worse, and Boeing has a rocket problem now too.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Monday, November 16th. Let's get into it.

So, easyJet. Shareholders just signed off on Apollo's buyout — 97 percent voted yes, which, when private equity offers you an 81 percent premium on your stock, yeah, you vote yes too.

Seven billion six hundred million dollars, cash, and easyJet goes private after 25 years on the London Stock Exchange. Founder Stelios is actually rolling his stake into the new company instead of cashing out, which tells you he thinks this works.

Here's the thing about private equity buying airlines, though — the track record is mixed, to put it kindly. Apollo says it wants bigger planes, more ancillary fees, and to turn easyJet Holidays into a real profit engine. Translation: more seats per flight, more stuff you pay extra for, and selling you a hotel room too.

That's basically the Ryanair playbook, except Ryanair built it from scratch instead of loading up on buyout debt to get there. The real test is whether Apollo starts squeezing for cash flow to pay down that debt, because that's the move that's broken other PE-owned travel companies before. easyJet's healthy right now — 665 million pounds in profit last year. The question is whether it's still healthy five years from now.

Speaking of fuel and who handled it well — Wizz Air and Ryanair just reported the same six months, same war, same fuel spike. Wizz's profit fell 67 percent. Ryanair's fell 15.

That is not a small gap. Same continent, same customers, same Iran-driven fuel shock. The difference is Ryanair hedged 80 percent of this year's fuel around 67 dollars a barrel. Wizz didn't lock in nearly as much, and it's paying for it now — literally, their fuel cost per seat jumped almost 15 percent.

Ryanair's CEO basically gets to spend this earnings call doing a victory lap and announcing an 800 million euro buyback. Wizz's CEO is stuck explaining why the hedge he bought was both more expensive and arranged too late. In an industry where everybody flies the same A320s to the same beach towns, hedging is apparently the whole game.

Quick update on Friday's ground stop — remember, the one we called only the second since 9/11? Turns out the contractor who botched those router upgrades, L3Harris, was doing the work without any FAA technicians involved at all. The union that represents FAA equipment specialists says this isn't normal — historically, their people do a risk assessment and coordinate with contractors before anything like this happens. Nobody did that Friday. So add that to the pile of "fragile system held together by nobody watching it closely enough."

Over in Asia, Korean Air is about to absorb Asiana's entire fleet, including six Airbus A380s, on top of the five it already flies. That's eleven superjumbos, making it one of the biggest A380 operators left on earth. But get this — one of Asiana's A380s is just sitting parked, and nobody's sure it's coming back. If it doesn't, Korean Air ends up with the exact same operational fleet size as Qantas, which is down to ten. Everybody's quietly shrinking their A380 numbers even while technically growing them. The plane isn't dead, but nobody's ordering more, and that tells you everything.

And last one — Delta's big push for fast satellite WiFi through Amazon's network just hit a snag that has nothing to do with Delta at all. A ULA Vulcan rocket — that's the Boeing-Lockheed joint venture — failed Sunday and took 40 Amazon satellites down with it. Delta doesn't build rockets, obviously, but its WiFi timeline is now tied to a launch program that just lost a chunk of its constellation. When Boeing's name shows up on your supply chain problem these days, you're probably not getting good news fast.
