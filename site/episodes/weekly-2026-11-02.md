Weekly Deep Dive: Is Jet Fuel About to Break the Airline Industry?

Episode Summary: Jet fuel prices are spiking hard enough to trigger layoffs at Alaska and AirBaltic, pay freezes at Ryanair, and route cuts at WestJet and Flydubai — all in the same week. Ryan and Hope dig into whether this is a temporary squeeze or a structural shift that reshapes fleets, bankrupts weaker carriers, and ends the A321XLR era before it even gets started.

RYAN: Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing Weekly. I'm Ryan, and I've got Hope with me.

HOPE: Hey! So this week we're digging into jet fuel, which sounds boring until you realize it just took down fifteen WestJet routes, two hundred Alaska jobs, three hundred AirBaltic jobs, and Ryanair's entire pay structure in like a five day span.

RYAN: It's not boring, it's the scariest line item on every airline's income statement right now. And the thing is, this isn't one company having a bad quarter. This is basically every airline on earth getting hit by the same wave at the same time.

HOPE: Which is kind of the point, right? Like, a normal crisis hits one airline because they made a bad decision. This is hitting Ryanair and WestJet and Frontier and Alaska, companies that have nothing in common business-model-wise.

RYAN: Exactly. Low cost, legacy, ultra low cost, doesn't matter. Fuel is usually twenty to twenty five percent of an airline's operating costs in a normal year. Right now some of these carriers are looking at thirty five, even forty percent depending on their hedging position.

HOPE: Okay wait, explain that jump for people who don't stare at ten-Ks for fun. Why would fuel suddenly be forty percent of costs instead of twenty five?

RYAN: Because jet fuel prices have spiked on the back of the Middle East conflict, and a lot of airlines just weren't hedged for it. Ryanair said this week they took a two billion dollar hit. Two billion. That's not a rounding error, that's their entire profit some years.

HOPE: And that's why they're freezing pay and leaning harder into AI, right? Like that wasn't really about AI being cool, that was about plugging a hole.

RYAN: Right, the AI stuff is cost-cutting theater dressed up as innovation. Michael O'Leary's not suddenly a tech visionary, he's looking for anything that reduces headcount cost because fuel just ate his margin.

HOPE: I mean, that's so Ryanair though. Every single time there's turbulence, financial or literal, their answer is cut something. Pay, staff, now apparently humans entirely.

RYAN: Which, to be fair, is why Ryanair usually survives these cycles better than people expect. They're built for exactly this. The ones I'm actually worried about are carriers that don't have that kind of cost flexibility.

HOPE: Like who?

RYAN: Like Frontier. They posted a hundred and four million dollar loss this quarter, on record revenue. Record revenue and still lost money. That tells you fuel inflation is outrunning their ability to raise fares fast enough to cover it.

HOPE: Wait, say that again, because that's actually the scariest stat of the week to me. Record revenue and still a loss?

RYAN: Yeah. It means ticket prices went up, more people flew, more money came in the door, and it still wasn't enough. That's not a demand problem. That's a cost problem completely overwhelming a revenue win.

HOPE: Okay, so that's not "airlines are struggling to sell seats." That's "even when they sell every seat at a higher price, the fuel bill still wins."

RYAN: Exactly. And JetBlue is telling a slightly different story in the same breath, losses too, but more self-inflicted, more about their own network issues. Frontier's loss is almost purely external. It's fuel.

HOPE: So how does this compare to, like, actual history? Because fuel spikes aren't new. Didn't this basically happen in 2008?

RYAN: Oh, totally, and it's the best comparison we've got. Oil went from around sixty dollars a barrel to a hundred and forty seven in the summer of 2008. Airlines that weren't hedged got destroyed. That's the year we lost Aloha Airlines, ATA, Skybus, a handful of smaller carriers just vanished.

HOPE: And Southwest was the hedging hero story from that era, right? I remember that being like, the one case study every business school used.

RYAN: Southwest was legendary for it. They'd locked in fuel contracts years ahead at like fifty one dollars a barrel while everyone else was paying spot price at a hundred and forty. It basically made them the only profitable major airline in 2008 and 2009.

HOPE: So why doesn't everyone just copy that? Lock in your fuel costs years out and sleep easy?

RYAN: Because hedging is a bet, and it can go really wrong too. If fuel prices drop instead of rise, you're stuck paying above market rate for years, and you look like an idiot compared to competitors who didn't hedge. Delta actually bought an oil refinery in 2012 trying to control this and it mostly didn't work out the way they hoped.

HOPE: Wait, Delta bought an actual refinery?

RYAN: Trainer refinery in Pennsylvania, yeah. The idea was to control their own fuel supply chain instead of hedging on paper. It's had mixed results over the years, it's less of a headline thing now but it's still part of how they manage exposure.

HOPE: Okay so that's wild, but it also tells me there's no clean answer here. Every strategy has a downside.

RYAN: Right, and that's what makes this round scary. A lot of carriers pulled back on aggressive hedging over the last few years because fuel was relatively calm, 2023, 2024, even early 2025 it was manageable. So heading into this spike, a lot of them were caught more exposed than they'd normally want to be.

HOPE: So is this actually as bad as 2008, or are we overreacting because it's a slow week?

RYAN: I think it's structurally different, and this is the key thing. 2008 was a demand shock combined with a fuel shock, right in the middle of the financial crisis, people stopped flying at the exact moment fuel got expensive. Right now demand is actually strong. People are still flying, prices are still high, Frontier's revenue record proves that.

HOPE: So it's not a demand collapse, it's purely a cost spike hitting a healthy revenue environment.

RYAN: Exactly, which in some ways is less scary because the industry isn't dying for lack of customers. But in another way it's scarier because it means even in a good demand environment, airlines still can't cover their costs. That's a much harder problem to fix with a marketing push.

HOPE: Yeah, you can't just run a sale to fix an oil price.

RYAN: Right, and this is where The Air Current's framing this week really lands. They called it aviation being on the verge of a correction, and I think what they mean is less a crash and more a forced reset of who gets to keep doing business the way they've been doing it.

HOPE: Okay, so let's talk about who actually survives this round. Because we've got layoffs everywhere, Alaska cutting two hundred roles, AirBaltic three hundred, WestJet gutting fifteen destinations. Is this the start of real consolidation, like airlines disappearing or getting bought?

RYAN: I think the smaller and mid-size carriers are the ones in real danger. AirBaltic has flirted with financial trouble for years, this just adds pressure on top of pressure. WestJet cutting fifteen international routes isn't a tweak, that's a company retreating to defend its core.

HOPE: And South African Airways is already basically unable to make payroll, even after selling their Heathrow slots, we talked about that a few days ago.

RYAN: Right, that's a carrier that was already terminal, fuel just speeds up the timeline. I think you'll see more of that. Not necessarily big US majors going bankrupt, but regional and national carriers in weaker markets getting squeezed out or absorbed.

HOPE: What about the big US three, Delta, United, American? Are any of them actually at risk here, or is this just a bad quarter for them?

RYAN: I don't think any of them are at existential risk, they've all got way more cash on hand than they did in 2008, and their balance sheets are in much better shape post-pandemic recovery. But United's CCO basically admitted this week they'd rather fly empty seats than lower fares, remember that from Tuesday's show?

HOPE: Yeah, that quote stuck with me.

RYAN: That's a direct read on this fuel environment. They're saying, we would rather protect margin per seat than chase load factor, because the cost per flight just got more expensive and they need every seat to pull its weight financially.

HOPE: So that's basically the big airlines' answer to fuel costs. Fewer discounts, fewer empty-seat giveaways, tighter capacity discipline.

RYAN: Exactly, and that's actually the opposite instinct from what WestJet or Alaska are doing. The majors with deep pockets are managing through pricing power. The smaller or thinner-margin carriers are managing through cutting routes and headcount because they don't have pricing power the same way.

HOPE: Which kind of splits the industry into two groups, doesn't it? Big legacy carriers who can ride this out by being stingier with seats, and everyone else who has to just shrink.

RYAN: Yeah, and that split is exactly how consolidation usually starts. The strong get stronger because they can absorb the shock, and the weak either get bought or they fold. I wouldn't be shocked if we see real M&A conversations heat up over the next year if fuel stays elevated.

HOPE: Who do you think becomes a target?

RYAN: Honestly, something like AirBaltic long-term, or a weaker Gulf-adjacent carrier dealing with the conflict fallout, Flydubai pulling out of twenty-four cities is a pretty loud signal that even well-capitalized Gulf carriers are retreating.

HOPE: Right, and that's not even really about their own fuel costs directly, that's about the conflict itself choking off routes and spiking fuel regionally at the same time.

RYAN: Exactly, it's a double hit for them. Higher input costs and a shrinking map of places they can safely or profitably fly to. That's brutal for a network carrier whose whole model is connecting through a hub.

HOPE: Okay, let's get into the fleet side of this because I think this is actually the most interesting long-term angle. Didn't you tell me a few weeks ago everyone was obsessed with the A321XLR because it's this super efficient narrowbody that can fly long routes without needing a widebody?

RYAN: Right, the whole appeal of the XLR was exactly this kind of moment, lower fuel burn per seat, long range without the cost of operating a widebody. It seemed like the perfect answer to expensive fuel.

HOPE: So shouldn't rising fuel prices make that plane even more popular? But didn't Delta just skip it entirely?

RYAN: They did, and that's actually the twist here. Delta's betting the other direction, they went all in on the A350 and A330neo instead, becoming the only US carrier flying Airbus's newest widebodies. Their logic is that for the routes they care about, the economics of a widebody with more seats actually beats a packed narrowbody flying ultra long haul.

HOPE: Wait, that seems backwards though. Shouldn't a smaller plane always burn less fuel total?

RYAN: Per flight, sure, but fuel efficiency is really about cost per seat per mile, not total burn. A widebody spreads its fuel cost over way more passengers. If you can fill it, the economics can actually be better than a skinny, cramped XLR flying the same route half full.

HOPE: So it's less about the plane being efficient and more about whether you can keep it full.

RYAN: Exactly, and that's the bet that gets riskier when fuel is expensive, because now the penalty for flying a half-empty widebody is way higher than it used to be. Delta's confident they can fill theirs. Other airlines aren't as sure, which is part of why the XLR still has a backlog.

HOPE: Okay but then explain Southwest to me, because this is the part that made zero sense to me this week. Southwest, the airline that has flown nothing but 737s for fifty years, is reportedly considering a widebody purchase. In the middle of a fuel crisis?

RYAN: I know, it sounds insane on the surface, and their CEO is still denying it using basically the exact same playbook he used right before they killed free checked bags, which, if you remember, means the denial is basically a confirmation in disguise.

HOPE: Right, that's the thing that cracked me up, like, we've seen this movie before.

RYAN: Totally. But here's the logic if it's true, Southwest's whole domestic, point-to-point, single-fleet-type model has been incredibly efficient for decades. If fuel stays structurally higher, long-haul widebody routes to places like Hawaii or potentially international leisure markets could actually make more sense economically than cramming more narrowbody frequencies into saturated domestic markets.

HOPE: So it's less "we love widebodies now" and more "the math on domestic-only just got worse, so we're finally looking elsewhere."

RYAN: Exactly, fuel pressure doesn't just cut routes, it also pushes airlines to rethink their entire fleet religion. Southwest guarded the one-fleet-type strategy forever because it kept maintenance and training costs low, but if fuel economics shift enough, that calculus can change too.

HOPE: It's kind of wild that the same crisis is pushing Delta away from the hot new narrowbody and pushing Southwest toward their first ever widebody. Like two completely opposite reactions to the exact same pressure.

RYAN: That's actually the best proof that nobody has a clean answer right now. Every airline is improvising its own version of the same question, how do we protect margin when the single biggest cost on our books just got way less predictable.

HOPE: Let's talk passengers for a second, because I feel like that's the part people actually care about. Is this permanent? Like, are these route cuts coming back when fuel calms down, or are we just losing access to places for good?

RYAN: I think it's a mix, honestly. Some of these cuts are classic seasonal rationalization that would've happened anyway, fuel just gave airlines cover to move faster on routes that were already marginal. WestJet's fifteen destinations, I'd bet half of those were underperforming before this spike even started.

HOPE: So fuel's kind of an excuse as much as a cause in some cases?

RYAN: A little bit, yeah. Airlines love having an external villain to point to when they cut something that wasn't working anyway. Nobody wants to say, this route never made money, it's easier to say, blame the Middle East conflict.

HOPE: That's a little cynical but I believe it.

RYAN: But some of it is real and structural. Flydubai pulling twenty-four cities, that's not just trimming fat, that's a genuine retreat from markets they can't profitably or safely serve right now. Those routes might come back in a year, they might not come back at all if the region stays unstable.

HOPE: So for a regular traveler, what should they actually expect? Fewer options on thinner routes, higher prices everywhere?

RYAN: Higher prices for sure, and probably fewer frequencies on routes that aren't dense enough to protect. The era of airlines throwing extra flights at marginal markets just to grow market share, that's over for now. We're back to flying only what pays.

HOPE: Which honestly sounds like it's good for airline balance sheets and bad for anyone who lives somewhere that's not a major hub.

RYAN: Pretty much. If you're flying JFK to LAX, you're fine, there's too much demand for airlines to cut that corridor. If you're flying out of a smaller regional market that was only getting served because fuel was cheap enough to make it marginally profitable, you're the one who loses first.

HOPE: Okay, last thing, I want your actual prediction. If you had to bet, does fuel stay this high through next year, or does this settle down?

RYAN: I think it stays elevated through at least the first half of next year, tied to how the Middle East conflict resolves or doesn't. I don't think we snap back to pre-spike prices quickly even if there's a ceasefire, because a lot of the supply chain disruption doesn't unwind overnight.

HOPE: So more layoffs, more route cuts coming?

RYAN: I'd bet on at least two or three more carriers announcing layoffs or route cuts before the end of the year. And I think you'll see more fleet strategy surprises like the Southwest widebody rumor, because airlines are genuinely rethinking assumptions they've held for decades.

HOPE: And consolidation?

RYAN: I think we see at least one meaningful acquisition or merger conversation go public within the next twelve months, probably involving a smaller international carrier rather than a US major. The big three are protected by scale, everyone below them is exposed.

HOPE: Yeah, this one actually felt different from a normal "oh no, fuel went up" news cycle. It's touching literally every part of the business at once, fleets, labor, routes, pricing.

RYAN: That's exactly why it's the story of the week instead of just a footnote in five different headlines. Fuel doesn't care about your business model, and right now it's the one variable nobody in this industry can fully control.

HOPE: Well, on that slightly ominous note.
