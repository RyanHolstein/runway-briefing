Episode Title: American's Big Shakeup, Lufthansa's Brutal Week, and a Flight to Buenos Aires That Barely Left the Ground
Episode Summary: American Airlines cleans house — a longtime COO exits with a severance that looks a lot like a firing, and the airline finally kills off its last true first-class cabin. Lufthansa gets hit from three directions at once: job cuts, a canceled city incentive, and a Boeing delay so bad the plane it ordered a decade later beat it to the gate. Plus, a JSX growth play, a lavatory that ruined a 10-hour flight, and Trump's air traffic control claims get fact-checked by his own pilot's competition.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Saturday, November 21st. Let's get into it.

So, American Airlines had itself a week. Their longtime operations chief, David Seymour, is "retiring" after 25-plus years at the company. Except the severance — two point seven five million dollars — is structured like a dismissal package, not a gold watch. JC Gulbranson already quietly took over day-to-day ops back in August, so this is basically just making it official.

And that's not even the biggest American story. This week they finally retired Flagship First — their last real international first-class cabin, and honestly the last one left in the entire US industry. Delta ditched standalone first class years ago. United killed it back in 2018. American was the holdout, and now they're not. It's getting replaced by a bigger, nicer business class with sliding doors, which, financially, just makes more sense — fewer seats, less revenue per square foot, versus business class that's almost as nice and sells way more of it.

Oh, and American also announced it's flying to Israel again for the first time since October 7th, 2023 — partnering with Israir, which, fun detail, is flying the route using American's own retired A330s. So American's old planes are now connecting its own future route. Weird little circle there.

Speaking of airlines having a day — Lufthansa. A leaked internal memo says they're cutting a thousand jobs at Frankfurt and Munich. The pilots' union called it a "grand spectacle of threats," which, respect for the phrasing. The core Lufthansa brand made a profit margin of just 0.9% last year. For comparison, British Airways did 15%, Ryanair did 11%, even their own sister airline SWISS did over 9%. The mothership is dragging down the whole group.

Then Columbus, Ohio pulled the plug on a 300-thousand-dollar incentive package for Lufthansa's new Frankfurt route before it even launches. And — this is my favorite one — Lufthansa ordered the Boeing 777-9 back in 2013. It was supposed to arrive in 2020. It's now 2026, the plane's still not here, new target is 2027, and in the meantime, Lufthansa ordered the Airbus A350-1000 in 2023 and already has it flying. They waited longer for the plane they ordered a decade earlier. That's Boeing's most delayed program basically making the Wright Brothers look rushed.

Also yesterday — American had a 777 headed to Buenos Aires, ten and a half hours in the air, ready to go. A lavatory overflowed over the Gulf of Mexico. They turned the whole plane around after just two hours and landed back at DFW. The jet is 26 years old, so, you know, maybe it's earned the right to be a little dramatic.

Quick hit on something more interesting — JSX just bought New Pacific Airlines, a carrier that already failed once, specifically to bring back 42-seat jets. That's 12 more seats than JSX normally flies. JSX operates under this public-charter rule that lets passengers skip TSA lines and treats the whole thing like semi-private flying. Bigger planes means they can finally scale that model past its current ceiling without changing the playbook. Smart move, and one to watch.

Last thing — Trump told reporters his private pilot used to rely on another country's air traffic control because the American system is, quote, so bad. Wouldn't say which country. A private pilot pretty quickly went public to dispute the claim. No further comment from Trump's pilot, who I assume is staying very quiet right now.
