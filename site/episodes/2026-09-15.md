Episode Title: Chicago's Airline Turf War Heats Up, JFK's Terminal One Slips Again, and Congress Wants to Gut National Airport
Episode Summary: American is literally handing out free elite status on planes to keep Chicago flyers loyal, while United counters with a Tokyo route relaunch — welcome to the hottest hub fight in the country. Plus, JFK's shiny new Terminal One pushes to 2027 (even as Air France unveils a massive new lounge for it), a Senate bill could gut half of Reagan National's long-haul flights, and new NTSB data reveals exactly what went wrong on that Amazon cargo jet in Miami.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Tuesday, September 15th. Let's get into it.

So, Chicago. American and United are basically in a bar fight over who gets to be the hometown airline, and today it got a little ridiculous.

Zach Griff over at View from the Wing reported that American had a manager board a flight out of O'Hare and just... hand out free Gold status. Personalized cards. About 25 of them that day, flight attendants trained on how to deliver them like it's a game show. That's not normal elite-status behavior, that's a retention panic.

Why? Because United is O'Hare's hub, and American's been trying to poach loyalty for years without much luck. And today United fired back with actual substance — they're relaunching nonstop O'Hare to Tokyo Narita starting October 24th, a route they haven't flown since March 2020. Fourteen hours on a 787-8, which, respect to whoever's in the window seat for that one.

Here's the thing though — giving away status cards on a plane is a Band-Aid. Bringing back a flagship long-haul route is a statement. United's telling Chicago flyers "we're building the network," American's telling them "please don't leave, here's a card." I know which one wins that argument long-term.

Next up, JFK. The Points Guy confirmed Terminal One, the big multi-billion dollar rebuild, is now pushed to early 2027. It was supposed to open this year. Then last year they said summer 2026. Now it's "early 2027," which in airport-construction-speak usually means "later 2027."

But here's the fun part — Air France announced their new lounge for that terminal today, and it's massive. Twenty-nine thousand square feet, two levels, seats over 400 people, there's apparently a dedicated space for beauty treatments and a lounge chef flying in from France, presumably to judge your economy seatmates. It'll be Air France's biggest lounge outside Paris. So the terminal that isn't open yet already has bragging rights for the best room inside it. Classic airport development — the amenities always outpace the concrete.

Now, less fun. There's a new Senate bill that would strip more than half of Reagan National's long-distance flight slots — the ones that go beyond the normal 1,250-mile perimeter rule. View from the Wing flagged this, and it's aimed squarely at Alaska and Frontier, the two carriers actually using those beyond-perimeter slots at DCA.

Here's what bugs me about this one — the bill lets the FAA study whether a specific cut actually improves safety, and even if the FAA says "nope, doesn't help," that finding can only delay the cut, not stop it. That's not a safety bill, that's a market-share bill wearing a safety costume. And who's pushed for exactly this kind of DCA restriction for years? United, protecting Dulles. Funny how that works out.

Quick update on something we covered yesterday — the Amazon Prime Air 767 that overran the runway in Miami. The NTSB released the final 30 seconds of flight data, and it's rough. The pilots added thrust, started a go-around, then aborted it, and never deployed reversers or speed brakes. That's the kind of detail that turns "runway excursion" into "we need to talk about what happened in that cockpit." Amazon already dropped the operator this week — this data isn't going to help that airline's case.

Last one, and it's a good one. Korean Air just flipped on free Starlink Wi-Fi across four planes today — three A350s and a 777. Fast enough to actually stream video, no SkyPass account needed, totally free. Refreshing, honestly, compared to the usual pay-per-flight nonsense.

But they smartly blocked voice calls and livestreaming. Which, as someone who's sat next to a guy FaceTiming his contractor for two hours at 35,000 feet, I fully support. Korean Air says it's about cabin comfort and security. I say it's about not wanting a mutiny at seat 34C.
