So, Delta. Thanksgiving week, the busiest travel period of the year, and Sunday morning their load control system — the thing that calculates weight and balance for every single flight — just stopped working.

It's called ALIS, Automated Load Instruction System, and when it goes down, ramp crews are literally counting bags by hand. At Atlanta, the world's busiest airport, that backs up everything. We're talking over 2,000 delayed mainline flights, more than 200 cancellations, and the FAA had to step in with a ground stop.

Here's why this one stings more than usual. Remember two episodes ago we talked about the FAA's nationwide ground stop on November 13th? This is the second major tech failure to snarl air travel in under two weeks, and it's happening right as Thanksgiving traffic ramps up. Delta says automation's restored, but they already preemptively canceled dozens of Monday flights. Honestly, if I'm flying through Atlanta this week, I'm padding my connections by a lot.

Speaking of travel chaos — Condor. Two A330s flying charter routes from the Dominican Republic to Frankfurt, packed with cruise passengers who'd just finished a 17-day trip on the AIDAbella. Somewhere over the Atlantic, people started getting sick. Like, dozens of people. Nausea, vomiting, the whole norovirus special.

Suspected cause is norovirus, which, if you've ever been on a cruise ship, you know is basically the final boss of ship life. The irony here is brutal — they survived 17 days at sea only to get hit with it on the flight home.

Over in Madrid, Barajas Airport had its own disaster. The automated train that connects the main terminal to the satellite terminal — where all the international flights outside Europe's Schengen zone land — broke down Sunday. And here's the kicker: there's no walkway as a backup. None.

So you've got thousands of passengers stuck in a building 2.7 kilometers away with no baggage claim, no ground transport, basically trapped until someone fixes a train. People were posting videos calling it a disgrace, and honestly, for one of the biggest airports in the world? Fair.

And if you're flying through SFO for Thanksgiving, here's a new problem nobody saw coming: the airfield might flood. The National Weather Service is predicting tides near ten feet between Tuesday and Thursday — peak Thanksgiving travel — and officials say that could overwhelm parts of the runway infrastructure. SFO sits right on the Bay, and this isn't the first time king tides have threatened it, but the timing here, right during the busiest week of the year, is just rough luck piled on top of everything else going on this week.

Alright, something more interesting for a change. Korean Air's marketing chief told Simple Flying that their booking pattern has flipped. Premium cabins are now selling out before economy does. That used to never happen — economy always filled first because, well, there's way more seats back there and people book early for the cheap fares.

This actually tells you a lot about where the industry's heading. Post-pandemic, people with money are prioritizing comfort over saving a few hundred bucks, and airlines have noticed — it's part of why you're seeing carriers like Qantas rework their whole fleet strategy around longer-range narrowbodies with better premium cabins instead of just cramming more economy seats in. If premium is selling out first, that's where the profit margin's moving, and airlines are going to keep shrinking economy and growing business class to chase it.
