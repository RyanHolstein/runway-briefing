Episode Summary: United has hundreds of business class seats sitting in storage for a plane it still can't fly, and the story is somehow both hilarious and expensive. Plus, a Ryanair 737 MAX scrapes a wingtip aborting a landing in Dublin crosswinds, the FAA quietly moves to end the 53-year ban on supersonic flight over the US, and Nashville's airport might soon be named after Dolly Parton.

So, United. They've got a problem, and it's a weirdly specific one. They ordered a bunch of Boeing 737 MAX 10s years ago, and as part of that order, they also bought hundreds of custom lie-flat business class seats designed specifically to fit that plane.

Here's the thing. The MAX 10 still isn't certified. Boeing's been promising it for years, and it's still stuck in FAA limbo. So United is sitting on a warehouse full of premium seats for an airplane that, legally, does not exist yet.

And get this — these seats can't just get bolted into a different plane. They're custom-engineered for the MAX 10's specific cabin dimensions. United has actually admitted, on the record, that they don't know what to do with them. That's not a rounding error, that's real money just parked in a hangar somewhere depreciating.

This is the same story we've been tracking with the MAX certification saga generally — Boeing keeps missing dates, and it's not just United's growth plans getting stalled, it's actual hardware sitting useless. Somebody in procurement is having a rough week.

Alright, Ryanair. There's video going around of a 737 MAX coming into Dublin, getting hit by a serious crosswind, rolling hard to the right right at the last second, and clipping a wingtip on the runway before going around. Passengers filmed the whole thing.

Nobody was hurt, the plane landed safely on the second attempt, but that's a genuinely scary few seconds if you're in seat 14A watching the wing dip toward the tarmac. Crosswind landings are routine for pilots, they train for exactly this, but Dublin's had brutal wind lately and this is a good reminder that "routine" doesn't mean "risk-free." Ryanair hasn't said much yet, but that plane's getting inspected before it flies again, that's for sure.

Now here's one that actually made me sit up. The FAA just proposed replacing the 1973 ban on supersonic flight over the US. For over 50 years, this has been the reason Concorde could only go supersonic over water on US routes.

The old rule just banned breaking the sound barrier, period, anywhere over land. The new proposal is different — it's based on how loud the sonic boom actually is on the ground. If a plane's boom stays under a certain noise threshold, it could legally fly supersonic over the continental US.

This matters because companies like Boom Supersonic have been building business models around exactly this kind of rule change. If this actually goes through, we could see the first legal supersonic flights over US soil since, well, ever, commercially speaking. Funny timing too, with everyone reflecting on Concorde's anniversary this week. The technology's been ready for years — it was the law that wasn't.
