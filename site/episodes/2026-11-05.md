Episode title: Qantas Clears Out the Fleet, KLM's Billion-Euro Pain, and Delta's Windowless Window Seats
Episode Summary: Qantas is saying goodbye to the A380 early and offloading 44 Boeing 737s as it bets big on new jets — we break down what that means. Plus, KLM announces a painful billion-euro overhaul, Delta has to face a lawsuit over "window seats" with no actual window, Priceline's hotel partner gets hit with a billion-dollar fine, and we find out which United economy seats you'll probably never get to sit in.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Thursday, November 5th. Let's get into it.

So, Qantas. Big fleet news out of Australia today, and it's actually two stories that are really one story.

First, they're retiring their Airbus A380s four years early. Qantas was supposed to fly these things into the 2030s, like Lufthansa and Korean Air are doing. Instead, they're pulling the plug early because they trust their incoming A350-1000s and 787 deliveries enough to not need the backup plan anymore.

And look, that trust is a big deal, because Boeing and Airbus have both had rough delivery records the last few years. Qantas betting its long-haul future on those jets actually showing up on time is either confidence or wishful thinking, we'll find out.

Second part of the story: Qantas is also selling off 44 Boeing 737s, handing them over gradually over the next seven years as Airbus A321XLRs take their place domestically. So basically, Qantas is doing a full teardown and rebuild of its fleet, widebody and narrowbody, all at once. That is not cheap, and it's not something airlines do unless they're all in on a twin-engine future. The four-engine A380 era is basically over, it just took longer to die than anyone expected.

Now, KLM. Not a fun day in Amsterdam. The airline announced what it's calling a "painful" billion-euro restructuring, trimming its workforce to shore up the hub.

This is the same story we keep hearing in 2026, honestly. Middle East airspace closures, fuel prices up, and airlines everywhere eating the cost. KLM's parent company, Air France-KLM, has been under pressure for a while, and this is them finally admitting the Amsterdam hub needs surgery, not a band-aid. When a legacy European carrier starts using the word "painful" in a press release, that usually means jobs, and it usually means more than they're letting on today.

Okay, Delta. This one's almost funny if it weren't costing people money. A federal judge says Delta has to face a class action lawsuit claiming over a million passengers paid extra for window seats that don't actually have windows. Apparently on some of their 737s, 757s, and A321s, there are seats marked as window seats that are just... next to a blank wall. You paid extra for a view of insulation.

I don't know how you accidentally charge a million people for a window that isn't there, but here we are. This is the kind of lawsuit that sounds small until you multiply it by a million tickets, and suddenly Delta's lawyers are having a very long year.

Speaking of getting charged for things that don't deliver what they promise — Priceline's in trouble too. Their booking partner, Guest Reservations, is facing a potential billion-dollar fine for allegedly tricking people into paying up to 85% more for hotel rooms by disguising itself as the hotel's actual booking page.

So you think you're booking direct, you're actually going through a middleman charging way more, and pocketing the difference. This has been an open secret in the industry for years — these shady lookalike booking sites — but a billion-dollar fine is the first time regulators have really gone after one with teeth. If this sticks, it could be the moment third-party booking scams actually become a legal liability instead of just a Reddit complaint.

Last one, and it's a fun little mystery. Simple Flying found that United has four economy seats on its Boeing 767-400ERs with 43 inches of pitch. That's basically premium economy space, hidden in the back of a regular economy cabin.

Here's the twist — they're not for you. They're crew rest seats, not passenger seats, which is honestly a little cruel information to have. Somewhere on a 767 right now, a flight attendant is stretching out in more legroom than you'll ever get, and you will never know which row it is.
