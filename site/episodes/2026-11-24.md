So, Plus Ultra. If you've never heard of them, that's kind of the point, and also kind of the problem. Spanish long-haul carrier, flew A330s between Madrid and Latin America — Lima, Buenos Aires, Bogota, that kind of thing. As of yesterday, they've suspended all operations.

They'd been in court-supervised talks with creditors for about a week, trying to find, quote, a "solid foundation" for the long term. Translation: they were out of cash and nobody wanted to write another check. They're blaming fuel costs, and sure, fuel's brutal right now, but let's be real — a seven-plane widebody operator competing against Iberia and the big Gulf carriers on the same routes was always running on thin margins.

Here's why this matters beyond just one small airline. This is now the second long-haul operator to go under this year citing the exact same fuel pressure, and it's not getting better anytime soon. If you're a niche widebody carrier without a mega-alliance backing you up, 2026 has not been kind. Employees, by the way, still don't know what happens next — the company says it's "evaluating alternatives," which is corporate-speak for nobody has a plan yet.

Okay, Delta. They're throwing an actual Asian night market in Seattle on December 2nd to announce something they could've just put in a press release — nonstop service to Singapore.

This is Delta picking a direct fight with Singapore Airlines, who's owned that route for basically ever. We're talking seventeen hours nonstop, no stopping in Seoul, no stopping anywhere. Singapore is one of the most important business hubs in Asia, and right now if you're flying there from the US you're probably connecting through the Gulf or through Incheon.

Why's Delta doing this now? Seattle's their quietly-growing Pacific gateway, and ultra-long-haul routes like this are basically a flex — it says "we can do what only the best airlines in the world can do." The A350 and 787 made this kind of flight possible without murdering fuel economy, and every airline with deep pockets wants one marquee route like this. Singapore Airlines is not going to just hand over market share though, so buckle up.

Speaking of planes doing less flying than the airlines want — Europe's quietly swapping domestic flights for trains, and it's actually working. Austrian Airlines just flew its last-ever Graz to Vienna flight. They already killed their Linz and Salzburg connections. They didn't stop serving those cities — they just code-share the train instead.

This is the Lufthansa Group's broader strategy, and honestly, it makes sense. Short domestic hops burn fuel, annoy regulators, and make almost no money once you account for airport fees. If a train gets someone to the hub in under three hours, why burn jet fuel for it? This is where European aviation's heading — short-haul flying is slowly becoming a connecting-flight problem, not a flying problem, and I think we'll see more carriers follow Lufthansa's lead here.

Quick hits on two planes that had rough nights. A JetBlue A321neo struck its tail during a go-around at LAX — sparks flying, caught on camera, aircraft still grounded. And an Alaska 737 at JFK literally collided with a catering truck before a flight to San Francisco, delaying it into Tuesday morning. Neither one is catastrophic, but two separate ground and approach mishaps in one day is a reminder that the unglamorous stuff — ramp operations, go-around procedures — is where most real risk actually lives.

Last thing — remember the O'Hare gate fight we talked about last week? American just dropped its lawsuit against the city after losing its injunction appeal. But here's the twist — American's actually in a stronger position than when it sued. They bought two gates off Spirit's corpse and grew their way into more space anyway. United wanted American gone from Chicago. That plan is not working.
