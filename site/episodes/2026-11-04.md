Episode title: Emirates vs. Italy, Lufthansa's Airline Collection Grows, and JetBlue's Bag Fees Just Won't Quit
Episode Summary: ITA Airways is racing Emirates to launch a route neither of them really needs to fly yet, Lufthansa makes it official on a billion-dollar stake in TAP Air Portugal, and six airlines just found out they've been sitting in a data breach for three years. Plus, Boeing's 787 gets a lightning-strike warning, and JetBlue hikes bag fees for the second time this year.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Wednesday, November 4th. Let's get into it.

So, ITA Airways. Weird little story, but stick with me. Back in August, Emirates applied to fly Milan to Lima, Peru — using something called fifth-freedom rights, which basically means a foreign airline can scoop up passengers on a route that isn't even connected to its home country.

Emirates does this a lot. Dubai to wherever, then onward to somewhere else, selling tickets for just the second leg like they're a local carrier.

Well, ITA caught wind of it and is now scrambling to launch its own Milan-Lima flights first. Which is funny, because ITA has never flown long-haul out of Milan. Ever. They deliberately centered all of that in Rome back when the airline relaunched in 2021, because Alitalia lost a fortune trying to do long-haul from Malpensa.

So now they're reportedly bringing back a route structure they abandoned for financial reasons, purely to keep Emirates out. That's not a business plan, that's a blocking maneuver. Sometimes the fastest way to grow a route network is watching a competitor almost do it first.

Bigger one. Lufthansa just signed the actual paperwork — one point two billion dollars for forty-four point nine percent of TAP Air Portugal. This closes out a few weeks of will-they-won't-they after Portugal picked Lufthansa over Air France-KLM.

Here's why this matters beyond the number. Lufthansa already has stakes in Austrian, Swiss, and Brussels Airlines. They're basically building a collection of former European flag carriers, one at a time, like some kind of airline Voltron.

And TAP is the prize piece here because of Lisbon. It's become a huge gateway between Europe and Brazil, way more efficient than routing through Madrid or Paris. Air France-KLM really wanted this. They didn't get it. That's a real loss for them in the battle over transatlantic and Latin American connections.

Okay, this next one's not funny, it's just bad. Six major airlines have now confirmed they got caught up in a data breach at Amadeus, the company that quietly runs the booking systems behind something like a hundred and thirty airlines worldwide.

Singapore Airlines, forty-six thousand bookings exposed. Cathay Pacific, fifty-three thousand. Japan Airlines, ninety-two thousand. ANA, Korean Air, and Condor are all on the list too. Names, booking codes, full itineraries — no credit cards, thankfully, but still.

And here's the part that bugs me. This breach happened over three years, starting back in 2023. Amadeus didn't even notice until September of this year. Three years. These social engineering attacks, tricking IT help desks into handing over access, have been hitting airlines for a while now. This is the biggest one yet, and honestly, probably not the last.

Boeing's got a safety story too. The FAA just ordered inspections on a bunch of 787 Dreamliners because of a sealant defect that could let a lightning strike spark inside the fuel tank. That's the same basic nightmare scenario behind TWA 800 back in the nineties — a fuel tank explosion from an ignition source nobody planned for.

To be clear, this is a precaution, not an active failure. The directive kicks in next month and only covers specific 787-8s, 9s, and 10s that Boeing identified. But it's one more item on Boeing's never-ending list of "we'll fix it, just give us some time."

And finally, JetBlue. They hiked checked bag fees again. Second time this year. Fifty bucks minimum for your first bag, sixty if you don't prepay, and up to ninety if you're flying transatlantic during peak season.

Two and a half years ago that minimum was thirty-five dollars. That's a forty-three percent jump. And JetBlue loves doing this — they raised fees first back in the spring, and United, Delta, American, and Southwest all followed within weeks. So if you're flying anyone this holiday season, I'd bet on a bag fee hike at your airline too, probably by Thanksgiving.
