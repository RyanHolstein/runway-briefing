Episode title: Mayday Over SFO, Kirby Airs All His Grievances, and Delta Quietly Wins the Widebody War
Episode Summary: A United 757 declares an emergency over San Francisco when a go-around burns through its fuel reserves. Then, United CEO Scott Kirby has a lot of feelings about American Airlines and a theory about Boeing and Airbus you have to hear. Plus, Delta quietly becomes the only U.S. airline flying Airbus's newest widebodies, and Alaska Airlines cuts 200 jobs as fuel costs keep biting.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Sunday, November 1st. Let's get into it.

So first up, a scary one. United flight 1023, Boston to San Francisco, Boeing 757, declares a Mayday on approach Friday morning. Here's what happened — the plane was on final to runway 28R, something about the approach wasn't stable, so the crew does the right thing and goes around.

Problem is, by the time they circled back, they were low enough on fuel that they had to declare an emergency to get priority back to the airport. And look, a go-around itself is routine, pilots train for that constantly. It's the fuel math afterward that got tight. Mayday declarations for fuel are rare specifically because crews are trained to divert way before it gets to that point — so this is one the NTSB and United are both going to want a very clear explanation for. Good news, the plane landed safely. But "safely" and "with margin to spare" are two different things, and this is the kind of incident that quietly gets a lot more scrutiny than the headline suggests.

Okay, now for some drama. Scott Kirby, United's CEO, went on a podcast this week and just... let it all out about American Airlines. He says Doug Parker, American's old CEO, took him to dinner years ago, gave him a retirement date, and promised him the top job. Then three months later, fired him instead. And more recently, Kirby says American was actually considering a merger pitch from him — and he found out they rejected it from a press release. American's version of events is, predictably, different.

But here's the part I actually think matters — Kirby also accused Boeing and Airbus of quietly agreeing not to build new planes, because the duopoly means neither one has to. Which, I get the frustration — he's been asking for a 757 replacement for twenty years. But accusing your two suppliers of a secret non-aggression pact, with zero evidence, while also pushing for more airline mergers that would reduce competition on his side of the business? That's a little rich.

Now, a quieter story that's actually a big deal if you care about fleets — Delta has become the only U.S. airline flying both the Airbus A350 and the A330neo. American and United both went other directions — more Boeing, more Dreamliners. Delta basically made a long-term bet on Airbus for its entire long-haul widebody strategy, and with more A350s on order, that bet is only getting bigger.

Why does this matter? Because fleet commonality saves airlines real money — same training, same parts, same maintenance pipeline. Delta's betting that simplicity around one manufacturer beats flexibility across two. If fuel prices stay high and that bet pays off, don't be surprised if American and United quietly start asking themselves if they picked the wrong side of this duopoly Kirby's so mad at.

Speaking of fuel prices — Alaska Airlines is cutting around 200 jobs by the end of the year, mostly management and non-contract roles. The airline's blaming fuel costs and a tough operating environment, which, fair, fuel really has been brutal this year. But it's also the second airline in two weeks we've talked about trimming headcount, and that's worth watching. When the pain starts hitting corporate offices instead of frontline staff, that usually means leadership thinks this isn't a short dip — it's a stretch.

And one fun fact to send you off — GE's GE9X engine, the one that'll power the Boeing 777X, set the world record for jet engine thrust back in 2017 at over 134,000 pounds of force. But get this — when it actually goes into service, GE's capping it below that, even below the engine it's replacing. Turns out building the most powerful engine ever and actually using all that power are two very different business decisions.
