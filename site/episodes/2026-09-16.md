Episode title: American's XLR Seats Keep Breaking, Delta Quietly Cuts 21 Cities, and Qatar Backs Off Its Avios Crackdown
Episode Summary: American's shiny new A321XLRs have a business class problem — the seats keep getting stuck. Plus, Delta's been trimming long-haul cities you probably didn't notice, Breeze pilots call in a federal mediator, and Qatar Airways quietly walks back one of the more annoying loyalty changes we've covered this year.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Wednesday, September 16th. Let's get into it.

So American Airlines has a new toy — the Airbus A321XLR, this narrowbody that can fly long, skinny international routes without needing a widebody. Cool plane. Except the fancy lie-flat business seats on it keep breaking.

We're talking seat controls just... giving up. Stuck upright, stuck reclined. According to aviation insider JonNYC, some of these jets have been flying with two broken business suites at once, just blocked off, empty, while people who paid business class money sit in coach.

American's only got eight of these things so far, with dozens more coming over the next year, headed to routes like Vienna and Nice. Here's why this actually matters — this plane is central to American's whole long-haul strategy. It's cheaper to fly than a widebody, which is exactly why Delta and United are chasing similar narrowbody long-haul plays too. But if the premium seats people are paying a premium for don't work, that math falls apart fast. Growing pains are normal with new aircraft, sure, but "the seat won't lie flat" is not a great look for a cabin you're charging four figures for.

Speaking of Delta — Simple Flying dug up something interesting. Delta's quietly dropped 21 long-haul destinations over the years. Singapore, Beijing, Dubai, plus European stops like Düsseldorf and Manchester, just gone from the route map.

And get this — Delta's overall network has actually grown 16% over the same period. So this isn't decline, this is a filter. Delta's not trying to fly everywhere anymore, they're trying to fly the routes that make money, and dropping the ones that don't, even big-name cities. That's the most profitable US airline talking with its route map instead of its mouth.

Over at Breeze, the pilots are done waiting. They've asked for federal mediation after bargaining that's dragged on since 2023. That's three years of talks going nowhere, which tells you where the relationship's at right now.

Mediation is basically the federal government stepping in and saying "okay, you two need a referee." It doesn't mean a strike is coming — under the Railway Labor Act, that's a long road with a lot of steps before anyone walks out. But it's a signal. Breeze has been trying to sell itself as this scrappy, different kind of airline. Pilots asking Washington to get involved after three years isn't the vibe they're going for.

airBaltic's Chapter 11 filing is still playing out too — we covered this yesterday — but worth noting: they're Europe's biggest A320... sorry, A220 operator, and they're doing this through a US bankruptcy court in New York, not back home in Latvia. That's a deliberate move, US Chapter 11 lets you keep flying while you restructure, which European insolvency law doesn't always allow as cleanly. Smart legal shopping, basically.

Okay, quick one — Qatar Airways actually walked something back, which almost never happens. Remember their "My List" feature, where you needed to fly Qatar or use their credit card before you could even redeem Avios for a friend? Total headache, and honestly it always smelled less like fraud prevention and more like "please engage with our loyalty program or else."

Well, they just quietly dropped that requirement. Now you just need an account that's 30 days old. The restrictions on how many people you can add and how long they stay on your list are still there, but the most annoying part — proving you're a loyal customer before you're allowed to be generous to your own family — that's gone. Small win for consumers, and a rare moment of an airline admitting a policy was dumb without actually saying it out loud.

Last thing — Etihad's rolling out a new lounge design concept, starting at Heathrow, all part of this ultra-premium repositioning under CEO Antonoaldo Neves. Worth watching, because a few years ago Etihad nearly went the way of Gulf Air — shrinking into irrelevance. Betting big on premium cabins and lounges instead of just cutting costs is a real gamble, but it's the same playbook that's working for Qatar and Emirates, so it's not a crazy one.
