Episode Title: Masked Agents at the Gate, O'Hare's Turf War Turns Legal, and the Jet That Started It All
Episode Summary: A jet bridge in Seattle turns into a flashpoint when ICE agents storm an Alaska Airlines boarding gate, leaving the crew too rattled to fly. Meanwhile, United and American take their O'Hare rivalry to court, Boeing can't get MAX production out of first gear, and we look back at the jetliner that started this whole industry — and crashed spectacularly trying.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Sunday, November 22nd. Let's get into it.

So this is a wild one. Saturday morning, Alaska Airlines flight 1306, Seattle to LA. Passengers are boarding, crowded onto the jet bridge, and masked ICE agents show up and arrest a father and his daughter right there at the aircraft door.

Not at security. Not in the terminal. At the door of the plane.

And here's the part that really gets me — the crew was so shaken by it that the flight attendants asked to be swapped out, and reportedly the pilot just walked away. Alaska ended up deplaning everybody and starting over with a whole new crew. The Port of Seattle put out a statement basically saying, hey, we didn't know this was happening, we have protocols for immigration enforcement at this airport, and we're trying to figure out if those got broken.

That's the real story here. It's not just that an arrest happened — it's that airports have worked out agreements for how this stuff is supposed to go, specifically so it doesn't turn into chaos on a jet bridge full of boarding passengers. When flight attendants are too traumatized to work a three-hour flight, that tells you procedure went out the window. Alaska says it's "concerned." I'd love to know what "concerned" actually translates into going forward, because this isn't the kind of thing you want becoming routine at an airport gate.

Okay, O'Hare. United and American have basically been fighting over Chicago since gate assignments were handed out in the Reagan administration, but this year it's gotten ugly. United grabbed a bunch of gates in late 2025, American's been clawing to get them back, and now it's not just lobbying — it's lawyers. Courtroom stuff.

On top of that, both airlines have been cramming more flights into O'Hare while the airport's mid-construction, which is a great way to create gridlock, so the feds actually had to step in and cap schedules to keep the airfield from seizing up. Why does this matter? Because O'Hare is the one hub where neither airline has ever fully won. Whoever controls more gates there controls connections across basically the whole middle of the country. This is a decades-old fight and it's not getting resolved anytime soon.

Boeing. Still can't get 737 MAX production out of second gear. They're stuck at 42 jets a month, trying to get to 47, and that climb keeps sliding — now they're saying early 2027, which is already past the summer target they missed. And when a union asked if more layoffs are coming after this month's cuts, Boeing basically said... maybe, we'll see.

Here's why that matters beyond Boeing's org chart — every airline waiting on MAX deliveries, and that's a lot of them, is planning fleet growth around a production number Boeing keeps not hitting. Combine that with the 777-9 running seven years late with its biggest customer already rejecting early jets, and you've got a manufacturer that's basically two airplanes behind on two different programs at once.

Last one, and it's a good palate cleanser. Simple Flying had a nice piece this week on the de Havilland Comet — the first commercial jetliner ever, back in 1949. Gorgeous plane, engines buried right into the wings, way ahead of its time.

And then it started literally breaking apart in the sky. Turned out the square windows were creating stress points that led to metal fatigue, and a few Comets came apart mid-flight. By the time de Havilland figured it out and redesigned the thing, Boeing had the 707 ready to go, and the Comet never recovered. Kind of feels relevant this week, honestly — rushing a new jet into service without fully understanding it has been a risk since day one of the jet age. Boeing and Airbus are still managing that same tension today, just with better testing and a lot more lawyers.
