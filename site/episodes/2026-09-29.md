At least 25 of their incoming MAX 10s are getting 12 "Aurora Suites" up front, direct aisle access, privacy stuff, the whole deal, starting around 2028. Basically Alaska looked at United's Polaris and Delta's flat beds on transcon routes and said, okay, we need to actually compete here instead of just showing up with a nice meal and hoping nobody notices we don't have a bed.

That last part matters because when Alaska bought Hawaiian, the fear was Hawaiian would just get starved of investment. Instead they're putting real money into it. Okay, Boeing. Update on something we've been tracking — the 737 MAX 10 just got delayed again, and this one's a little embarrassing. There's a software glitch that can leave pilots without automated flight guidance after an aborted landing. Not nothing.

Here's the part that stings: Boeing apparently knew about this for almost two years before telling anyone. And now United and Southwest have both told Boeing, flat out, they don't want new jets with that software installed. Southwest's actually asking for an older software version instead, like returning a phone update because it broke your camera.

The MAX 10 still isn't certified, still doesn't have a new date, and this is exactly the kind of thing that keeps eroding trust with regulators and airlines alike. Boeing just landed that huge order we talked about a couple days ago. Doesn't mean much if they can't get planes out the door clean.

Alright, weird one. There's a tiny airport out on Midway Atoll, way out in the Pacific, that has zero scheduled flights. Like, nobody actually flies there. But airlines are begging the FAA to save it anyway, and here's why that's not crazy.

It's an emergency landing site. Under ETOPS rules, twin-engine jets flying long Pacific routes need somewhere to divert if an engine quits. Midway is one of very few options out there. No Midway, no diversion point, no flight path — or at least, a much longer, more expensive one.

The runway needs over a hundred million dollars in repairs, and this actually threatens a specific future flight: Qantas's planned nonstop from Sydney to London. Wild that a runway nobody lands on is quietly holding up one of the most ambitious routes in the industry.

Last one, quick hit. Canada might be about to hand over its four biggest airports — Toronto, Montreal, Vancouver, Calgary — to private operators. And there's already a $36 billion fund sitting there ready to buy in, built by two of Canada's biggest pension investors, seemingly designed for exactly this deal before it was even announced.

This is the same playbook we've seen with airports in the UK and Australia — governments cash out, pension funds become airport landlords, and passengers usually end up paying more in fees down the line. Worth watching where this lands.
