Episode title: LAX Near-Miss, a Cessna Flip at John Wayne, and American's Split Personality on First Class
Episode Summary: An Air France A350 and an American A321 come within 400 feet of each other over LAX, and a Cessna gets flipped by jet blast at John Wayne the same weekend. Plus, a Southwest flight attendant apparently quits mid-flight after a crew blowup, and American Airlines can't decide if it loves or hates first class.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Sunday, November 8th. Let's get into it.

So we've got two scary moments at two different airports, and they both happened basically back to back. Let's start at LAX. An Air France A350 and an American A321 both took off and ended up with about 400 feet of vertical separation between them. For context, that's closer than a 737 is long. Not a lot of room for error at six hundred feet a minute closing rate.

From what's being reported, one of the jets made a wrong turn after departure — basically didn't go where it was supposed to go — and air traffic control had to scramble to keep them apart. Quick calls from both the controllers and the pilots is the only reason this is a "close call" story and not a very different kind of story. These things are getting attention lately because when they happen at a hub as busy as LAX, there's zero margin. The NTSB tracks these near-miss reports closely, and historically, a cluster of them is usually a sign that controller staffing or procedures need a second look. Worth watching whether the FAA says anything official this week.

And then, same weekend, over at John Wayne Airport in Orange County, a Cessna 172 got flipped right over on the ramp. ATC audio suggests it got caught in the jet blast of a departing United 737. Airport officials are blaming wind. The Cessna's operator is saying, pretty clearly, it was the 737. I'll just say — small planes and jet blast have a long, well-documented history of not mixing well, and "it was windy" is a pretty convenient explanation when the alternative is "our departure procedure clipped a parked airplane." We'll see which story holds up.

Okay, something lighter — or at least weirder. A Southwest passenger says a flight attendant quit mid-flight after a loud argument with another crew member, in front of the whole cabin. Passengers were first told a flight attendant was "sick." Then the pilot got on the PA and called it "personality differences." Which, sure, that's one way to describe a coworker blowup bad enough that someone walks off the job between flights. Southwest's had a rough stretch of headlines lately — we talked about the tail damage report and the engine issue just this week — and this one's obviously much smaller, but it's not helping the vibe.

Last one, and it's a good one for showing how confused American Airlines' strategy looks right now. The airline just reopened Flagship Dining at LAX — that's the sit-down, restaurant-style meal service in the lounge — after a six-year hiatus, and it's now opening that up to more business class flyers, not just the old international first class crowd. Sounds like a nice upgrade, right? Except at the very same time, American is finishing a project adding first class seats to 32 Airbus A319s, while simultaneously ripping international first class out of its 777s. So it's adding first class here, killing it there, and widening lounge dining access in between. Doug Parker, the former American CEO, said this week that flying was never a commodity business. Which is a funny thing to say after spending a decade making American's product feel like exactly that. The airline's clearly trying to chase premium revenue again — everyone is, honestly — but right now it looks less like a strategy and more like a plane doing turns in two directions at once.

Quick one to close: Air Canada is swapping some of its 787 Dreamliner flights for Airbus A321XLRs. The XLR is a single-aisle jet that can fly widebody-length routes but burns way less fuel and costs way less to operate. It's a leisure-focused layout, only 182 seats, and it signals something bigger happening across the industry — airlines increasingly using narrowbodies to serve long, thin routes that used to require a widebody. Lower demand routes get a cheaper plane, high demand routes keep the big jets. That's the new math.
