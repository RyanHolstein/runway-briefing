Episode title: Boeing Finally Clears MAX 10, O'Leary Predicts Pain, and Everyone's Canceling Routes At Once
Episode Summary: The 737 MAX 10 just cleared its last hurdle after seven years of delays — but does anyone actually want it anymore? Plus, Ryanair's CEO says European airfares could jump 20% next year, American's about to make a massive bet on Asia, and two airlines quietly gut their route maps on the same day.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Tuesday, October 20th. Let's get into it.

So the big one. The 737 MAX 10 is finally certified. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy made it official today out at Boeing's Everett factory, and honestly, it's been a long time coming.

Remember, this plane was supposed to enter service back in 2019 with Southwest as the launch customer. Then two MAX 8 crashes happened, the whole family got grounded, and the MAX 10 got stuck behind years of extra scrutiny. There was even a last-minute scare with the navigation computer software that almost derailed this week's announcement.

Here's the thing though — the MAX 10 is the biggest, highest-capacity version of the MAX, but it also has the shortest range of the bunch. So the question isn't just "can Boeing deliver it," it's "do airlines still want it the way they did seven years ago?" A lot has changed. Airbus has been eating Boeing's lunch with the A321XLR this whole time, and some carriers have had years to rethink their fleet plans. Good news for Boeing's backlog, sure. But this is a plane that's arriving to a party that's already been going on without it.

Okay, Ryanair. Michael O'Leary stood up in Brussels today and said European airfares could jump 10 to 20 percent next year — the biggest increase in the region's history, by his own words.

Here's why this isn't just O'Leary being O'Leary. Ryanair's fuel hedging is incredible through March — eighty percent locked in at 67 bucks a barrel. But after that, they're only hedged 15 percent, at 85 bucks, which is roughly half of what fuel actually costs right now. So the cushion just disappears in April.

And it's not only fuel. EasyJet and Wizz Air are both cutting schedules too, which means fewer seats chasing the same demand. Less competition plus pricier fuel is a brutal combo for your wallet. I don't think fares spike exactly 20 percent on every route, but when the guy running Europe's biggest low-cost carrier is waving this flag, pay attention.

American might be about to make a huge move. Word is they could order up to 70 long-haul jets this week, and the leading theory is Airbus A350s loaded up with business class, aimed at rebuilding LA as their gateway to Asia.

This matters because American has been quietly losing the transpacific fight for years while United and Delta built bigger Asia networks. If this order is real, it's American finally admitting they need to spend real money to compete, not just shuffle around existing planes. Zach Griff and the View from the Wing crew have been tracking this one closely — worth watching when it's officially announced.

Meanwhile, two airlines are cutting in the opposite direction. Allegiant just killed 20 nonstop Florida routes across seven airports — Destin–Fort Walton Beach alone lost seven. And Aer Lingus is walking away from five destinations entirely.

Neither of these is random. This is the broader story we keep circling back to — carriers are trimming the stuff that doesn't pencil out anymore now that fuel's expensive and the post-pandemic leisure boom has cooled off. Allegiant's also adding new routes elsewhere, so it's less "we're shrinking" and more "we're done subsidizing routes that don't work." Still, if you live near Destin, bad week for you.

On a happier note — Alaska Airlines keeps building out Seattle as a real international hub. New nonstops to Osaka and Tahiti next year, both flown on borrowed Hawaiian A330s, which is a little funny — flying "Hawaiian" between Seattle and Japan without going anywhere near Hawaii. Osaka makes sense with Japan Airlines backing it up. Tahiti's trickier — Air Tahiti Nui actually abandoned that exact route before, so we'll see if Alaska can make the math work where someone else couldn't.

And quickly — Europe's top court just ruled that airlines don't actually own their historic airport slots, meaning regulators have more power to claw them back for competition. That's a bigger deal than it sounds, especially for crowded airports like Heathrow where slots are basically gold.
