Episode title: Mar-a-Lago Jet Tragedy, Ryanair Freezes Pay, and Southwest's Expiring Denials
Episode Summary: A Learjet goes down near Mar-a-Lago with nine on board, and we talk about what that nose-dive means for the investigation ahead. Then: Ryanair blames the Middle East conflict for a two billion dollar fuel hit and starts freezing paychecks, Boeing gets a surprising green light to sell jets to Belarus despite Russia fears, and Southwest's CEO denies a widebody order using the exact same playbook he used right before killing free bags.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Monday, November 2nd, 2026. Let's get into it.

So the big one today is heavy. A Learjet 60 went down near Mar-a-Lago in Florida, and witnesses are describing it as a near-vertical, almost missile-like dive. Nine people were on board. There were no survivors.

We don't know yet what caused that kind of dive. That's not a normal failure pattern, even for general aviation accidents, which is why investigators are going to be looking hard at this one. The Learjet 60 isn't some flimsy little prop plane either — it's a serious midsize business jet, flown by professional crews all over the world. And given where this happened, right next to one of the most watched patches of airspace in the country, expect this investigation to get way more scrutiny than your typical GA crash. We'll follow it as the NTSB digs in.

Okay. Ryanair. Michael O'Leary's airline just told investors that fares are going up "materially" next year unless the Middle East conflict cools off. Here's the thing — Ryanair's actually been protected so far because they hedged eighty percent of their fuel through March of 2027. Smart move, saved them a ton of pain everyone else has been feeling since March.

But that hedge runs out, and they're bracing for a two billion euro hit to next year's fuel bill. So what's the plan? Pay freezes across the company, more AI in the back office — which is corporate speak for fewer people in Dublin — and thinner cabin crew ratios on some flights. Classic Ryanair. Protect the margin first, figure out the passenger experience later.

Boeing's back in business with Belarus, sort of. The Commerce Department just cleared Boeing to sell parts and planes directly to Belavia, the Belarusian flag carrier, after sanctions got eased back in September of 2025. Belavia flies a dozen Boeing jets already, so this is really about keeping those planes flying with proper support.

Here's what bugs me about this one though. Belarus isn't exactly operating independently from Russia right now, and there's real concern that parts cleared for Belavia could quietly end up helping Russian aviation dodge its own sanctions. The U.S. is clearly betting that direct oversight beats a black market workaround. I'm not totally sold, but we'll see how it plays out.

Update on something we've basically seen before — Southwest. On their October earnings call, CEO Bob Jordan shot down those Reuters reports about Southwest eyeing 787s for long-haul flying, calling them "no credibility." Which, fine, except remember the bag fee saga? In September of 2024, Southwest told The Points Guy there was "no work currently underway" to end free bags. Six months later, bags weren't free anymore.

I'm not saying a widebody is coming next month. But Southwest's denials have a pretty short shelf life lately, and this is a company under serious pressure from Elliott Management to look more like a normal airline. A 787 order wouldn't shock me in six months. Neither would eating these words.

Quick one before we go. A Delta passenger says a flight attendant told her he snapped a photo of her sleeping on the plane and joked about posting it on Facebook — while his coworkers laughed. She said it left her feeling unsafe, which, yeah, that tracks. This isn't a policy story or a fleet story, it's just a reminder that crew professionalism still matters, and Delta's got some explaining to do here.
