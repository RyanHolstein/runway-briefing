Episode title: Amazon's 767 Crash Gets Grimmer, London Grinds to a Halt, and Delta Quietly Shrinks Vegas
Episode Summary: The NTSB drops new flight data from Sunday's fatal Amazon cargo crash in Miami, and it raises more questions than it answers. Heathrow and the rest of London's airports basically shut down after an air traffic control glitch, Delta trims five routes heading into winter, and American and Hyatt are calling it quits. Plus, GE just wrote an $11.75 billion check to fix a problem that's been grounding planes.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Wednesday, September 9th. Let's get into it.

So, update on that Amazon Prime Air crash in Miami we've been following. The NTSB released the final 30 seconds of flight data, and it's rough. The pilots applied thrust like they were going around — standard move if a landing doesn't look right. Then they aborted that go-around. And here's the part that's got everyone talking — they apparently never deployed the thrust reversers or the speed brakes.

Think about what that means. You're trying to stop a 767 on a runway, and the two biggest tools you have to slow the plane down just... didn't happen. We don't know why yet — could be a mechanical failure, could be a mix-up in the cockpit, could be something in between. The NTSB isn't speculating publicly, and neither will I. But three people are still in the hospital, and this is now looking less like a random accident and more like there's a real chain of decisions we need to understand. This one's going to take a while to fully unpack.

Meanwhile, London basically broke yesterday. A software bug hit NATS — that's the UK's air traffic control provider — and it choked departures out of all five major London airports for hours. So planes kept landing, nobody could take off, and eventually Heathrow and Gatwick just ran out of gates. Eurocontrol had to ban any more arrivals into London airports until they could dig out.

Tens of thousands of people got stranded. And because it's officially an "extraordinary circumstance," airlines don't owe compensation, just meals and hotels. Convenient timing for them. This is the second or third major ATC meltdown we've seen from a legacy system in the last couple years — Europe's air traffic infrastructure is genuinely showing its age.

Delta's trimming its network a bit for winter. Five routes are getting the axe — two out of JFK, one from LaGuardia, and two flights into Vegas from Southern California. Delta's blaming softer demand, and honestly, that Vegas cut tracks with what we've heard all year — the post-pandemic revenge-travel boom to Vegas has cooled off hard. New York's a slower burn, but between this and what we talked about with Delta pulling back at JFK last week, the pattern's pretty clear. Delta's not abandoning New York, but they're definitely trimming the fat.

Big loyalty program shakeup — American Airlines and Hyatt are breaking up. If you've been pooling points between the two, you've got a deadline. Link your accounts by November 15 if you want to keep earning through the transition, Hyatt's side closes out at the end of the year, American's runs a little longer into February. But the free nights, the elite status matching, the extra legroom perks — all of that eventually goes away.

This is the second shake-up to this partnership in two years, and it's part of a bigger trend — airlines keep quietly downgrading these cross-brand deals once the initial hype wears off. Great headline when you sign the partnership, less great when you're the one holding a stack of points wondering what they're worth now.

Last one, and this is a real inside-baseball story but it matters. GE Aerospace is buying a company called Consolidated Precision Products for eleven-point-seven-five billion dollars. That's not a small check. CPP makes castings — basically the heat-resistant metal parts that go inside jet engines and have to survive absolutely brutal conditions.

Here's why this matters — shortages of exactly these parts have been a huge reason engine deliveries have been backed up industry-wide, contributing to those aircraft groundings we've talked about all year. GE's CEO Larry Culp is basically buying his way out of a supply chain bottleneck instead of just complaining about it like everyone else has been. If it works, expect engine deliveries to speed up. If it doesn't, eleven billion dollars is a very expensive lesson.
