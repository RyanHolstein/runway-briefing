Weekly Deep Dive: AirBaltic's Bankruptcy and the A220's Reckoning

Episode Summary: AirBaltic just filed for Chapter 11, and it's not just a Latvian airline's problem — it's a stress test for the entire A220 program and the idea that betting your whole airline on one aircraft type is a smart strategy. Ryan and Hope dig into what actually broke at airBaltic, why the engines are the real villain of this story, and what it means for every other airline that's ordered hundreds of these jets.

RYAN: Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing Weekly. I'm Ryan, and I've got Hope with me.

HOPE: Hey! So this week we're digging into airBaltic filing for Chapter 11, which honestly sounds like a small regional story until you realize what it's actually about.

RYAN: Right, and that's exactly why we picked it. On the surface it's like, okay, small Baltic flag carrier, financial trouble, whatever. But this is the airline that went all in on the Airbus A220. Like, all in. And now it's in a New York bankruptcy court.

HOPE: A New York bankruptcy court. For a Latvian airline. That alone is a little wild.

RYAN: It's actually not that unusual, Chapter 11 is a US process but foreign airlines use it when they've got US-dollar debt or leased aircraft financed through American lessors, which airBaltic absolutely does. LATAM did the same thing back in 2020. But yeah, it's a very American process for a very Baltic problem.

HOPE: Okay so before we get into the A220 of it all, give me the airBaltic origin story. Who even are these guys?

RYAN: So airBaltic's been around since 1995, Latvian flag carrier, based at Riga. For most of its life it ran a mixed fleet, Boeing 737s, some Fokkers, De Havilland Dash 8s, the usual regional hodgepodge. Then around 2016 they made this decision that at the time got written up as visionary. They said, we're going all-Airbus, and specifically we're going all in on this new jet called the Bombardier CSeries.

HOPE: Which becomes the A220.

RYAN: Which becomes the A220, yeah, but at the time it was still a Bombardier product and airBaltic was basically the flagship European launch customer betting the whole airline on a plane that hadn't even really proven itself yet.

HOPE: And this is the plane that was supposed to fix regional aviation, right? Like I remember when this was being talked about as the biggest thing to happen to smaller jets in decades.

RYAN: It genuinely was pitched that way, and for good reason. Think about what came before it. You had the CRJs, the old Embraer E-Jets, these planes that were fine but fuel-hungry, cramped, and just not economical past a certain stage length. The CSeries, now A220, promised something totally different — a clean-sheet design, geared turbofan engines, way better fuel burn, a real five-abreast cabin that didn't feel like a sardine can, and range that let you fly transcontinental US routes or transatlantic hops with under 150 seats.

HOPE: So it's basically the plane that lets you run big-jet economics on a route that doesn't have big-jet demand.

RYAN: That's the pitch exactly. And Delta bought in early, JetBlue bought in, Air Canada, Korean Air, Egyptair. Breeze Airways built its entire startup around the A220. It became the it-plane of the last decade for anyone trying to do thin, long routes profitably.

HOPE: So if the plane is that good, why is the launch customer, the one who bet everything on it, now sitting in bankruptcy court?

RYAN: This is the million dollar question, or actually more like the eight-hundred-million-dollar question given the losses we're talking about. And the answer is not really the plane itself. It's everything around the plane.

HOPE: Okay, unpack that for me.

RYAN: So the first big problem, and this is the one that's honestly haunted the entire A220 program, is the Pratt & Whitney PW1500G engine. This is the geared turbofan that powers the A220, and it's had this ongoing saga of durability issues, contamination in the powder metal used in some turbine disks, requiring inspections and early removals way more often than anybody planned for.

HOPE: Wait, this is the same GTF issue that's been grounding planes for other airlines too, right? We literally talked about GE writing an eleven billion dollar check a few days ago on the daily show.

RYAN: Right, so that GE story was actually about the CFM LEAP engine issue for the daily, slightly different engine family, but same category of problem — modern geared and high-bypass engines running hotter and tighter tolerances than the old designs, and manufacturers underestimating how often they'd need overhaul. Pratt's version of this on the PW1500G has been arguably worse and it's hit the A220 fleet hard.

HOPE: So airBaltic's planes are just sitting on the ground because the engines need inspection.

RYAN: A meaningful chunk of their fleet has been grounded at various points over the last couple years for exactly that reason. And when you're an airline that only flies one aircraft type, there is no backup. If you're Delta and you've got ten different fleet types, you shuffle capacity around, you lease something else for six months, you absorb it. If you're airBaltic and every single one of your jets is an A220, and a third of them are grounded for engine inspections, you don't have a plan B. You just have empty gates.

HOPE: That's the concentration risk part of the story, right there.

RYAN: That's exactly it, and it's the core of why this matters beyond just one airline's balance sheet. AirBaltic's entire operational and financial model depended on fleet commonality delivering lower costs — one type of pilot training, one type of maintenance program, one parts inventory. And it does deliver that, in good times. But it also means you've concentrated all your operational risk onto a single point of failure, which in this case turned out to be an engine program that wasn't mature yet.

HOPE: Okay but I want to push back a little, because airlines have run single-type fleets before and been fine. Southwest ran an all-737 fleet for fifty years and became the most profitable airline in America doing it.

RYAN: That's a totally fair point and it's actually the counter-argument everyone's going to make this week. But there's a difference between single-type and single-everything. Southwest ran hundreds of 737s, an incredibly mature, decades-proven airframe with multiple engine options over its life, and Southwest had scale — enormous scale — to absorb any one issue. AirBaltic has, what, around fifty aircraft. It's a small airline betting on a brand new engine technology with basically no cushion.

HOPE: So it's not single-type that's the problem, it's small-and-single-type-and-new-technology all stacked together.

RYAN: Exactly, it's a pile-up of risks that individually might be manageable but together become existential. And then you layer geopolitics on top of it.

HOPE: Oh right, they're a Baltic airline. Latvia. That's basically Russia's backyard.

RYAN: Right next door. And since the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, Baltic airspace and Baltic tourism have both taken a hit. Russian airspace has been closed to European carriers, which lengthens a lot of eastbound routes and kills some markets entirely. Regional demand out of Riga just isn't what it was, and airBaltic's whole route network was built around being this connecting hub between Scandinavia, the Baltics, and points east and south.

HOPE: So the market they were built to serve kind of shrank underneath them.

RYAN: It shrank and it got more expensive to serve. And on top of the grounded planes and the shrinking market, they were also leasing out some of their own A220 capacity and crews to other airlines just to generate cash, which tells you they were already stretched thin operationally even before this filing.

HOPE: Wait, so they were wet-leasing their planes to other carriers to survive? That's a pretty telling sign of financial stress.

RYAN: It's a classic move when you're cash-strapped — you contract out your metal and crews to somebody who needs capacity, get some near-term revenue, but it also means you're not fully in control of your own schedule integrity. It's a Band-Aid, not a strategy.

HOPE: Okay so let's talk money. How bad is this actually, in dollar terms?

RYAN: The airline has been posting losses for a few years running, and estimates on total accumulated losses are in the hundreds of millions of euros at this point. The Latvian government, which owns the vast majority of the airline, has already pumped in a lot of state aid and equity over the past several years just to keep it capitalized, and that's actually part of the complication here, because the European Commission has state-aid rules that limit how many times you can bail out an airline before it starts looking like illegal market distortion.

HOPE: Oh, so the government can't just keep writing checks even if they wanted to.

RYAN: Right, there's real regulatory scrutiny on that, which is part of why Chapter 11 in the US, rather than just another government bailout, is the path here. It lets them restructure debt, potentially renegotiate aircraft leases and orders, and reorganize under court protection while — and this is the part they're emphasizing hard in every statement — continuing to operate flights.

HOPE: Right, they keep saying operations continue as normal. Is that actually believable, or is that just what every airline says right before things get messy?

RYAN: It's honestly usually true in the early going. This is the playbook — LATAM did it, Avianca did it back in 2020, even further back you had companies like Delta and United go through Chapter 11 in the 2000s and keep flying the whole time. The point of Chapter 11 versus liquidation is specifically that the business keeps operating while the balance sheet gets fixed. Planes keep flying, employees mostly keep their jobs, tickets you've already bought are usually honored.

HOPE: So this isn't a "the airline is about to disappear tomorrow" story.

RYAN: Not in the near term, no. But it is a "let's watch what gets cut, renegotiated, or spun off over the next six to twelve months" story. And that's where it gets really interesting for the A220 conversation, because part of what's on the table in restructuring is almost certainly their aircraft order book and lease terms with Airbus and various lessors.

HOPE: So this is where it circles back to Airbus.

RYAN: This is exactly where it circles back, and this is the part that should make Airbus's sales team a little nervous, or at least attentive. AirBaltic has more A220s on order to keep growing that fleet, and if a Chapter 11 restructuring means canceling or deferring some of those orders, that's real numbers coming off Airbus's backlog for a program that's still trying to hit production targets.

HOPE: Remind me where Airbus actually is with A220 production right now.

RYAN: They've been targeting a ramp up toward something like fourteen aircraft a month by the middle of this decade, and they've got a backlog north of five hundred aircraft in total across both the smaller dash-100 and bigger dash-300 variants. It's actually been one of the bright spots in Airbus's whole portfolio because unlike the A320neo family, which has this insane multi-year backlog choking everybody, the A220 has more room to actually sell into open white space in the regional market.

HOPE: So does one airline's bankruptcy actually threaten that ramp-up, or is it a rounding error for a program with five hundred plus jets on order?

RYAN: Honestly, in pure numbers, it's closer to a rounding error. AirBaltic's order is meaningful to them, it's basically their whole existence, but against Airbus's total backlog it's not going to derail the production ramp on its own. The bigger risk isn't the units, it's the narrative.

HOPE: What do you mean, the narrative?

RYAN: I mean every airline CFO evaluating whether to order A220s right now is going to have this story on their radar. It becomes ammunition in the boardroom for anyone who's skeptical of committing to a single fleet type, or skeptical of the PW1500G specifically. If you're Breeze Airways, who's also all A220 and also flying Pratt engines, people are going to be asking you this week whether you're the next airBaltic.

HOPE: Are they? Is Breeze actually in that kind of danger?

RYAN: I don't think it's a direct comparison honestly, and I want to be careful not to overstate this. Breeze is privately funded by David Neeleman with deep pockets behind it, it's US-based so it's not dealing with the same regional demand collapse from a war next door, and it doesn't have the same debt load airBaltic's built up over a decade. But Breeze has absolutely dealt with the same GTF engine groundings, and Breeze is also a single-type operator with zero fleet diversity to fall back on.

HOPE: So same structural vulnerability, different set of external pressures.

RYAN: Exactly, and that's actually the useful way to think about this whole story. It's not "the A220 is a bad plane," because it's genuinely not, operators love the fuel burn and the cabin and the range once it's actually flying. It's "single-type, single-engine-supplier strategies work great until the engine has a problem, and then you find out how much cushion you actually have." AirBaltic just happened to have the least cushion of anybody doing this bet.

HOPE: Let's talk about the other operators for a second, because I think this matters for people who fly these planes and don't even realize it. Delta's got A220s, JetBlue's got them, Air Canada, Korean Air, Egyptair. Are any of them in real trouble here?

RYAN: No, and this is important — none of those are single-type operators. Delta's got A220s as maybe ten percent of a fleet that also includes 737s, A321s, A330s, A350s, the whole rainbow. Same with JetBlue and Air Canada, these are diversified fleets where the A220 is a piece of the puzzle, not the entire puzzle. If their A220s get grounded for engine inspections, they shuffle other aircraft onto those routes and keep moving.

HOPE: So the lesson really is about the airlines that went all in, not about the plane being fragile.

RYAN: Right, and honestly the closest parallel in aviation history that I keep thinking about is what happened to some of the all-DC-10 or all-L-1011 operators back in the seventies and eighties, or more recently what almost happened to Norwegian Air Shuttle when they bet huge on the 787 for long haul and then Rolls-Royce Trent 1000 engine issues grounded a chunk of their widebody fleet at the worst possible time.

HOPE: Oh, I remember that story a little, that was brutal for them.

RYAN: It nearly killed Norwegian. They had planes sitting grounded, had to lease replacement widebodies at insane rates during peak season, and it was a huge factor in their financial collapse and eventual restructuring around 2021. Same exact shape of problem — small-ish carrier, single or dominant aircraft type, new engine technology that wasn't mature, external demand shock on top of it. History rhymes here.

HOPE: So this basically happens every decade with some airline that bets big on next-gen engine tech before it's fully proven.

RYAN: Pretty much, and it's almost a law of the industry at this point. New engine architectures, geared turbofans, higher bypass ratios, more exotic alloys, they promise incredible efficiency gains, and they deliver them eventually, but the first five to ten years of any new engine family tends to have durability surprises. The airlines that get burned worst are the ones with the least fleet flexibility to absorb it.

HOPE: Okay so let's get practical. What actually happens to airBaltic from here? Walk me through the next six months.

RYAN: So under Chapter 11 they'll go through a formal reorganization process, likely negotiating with Airbus on deferring or restructuring some of that A220 order, negotiating with lessors on lease terms for the aircraft they already operate, probably trimming some routes that aren't pulling their weight, and figuring out a capital structure that doesn't require the Latvian government to keep pouring in equity that runs into EU state-aid limits.

HOPE: Could they actually get sold to somebody, or merged into another airline?

RYAN: That's the interesting wildcard. There's been chatter for years about consolidation in the Baltic and Nordic space, whether it's SAS, which itself went through a painful Chapter 11 a few years back and got recapitalized with new ownership including Air France-KLM, or some other regional consolidation play. I don't think an outright sale is the most likely outcome in the next six months, but if the restructuring doesn't stick, it becomes a real possibility down the line.

HOPE: What about the government? Does Latvia just let its flag carrier get eaten by a foreign airline group?

RYAN: There's real national pride and strategic wrapped up in this — Riga positions itself as a connecting hub for the Baltics, and losing your flag carrier is a political problem, not just an economic one. So I'd expect the Latvian government to fight hard to keep majority control even through this process, probably converting some debt to equity, maybe bringing in a strategic partner for capital without giving up control outright.

HOPE: So if you had to bet, what's the actual outcome here? Does airBaltic come out the other side smaller but stable, or is this the beginning of the end?

RYAN: If I had to bet, I think they come out smaller. Fewer aircraft, a trimmed network, probably some of those A220 orders pushed back or canceled outright, and a much more conservative growth plan than the one they had a few years ago when they were talking about becoming this dominant Baltic-to-everywhere hub carrier. I don't think this is a liquidation story. I think it's a downsizing-and-survive story.

HOPE: And what does it mean for the A220 program itself, big picture? Like if you're Airbus's sales team right now, what's your talking point when a customer calls up nervous about this?

RYAN: Honestly, the talking point writes itself if you're being straight with people: the airplane works, the economics work when you're running it inside a diversified fleet with real backup capacity, and the failure here is about balance sheet fragility and engine maturity timing colliding with a small operator that had no room to absorb either. Airbus will probably also point to Pratt making real progress on inspection intervals and durability fixes as the fleet matures.

HOPE: So basically, don't blame the plane, blame the business model wrapped around the plane.

RYAN: That's the honest read. And I think the bigger industry lesson here, for anybody running an airline or thinking about ordering a new jet type, is that fleet commonality is a real cost advantage, it's not a myth, but it has to be paired with either massive scale or serious financial reserves, because commonality without a cushion just means one problem becomes every problem.

HOPE: It's kind of like putting all your retirement savings into one stock because the fundamentals look great. Fine until they don't.

RYAN: That's a great way to put it, honestly. Diversification exists as a concept for a reason, and airlines are not exempt from that logic just because the asset happens to be a Pratt & Whitney-powered regional jet instead of a stock ticker.

HOPE: Last thing — do you think other A220 customers pull back on future orders because of this?

RYAN: I don't think we see wholesale order cancellations. The backlog's too deep and the interest in the plane is too real. But I do think you see more customers structuring their fleets the smart way going forward, keeping the A220 as a piece of a diversified mix rather than betting the entire airline on it the way airBaltic did. In a weird way, airBaltic might end up being the cautionary tale that makes the rest of the A220 ecosystem healthier.

HOPE: A sacrifice that saves everybody else. Very aviation industry of them.

RYAN: Unfortunately, yeah. That's kind of how this business has always learned its lessons.
