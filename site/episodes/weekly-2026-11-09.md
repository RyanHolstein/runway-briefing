Weekly Deep Dive: Three Near-Misses in Seven Days — Is US Air Traffic Control Breaking Down?

Episode Summary: Three serious near-miss incidents in a single week — Miami, LAX, and John Wayne Airport — have aviation watchers asking if this is a statistical blip or a system finally buckling under years of controller shortages and post-COVID strain. Ryan and Hope dig into the history of ATC staffing crises, what the FAA and NTSB can actually do about it, and why this might be the moment airport capacity plans hit a wall.

RYAN: Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing Weekly. I'm Ryan, and I've got Hope with me.

HOPE: Hey! So this week we're digging into something that honestly freaked me out a little when I was reading through it — three near-miss incidents at major US airports in like seven days.

RYAN: Yeah. Miami, LAX, and John Wayne. And normally when we talk near-misses on the daily show, it's one incident, we cover it, we move on. This is different because it's a pattern.

HOPE: Right, and patterns are what you actually care about on this show.

RYAN: Patterns are everything. One near-miss is a bad day. Three in a week at three different towers is either a wild coincidence or it's telling you something about the system.

HOPE: Okay, so walk me through what actually happened, because I only half-absorbed this during the week.

RYAN: So Miami first, because that one's the scariest to me. Lufthansa 747, American 787, and they get described as "very close to an accident." The American jet turns into the Lufthansa's path. We're talking a widebody-on-widebody conflict at one of the busiest airports in the country.

HOPE: A 747. Those aren't exactly nimble.

RYAN: No, and that's part of what makes it terrifying. A 747 doesn't get out of the way fast. If there's a real conflict developing, the margin for error is basically however many seconds the controller has to catch it and however fast both flight crews can execute.

HOPE: And then LAX.

RYAN: LAX is actually the one with the most detail, because we've got the controller's actual words. Air France A350 takes off, turns toward an American A321, and you hear the controller go "traffic, 12 o'clock" — which is controller-speak for "there is an airplane directly in front of you and this is not a drill."

HOPE: Four hundred feet, right? That's the separation number I saw.

RYAN: Four hundred feet laterally, yeah, which at altitude, at speed, closing on each other — that's nothing. That's a blink. I mean, put it this way — a 737 is about forty feet wide. Four hundred feet is ten wingspans. At the speed these things are moving after takeoff, you're talking a few seconds of reaction time.

HOPE: And then the Cessna at John Wayne is a totally different flavor of incident.

RYAN: Totally different. That one's jet blast — a United 737's engine wash basically flips a small Cessna on the ground. Officials are blaming wind.

HOPE: Which, I noticed you did the thing with your eyebrows just now.

RYAN: Because "blame the wind" is the oldest trick in the aviation incident playbook. Sometimes it's true! Sometimes wind really is a factor. But when it's the third weird event in the same window, you start asking whether runway spacing, ground sequencing, and procedures around smaller aircraft near heavies are keeping up with how much traffic these airports are actually running now.

HOPE: Okay so here's my first dumb question. Are near-misses actually more common right now, or are we just hearing about more of them because people are paying attention after the Southwest stuff and the NTSB report from last week?

RYAN: It's a great question and it's not dumb at all, because this is the exact trap people fall into with crime statistics, or shark attacks, or whatever — more reporting doesn't mean more incidents. But I don't think that's what's happening here, because these aren't social media rumors. These are FAA-reportable events, controllers are on tape, there are NTSB and FAA review processes that get triggered.

HOPE: So these are real, verified, in-the-system incidents.

RYAN: Right. And when you zoom out, this isn't actually the first time we've seen a cluster like this. Go back to early 2023 — there was a run of runway incursions. JFK, a Delta and an American Airlines jet. Austin, a FedEx 767 nearly landing on top of a Southwest 737 that hadn't cleared the runway yet. Honolulu. Burbank. Like six or seven serious events in about two months.

HOPE: I remember that. That's when the FAA did the whole safety summit thing.

RYAN: Exactly, they called an emergency safety summit, had airlines, unions, everybody in a room. And the conclusion basically was — the system is running hotter than it's designed to run, and the margin that used to absorb small mistakes has shrunk.

HOPE: So this has happened before. Which honestly makes me feel a little better and a little worse at the same time.

RYAN: Right, better because the system caught it before, and worse because here we are again, three years later, having basically the same conversation.

HOPE: So what's actually underneath this? Like what's the root cause people are pointing to?

RYAN: The number everyone always comes back to is controller staffing. The FAA has been short roughly three thousand certified controllers nationally for years now. This isn't new — it goes back to the 2013 sequestration cuts, it goes back honestly all the way to the PATCO strike in 1981 when Reagan fired eleven thousand controllers and the system never fully recovered its pipeline.

HOPE: Wait, 1981 is still relevant to this?

RYAN: It's wild, but yes, because that strike created a hiring bubble — the FAA hired a huge wave of replacement controllers all at once, they all aged out and retired together decades later, and the FAA has been playing catch-up on training new ones ever since. Then you add the pandemic, which shut down the training academy in Oklahoma City for over a year.

HOPE: Right, because you can't exactly do controller training over Zoom.

RYAN: You cannot. It's extremely hands-on, extremely slow to certify people, and it takes two to three years to go from hire to fully certified controller at a major facility. So COVID didn't just pause hiring, it blew a hole in the pipeline that's still working its way through the system now, in 2026.

HOPE: So the people who would've been fully trained and seasoned by now just... aren't.

RYAN: Exactly. You've got facilities running on mandatory overtime, six-day work weeks, controllers working extended shifts because there just aren't enough bodies. And fatigue is one of the oldest, most well-documented contributors to human error in any safety-critical job.

HOPE: Okay but let me push back a little, because it feels a little too easy to blame everything on "not enough controllers." Miami and LAX aren't exactly rural towers. Those are two of the best-staffed, highest-priority facilities in the country.

RYAN: That's fair, and that's actually the more interesting layer here. It's not just raw numbers — it's complexity. These are also two of the busiest, most structurally complicated airspaces in America. LAX has parallel runway operations that require incredibly tight coordination. Miami's got heavy international traffic merging with domestic, different speeds, different aircraft types all stacking into the same corridors.

HOPE: So even with good staffing, the margin for error is just naturally thinner there.

RYAN: Right, and when you add any fatigue, any handoff confusion, any pilot who's a half-second slow to read back a clearance — in a lower-traffic airport that slack gets absorbed. At LAX or Miami, there's no slack to absorb it with.

HOPE: Okay, so let's talk about the pilot side too, because it feels like we're only blaming controllers so far, and two of these three incidents involve pilots doing unexpected turns.

RYAN: Good catch, because that is a real thread here. Miami — American's jet turning into Lufthansa's path. LAX — Air France turning toward the American jet. Both of those are flight crew actions, not controller actions, at least initially.

HOPE: So is this a training issue on the pilot side?

RYAN: It could be a few things. One theory going around — and I want to flag this is speculation, nobody's confirmed root cause yet — is that pilot experience levels have shifted since COVID. Airlines furloughed huge numbers of senior pilots in 2020, then had to hire back fast once travel demand roared back in 2022 and 2023. That means a chunk of the pilot workforce right now has fewer years in type, fewer years in these specific complex airports, than the workforce had pre-pandemic.

HOPE: So less institutional muscle memory.

RYAN: Exactly, less of that "I've flown into LAX four hundred times, I know exactly how this departure corridor behaves" instinct. You're relying more heavily on procedure and checklist discipline because the experience cushion is thinner.

HOPE: And procedure plus fatigue plus thin staffing on the ATC side is kind of a perfect storm.

RYAN: It's a convergence of several things that individually are manageable, and together are not. That's actually how most aviation safety failures work historically — it's never one big catastrophic thing, it's three or four small degraded things lining up. Swiss cheese model, right, the holes all lining up at once.

HOPE: Let's talk money, because I feel like every episode we do, it comes back to money somehow.

RYAN: It always comes back to money. So here's the thing — controller staffing is expensive to fix and slow to fix. You can't just hire three thousand controllers tomorrow. Training capacity at the academy is a bottleneck, certification time is a bottleneck. Congress has actually appropriated more money for this, there was a bipartisan push after the 2023 incident wave to boost academy throughput and controller pay to retain people.

HOPE: Did it work?

RYAN: Partially. Hiring numbers are up, academy class sizes are bigger. But remember, two to three years to fully certify someone at a major facility means anyone hired in that 2023 push is only just now becoming useful at a place like Miami or LAX, if they went to a major facility at all. A lot of new hires start at smaller towers and work their way up.

HOPE: So we're basically still in the valley of the staffing fix, not at the peak of it.

RYAN: Right, we're mid-fix, not post-fix. And that's exactly where you'd expect incidents to still be happening, honestly.

HOPE: Okay, so what does this cost the airlines? Because near-misses don't make headlines about dollars, but there's gotta be exposure here.

RYAN: There's exposure in a few ways. First, direct — if the FAA or NTSB determines procedural fault on an airline's part, you can see operational restrictions, additional training mandates, potentially fines, though fines in these cases are rare compared to the reputational hit. Second, insurance — near-miss clusters at major hubs tend to nudge hull and liability insurance conversations industry-wide, even without an actual collision.

HOPE: Insurance companies pay attention to near-misses?

RYAN: Insurers absolutely pay attention to near-misses, because near-misses are the leading indicator for the thing they actually have to pay out on. The entire logic of aviation safety management systems is built on that idea — you treat every near-miss like a free data point about a crash that didn't happen, and you fix the conditions before the data point becomes a fatality.

HOPE: That's actually a pretty smart way to think about it. Near-misses as free lessons.

RYAN: It's the whole philosophy behind modern aviation safety, and it's genuinely one of the reasons flying is as safe as it is. The system is obsessive about near-misses specifically because it refuses to wait for an actual crash to learn the lesson.

HOPE: So what actually happens next, procedurally? Like, does the NTSB show up and reassign people?

RYAN: So for each of these, there'll be an incident investigation — NTSB typically takes lead on anything serious enough, FAA runs parallel administrative review. They pull the tower tapes, the cockpit voice recorders if applicable, radar track data, and they reconstruct second by second what each person said, heard, and did.

HOPE: And then?

RYAN: Then you get a report, usually months later, with findings and recommendations. Past incident clusters like this have led to things like revised phraseology requirements — literally changing the exact words controllers and pilots are required to use, because ambiguous language has caused real accidents historically.

HOPE: Give me an example.

RYAN: The granddaddy example is Tenerife, 1977, worst aviation disaster ever, 583 dead, and a massive contributing factor was ambiguous radio phrasing — a KLM captain saying something that sounded like a takeoff clearance when it wasn't actually one. That disaster is the reason modern phraseology is as rigid and over-specified as it is. "Cleared for takeoff" has to mean exactly one thing, every time, worldwide.

HOPE: Wow, okay, so these near-miss reviews could actually change the literal words controllers are required to say at LAX or Miami specifically.

RYAN: Potentially, yeah, especially around parallel runway operations or merging traffic situations, since that's the common thread in Miami and LAX. You might also see recommended changes to minimum separation standards in specific congested corridors, or additional technology requirements — stuff like more aggressive use of runway incursion warning systems.

HOPE: Which exist already, right? Like airports have tech for this.

RYAN: They do, systems like ASDE-X that track ground movement and should flag conflicts, but the rollout and sensitivity tuning of these systems varies by airport, and there's always a human in the loop who has to act on the alert fast enough.

HOPE: So even good tech doesn't fully solve the human bottleneck.

RYAN: No technology solves it completely, because somebody still has to look at the screen, interpret it correctly, and act in the three or four seconds they've got. That's the uncomfortable truth in all of this — you can throw money and software at the problem, but air traffic control is fundamentally a human cognition problem at the sharp end.

HOPE: Okay, let's get speculative, because that's the fun part. If you had to bet, what actually happens in the next six months?

RYAN: I think you get a formal FAA statement acknowledging a review is underway, possibly another safety summit like 2023, because that's become the political playbook now — show urgency without committing to anything that costs real money upfront. I think you get targeted phraseology or procedure tweaks at Miami and LAX specifically within a few months.

HOPE: What about staffing? Does this finally force Congress's hand on a bigger funding push?

RYAN: I'm a little skeptical there, honestly, because Congress already did a funding push in 2023 and the headlines kind of moved on. For this to really force new money, you'd probably need an actual collision, which is a horrible thing to say, but historically that's the pattern — near-misses get studies, actual accidents get budget.

HOPE: That's bleak but I think you're right. Tenerife is the example you just gave — it took five hundred and eighty-three deaths to fix a radio phrase.

RYAN: Right, and it's not that regulators are callous, it's that political will and budget allocation in this country tends to be reactive rather than preventive, across basically every domain, not just aviation.

HOPE: So what about airport expansion and capacity? Because this week we also talked about Qantas retiring the A380, airlines adding bigger jets, more routes everywhere. If these major airports are already stretched thin on the human side, how does that square with everyone wanting to fly bigger planes into the same slots?

RYAN: This is honestly the angle that worries me most long term. You've got airlines chasing capacity growth — more widebodies, more international routes stacking into JFK, LAX, Miami — at the exact same time the human infrastructure managing that airspace is still recovering. Slot allocation fights, by the way, we literally talked about this with Heathrow and Delta earlier this week.

HOPE: Right, Delta threatening to sue over the Heathrow terminal shuffle.

RYAN: Same underlying tension, just a different flavor — infrastructure and capacity decisions getting made by business and political priorities, while the operational reality on the ground, or in the tower, isn't necessarily keeping pace. Airports want more flights because more flights means more landing fees, more concession revenue. Airlines want more flights because growth is the whole business model. Nobody's incentive structure says "actually, let's fly fewer planes into LAX so our margin for error gets bigger."

HOPE: That's a genuinely uncomfortable thought. The business incentives all point toward adding stress to a system that's already showing cracks.

RYAN: That's exactly it. And historically that tension doesn't resolve itself gracefully — it resolves through a forced correction, usually after something bad happens. I'd love to be wrong about that.

HOPE: Do you think passengers should actually be worried? Like, should someone flying out of LAX next week feel nervous about this?

RYAN: Statistically, no, not really — commercial aviation remains incredibly safe, these systems have enormous redundancy built in specifically so that a near-miss stays a near-miss instead of becoming something worse. That's literally what "near-miss" means — the safety margin held, even if it held by less than anybody would like.

HOPE: But the margin held three times in one week, which is also kind of the point of this whole conversation.

RYAN: Right, that's the tension — I can simultaneously tell you flying is safe and tell you this pattern is a warning sign worth taking seriously, because those two things aren't actually contradictory. The system worked. But a system that only works because humans catch mistakes at the last second isn't a system with much margin left to give.

HOPE: So if I'm boiling this down for our listener who's got a flight out of one of these airports next month — what's the actual takeaway?

RYAN: I think the takeaway is, this isn't a story about any one airline or any one bad pilot or controller. It's a story about a system running close to its limits after years of staffing gaps and a pandemic-sized hole in the training pipeline, and three incidents in one week is the system basically waving a flag saying "pay attention to this now, before it waves a much bigger flag later."

HOPE: Not exactly comforting, but at least it's honest.
