Episode title: KLM's Ex-CEO Sets Business Class on Fire, Delta Piles Into LAX, and AirAsia Says It's Fine, Actually
Episode Summary: A former KLM CEO becomes the star of his own airline's power bank nightmare somewhere over the Atlantic. Plus, Delta goes all in on Los Angeles with its biggest schedule ever, AirAsia insists it's not collapsing despite the government quietly lining up a backup plan, and a private jet broker sits on $150 million of your money while it "reviews things."

Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Friday, September 18th. Let's get into it.

So this is one of those stories you couldn't make up. A KLM 777 flying from Amsterdam to Curaçao had to turn around over the Atlantic yesterday because a power bank in business class caught fire and exploded. Scorch marks up the window, up the wall, the whole thing. Crew doused it, dunked it in water, emergency diversion back to Schiphol.

Here's the part that makes it a story instead of just an incident. The passenger is reportedly Camiel Eurlings — KLM's own former CEO, ran the airline from 2010 to 2014. Reports say he fell asleep while the power bank was charging, which is exactly the thing every airline tells you never to do, because if you're asleep, nobody's watching it when it starts venting.

I don't really have a joke here better than the actual facts. The guy who used to run the airline set off the exact safety hazard the airline warns you about, on the airline he used to run. That's not irony, that's poetry. Anyway, everyone's fine, minor injuries only, but lithium battery fires on planes are becoming a real recurring problem industry-wide, and this is going to get cited in every one of those stories for a while.

Okay, Delta. They announced a new LAX to Philadelphia route today, plus extra flights on seven other routes out of LA — Boston, Houston, Seattle, San Diego, that kind of thing. Doesn't sound huge on its own, but stack it up: this is the second new LAX route Delta's announced this week, after they said they're bringing back Monterey after a 19 year absence.

Delta's basically trying to build a wall around Los Angeles. They've added Hong Kong, Melbourne, Manila, and Vancouver's coming back in November. Next year is going to be their biggest schedule ever out of LAX. Why does this matter? Because LAX is the one major West Coast gateway where nobody has a dominant hub advantage — Alaska, United, American, Southwest are all fighting for it too. Delta's playing the same game they're playing in Austin against American, basically: outspend everyone on capacity before the market settles, and lock in the loyal flyers and the credit card business before anyone else can.

Now, AirAsia. The Malaysian budget carrier spent this week publicly insisting it's not about to collapse, which — if you have to say that out loud, it's usually not going great. Turns out the government has apparently been in quiet talks with rival airlines about who'd absorb AirAsia's domestic routes if it does go under.

The root problem: jet fuel spiked 66% for AirAsia last quarter because, unlike Ryanair, they don't hedge fuel. They just buy at whatever the market rate is. And with the Gulf situation still pushing oil prices up, that's brutal for a low-cost carrier running on thin margins to begin with. AirAsia says they've clawed back about 70% of the cost through fares and capacity cuts. Maybe that's true. But when a government's already drawing up a Plan B behind your back, that tells you something about how nervous people actually are.

Quick one — American's CEO Robert Isom said yesterday that if fuel prices don't come down, American might have to cut capacity too. We've talked about this fuel spike all week, United and Southwest are already doing it quietly. Now it's not quiet anymore, American's saying it out loud on an earnings call. That's three of the big four US carriers now openly signaling cuts. Watch this one, because if fares start creeping up this fall, this is why.

Last thing, and this one should scare you if you fly private. A charter broker called ONEflight just paused all flights for at least 30 days — "or until further notice," which is never a phrase you want attached to your money. They're sitting on more than 150 million dollars in prepaid customer funds. Remember, brokers like this aren't airlines, they don't own planes, they just resell flights from operators and pocket the spread. When the money coming in doesn't match the money going out to actual operators, this is what happens. If you've got a jet card with these guys, I'd be making some phone calls today.
