Weekly Deep Dive: United Just Told You Loyalty Doesn't Pay What It Used To

Episode Summary: United Airlines just raised MileagePlus status requirements by as much as 45.5% while quietly stripping out benefits — and it's not alone. Ryan and Hope dig into the forty-year history of frequent flyer programs, the billions airlines now make selling miles to banks, and why the entire industry seems to be betting, all at once, that you're too loyal to leave.

RYAN: Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing Weekly. I'm Ryan, and I've got Hope with me.

HOPE: Hey! So this week we're digging into the United MileagePlus overhaul, which, I'll be honest, when I first read the headline I thought, okay, another loyalty program tweak, who cares. But then I actually read the details.

RYAN: Yeah, this isn't a tweak. This is United basically rewriting the rules of what it means to be a loyal customer, and doing it in the same week Southwest gutted its Companion Pass. Two major carriers, same month, moving in the same direction.

HOPE: Which already makes me suspicious. Like, is this a coincidence or is this a memo that went around?

RYAN: I want to get into that, because it's a real question. But let's back up first, because I don't think people understand how we even got here. Frequent flyer programs are forty-five years old at this point.

HOPE: Forty-five years. Okay, origin story, go.

RYAN: American Airlines launches AAdvantage in 1981. And the genius of it, at the time, wasn't really about rewarding loyalty, it was a response to deregulation. Suddenly there's price competition everywhere, and American needs a way to lock in business travelers without just slashing fares.

HOPE: So it was never really about the free flights, it was about handcuffs.

RYAN: Golden handcuffs, yeah, from day one. And it worked incredibly well. Within a year United and Delta both launch their own programs because they have to, or they're going to bleed business travelers to American. And for like twenty years the model is pretty simple, you fly miles, you earn miles, you redeem miles for flights.

HOPE: Which is the version of this I grew up thinking was the whole thing. Fly enough, get a free ticket to see your aunt in Phoenix.

RYAN: Right, but the real shift happens around 2005 to 2015, when airlines figure out something way more lucrative than flying you around.

HOPE: Which is?

RYAN: Selling miles to banks. Delta SkyMiles American Express, United MileagePlus Chase, these co-brand card deals become insanely profitable, to the point where Delta's deal with Amex is worth something like seven billion dollars a year now in total value. That's not a rewards program anymore, that's a financial product.

HOPE: Wait, seven billion. From Delta.

RYAN: From one card partnership. And this is the thing people miss — during the pandemic, when airlines were on their knees, begging for bailouts, what did they put up as collateral for loans?

HOPE: Oh, I remember this. The loyalty programs themselves.

RYAN: United borrowed against MileagePlus and it was valued at something like twenty-two billion dollars. More than the airline's own market cap at the time. So we're not talking about a perk program anymore, we're talking about the single most valuable asset most of these airlines own.

HOPE: Okay so that's the context that makes this week's news actually matter. Because if the loyalty program is worth more than the airline, every decision about it is basically a financial engineering decision, not a customer service decision.

RYAN: Exactly. And that's why Kirby's comments this week landed the way they did. He wasn't cagey about it. He basically said the quiet part out loud — we're going to monetize every touchpoint we can, paid upgrades, seat selection fees, all of it, and he's betting people stick around anyway.

HOPE: Can we talk about what actually changed, though? Because I want to make sure listeners who didn't obsess over this like you did understand the mechanics.

RYAN: Yeah, so the headline number is United raised status requirements by up to 45.5 percent depending on the tier. That's a massive jump in one move. Historically these programs creep up five, ten percent a year if that. This is them ripping the band-aid off all at once.

HOPE: And the mechanism changed too, right? Not just the number.

RYAN: This is the part that actually matters more than the percentage. United is moving away from counting miles flown and toward counting segments flown. So it used to be, if you flew fewer, longer flights — say you're doing transatlantic business class twice a month — you could rack up status through sheer mileage even without flying that often.

HOPE: Right, the guy who flies New York to Tokyo in business four times a year used to be golden.

RYAN: Used to be golden, exactly. Under the new system, segments matter more than distance. So now you actually have to fly more often, more legs, to hit the same status. Which punishes exactly the kind of premium long-haul flyer the program used to reward.

HOPE: That seems like a weird group to punish though. Aren't those your highest-value customers? The people paying for business class seats?

RYAN: You'd think so. But here's Kirby's logic, and it's actually kind of coherent if you're cold-blooded about it. Those premium long-haul passengers are already paying a premium fare. United's already extracting a lot of revenue from them per trip. What United wants more of is frequency — the road warrior flying Newark to Chicago every single week on a cheap fare, who's incredibly sticky, incredibly habitual, and who they can upsell constantly on ancillary fees.

HOPE: So it's less about who spends the most per ticket and more about who they can nickel and dime the most times per year.

RYAN: That's a really good way to put it. It's lifetime touchpoints over total dollars. They'd rather have you in the airport forty-three times a year getting hit with a seat upgrade offer each time than four times a year on a fancy ticket.

HOPE: Okay that's actually a good segue, because one of the pieces I read this week was literally that, somebody who's flown United forty-three times this year writing about how bad this is for him specifically.

RYAN: Right, and that's the most honest reaction piece out there, because he's exactly the demographic this was allegedly designed to protect, and even he's getting squeezed. Free checked bags getting cut back, lounge access getting harder to reach — these are things road warriors relied on as basic infrastructure, not luxury.

HOPE: Which raises the question I keep coming back to. If you make the program worse for your most loyal flyers, at what point do they actually leave? Like, is there a breaking point, or is Kirby right that there isn't one?

RYAN: This is the billion dollar question, literally. And historically, airlines have been right more often than wrong that there isn't a breaking point, at least not a sudden one. Switching costs are huge. If you've got ten years of status history with United, a stack of miles, a co-brand card with an annual fee you're already paying, the psychological and practical cost of starting over at zero with Delta or American is enormous.

HOPE: It's like a gym membership you hate but you've already paid for the year.

RYAN: Sort of, except worse, because the gym membership doesn't compound. Your miles balance, your status tenure, your Global Services recognition if you've built up a relationship over a decade — all of that resets to nothing if you switch. Airlines know this. They're playing a very cynical, very accurate read of consumer behavior.

HOPE: But isn't there precedent for people actually leaving when a devaluation goes too far? I feel like I've heard this story before.

RYAN: You have, and it's a great parallel. 2014, Delta announces it's moving to revenue-based mileage earning instead of distance-based, so suddenly how much you pay matters way more than how far you fly. American and United follow within about a year. There was outrage, blog posts, boycotts threatened.

HOPE: And?

RYAN: And basically nothing happened. Enrollment didn't drop, card signups didn't drop, the programs got more profitable. That's actually the precedent that gives Kirby the confidence to do what he's doing now. The industry ran this exact experiment eleven years ago and the answer was, people complain loudly online and then keep flying.

HOPE: Okay but I want to push back a little, because the online complaining this time feels different in volume. Is that just because social media's bigger now, or is something actually different?

RYAN: I think two things are different. One, this time it's not just one airline doing it in isolation, it's United and Southwest basically simultaneously, and American and Delta are reportedly running their own elite tier reviews too. So there's no obvious "just switch to the airline that didn't do this" option the way there was in 2014.

HOPE: Right, in 2014 you could at least threaten to defect to whichever airline hadn't devalued yet.

RYAN: Exactly, and that threat had some teeth because airlines compete for loyalty share. This time it looks coordinated enough, or at least convergent enough, that there's no safe harbor. If everyone's tightening the screws at once, where do you even go?

HOPE: Which is either really smart collusion-adjacent strategy or it's just that they're all staring at the same cost pressures and landing on the same answer independently.

RYAN: I actually lean toward the second explanation, and I think it's more interesting than conspiracy. These airlines aren't on a group call planning this together, that would be an antitrust nightmare. What's happening is they're all looking at the same data: elite status populations have ballooned over the last decade because of pandemic status extensions, credit card status shortcuts, all these side doors into premium tiers.

HOPE: Oh, this is the thing where everybody on your flight seems to have status now.

RYAN: Right, status inflation. If forty percent of the cabin is Premier Gold or above, the upgrade pool is overwhelmed, lounges are packed past capacity, and the status itself stops meaning anything, which actually hurts the airline's ability to sell the aspirational version of status to new customers. So part of this devaluation is genuinely about restoring scarcity.

HOPE: Scarcity as a feature, not a bug.

RYAN: Exactly, you need status to feel exclusive for it to retain value as a thing people chase. If everyone has gold status, gold status is worthless as a differentiator, and then why would anyone bother chasing it.

HOPE: Okay but then that's a slightly different motive than pure monetization, right? That's more like protecting the brand value of status itself.

RYAN: It's both, honestly, and I think that's what makes Kirby's interview this week so useful for understanding the strategy. He wasn't just talking about status requirements, he was talking about seat fees, paid upgrades, the whole monetization stack. The status changes are one piece of a much bigger philosophy, which is basically, nothing is free anymore, everything is a revenue opportunity, and the plane itself is a retail environment.

HOPE: The flying Costco.

RYAN: I mean, kind of, yeah. Southwest's doing this too, and Southwest's case is actually the more dramatic one in some ways, because Southwest's entire brand for fifty years was "we don't nickel and dime you." Bags fly free, no change fees, no seat assignments to upsell.

HOPE: Right, that was literally their advertising for decades.

RYAN: And this year they killed open seating, they started charging for bags, and now they've made the Companion Pass — which was the single best loyalty perk in the entire industry, arguably — significantly harder to earn. That's Southwest explicitly abandoning the thing that made it Southwest.

HOPE: Can we talk about why the Companion Pass mattered so much? Because I don't think people outside the loyalty nerd world appreciate it.

RYAN: Sure, so the Companion Pass let you designate someone who flies free, just taxes and fees, on every single one of your flights for up to two years, once you hit a certain threshold of flights or points. It was genuinely one of the best deals in consumer travel, period, way better than anything the legacy carriers offered. It's why Southwest had this insanely devoted following of points-and-miles people who'd structure their entire travel life around hitting that threshold.

HOPE: And now?

RYAN: Now the threshold's higher, some of the qualifying methods got stripped out, and functionally it's going to be a lot fewer people earning it. Which tells you Southwest looked at the math and said, we're giving away too much value for too little in return.

HOPE: So across the board, everyone's recalibrating the exchange rate, and the exchange rate is getting worse for the customer.

RYAN: That's the one-sentence summary of basically this entire era of the industry, yeah.

HOPE: Let's talk money for a second, because I want real numbers. How much are we actually talking about here, industry-wide?

RYAN: So if you look at the big loyalty programs combined — American's AAdvantage, Delta SkyMiles, United MileagePlus, Southwest Rapid Rewards — you're talking tens of billions of dollars a year in value, mostly flowing through credit card partnerships. Delta's Amex relationship alone, as I said, is pushing seven billion a year and growing. United's Chase deal isn't far behind in relative terms. These deals have been renegotiated upward multiple times in the last decade specifically because the banks keep wanting more cardholders and the airlines keep wanting bigger guaranteed payments.

HOPE: So the banks are basically subsidizing the airline's whole operation at this point.

RYAN: In some real sense, yes. Analysts have pointed out for years that these loyalty programs, especially post-bankruptcy valuations, are often worth more standalone than the airline's core flying operation. United literally spun pieces of this out as collateral during Covid because lenders trusted the loyalty business more than they trusted the airline business.

HOPE: Which is wild, because the loyalty program doesn't exist without the airline flying planes.

RYAN: Right, it's symbiotic, but the point is the mileage business has become the actual profit engine, and the flights themselves are almost the delivery mechanism for mileage-earning opportunities. That's an inversion of how you'd assume this works.

HOPE: Okay, so here's my angle I want you to speculate on. If elite status gets harder and harder to earn through flying, and the whole point of these co-brand cards is "get status through spending on the card instead," doesn't that create tension between the airline and the bank?

RYAN: This is honestly the most interesting under-the-radar part of this story. Because the card issuers — Chase, Amex, Citi — their entire pitch to new cardholders is, "get this card, and status is within reach." If United makes actual flying-based status dramatically harder while leaning on cards to backfill it, that's fine for United short term, because card spend and card fees are pure margin. But eventually, if status becomes something you basically buy with spend rather than earn by flying, the whole prestige mechanism of status collapses.

HOPE: Because then it's not actually about being a frequent flyer, it's about being a frequent spender, which is a totally different value proposition.

RYAN: Exactly, and that's a real tension, because the banks want status to feel attainable enough that people sign up for the card, but the airline wants status to feel exclusive enough that it still means something when you get upgraded in front of other passengers. Those two goals are in direct conflict, and I don't think anyone's fully solved it yet.

HOPE: So who actually wins in all this, if we're doing a scorecard? Because it feels like, so far, we've mostly talked about who loses.

RYAN: Winners first, then. United wins in the near term, financially — they extract more revenue per flight, they thin the elite tier population back down to something manageable, their cost per upgrade and per lounge visit goes down. Banks win too, arguably, because card spend becomes even more important as the path to status, so people spend more on the card to compensate.

HOPE: Who loses?

RYAN: The mid-tier frequent flyer loses hardest. Not the ultra-elite, who still get taken care of because they're the top of the pyramid and airlines protect that relationship fiercely. Not the occasional flyer, who never had status anyway and isn't losing anything they had. It's that middle band, the forty-three-trips-a-year guy, the person who built an entire travel strategy around hitting Premier Platinum, who's now getting told the goalposts moved and he has to fly even more to stay where he was.

HOPE: That's such a specific kind of betrayal too, because that person probably structured years of their life, which flights to book, which routes to fly, around hitting a number.

RYAN: Right, and it's worth saying, this isn't new in the sense that devaluations happen, but the scale this time, 45.5 percent in requirements plus benefit cuts simultaneously, that's a bigger single jump than I can remember in a long time. Normally it's incremental enough that people grumble and adjust. This is a gap that's hard to just adjust around.

HOPE: So what do you think actually happens next? Like, give me your prediction.

RYAN: I think we see one of two things, maybe both. One, we see some meaningful attrition at the margin, not a mass exodus, but enough mid-tier flyers who are geographically flexible — people who live in multi-airline hub cities like Chicago or Dallas — start deliberately splitting loyalty or shifting toward whichever competitor hasn't devalued as aggressively yet.

HOPE: Arbitrage opportunity.

RYAN: Exactly, and this is actually a case where the "everyone's doing it together" theory has a hole in it, because if even one of the big four doesn't devalue as hard, or times its devaluation differently, they become the arbitrage play for serious points people for a year or two, until they presumably follow suit.

HOPE: And the second thing?

RYAN: The second thing is regulatory attention, actually. There's been noise in Washington for a couple years now about loyalty program transparency, whether these programs should be regulated more like financial products since they functionally are one at this scale. If devaluations keep accelerating and getting more aggressive, I wouldn't be shocked to see a push for disclosure requirements, maybe DOT getting involved the way they did with the tarmac delay rules. It wouldn't stop devaluation, but it might force more notice periods, more transparency around how status math works.

HOPE: Do you think that actually has teeth though, or is that a "congressional hearing that goes nowhere" situation?

RYAN: Probably the second one, if I'm honest. Airlines have an incredibly strong lobby, and loyalty programs, unlike baggage fees or tarmac delays, don't generate the same kind of visceral public sympathy. Nobody's going viral over not getting a free lounge visit the way they do over sitting on a plane for six hours.

HOPE: Fair. So bottom line, you think this sticks, this becomes the new normal, and the industry just keeps pushing the line forward.

RYAN: I think it sticks, yeah. I think the real test isn't this year, it's two or three years from now, when we see whether premium long-haul traffic patterns actually shift, whether United starts seeing softness in business class loads because the people who used to chase status on those routes stopped caring. That's the actual signal to watch, not the social media outrage this week.

HOPE: Which is a very unsatisfying answer in the sense that it means we won't really know if this was a mistake for years.

RYAN: Welcome to airline strategy, nothing's confirmed right or wrong until the earnings calls two years out.

HOPE: Well, on that deeply uncertain note, I think that's a great place to leave it.
