Runway Briefing: United's Stranded Seats, Delta's Hawaii Whiplash, and JetBlue Buys Its Way Into LaGuardia
Episode Summary: United bought hundreds of fancy lie-flat seats for a plane that still doesn't exist, and now they might not even fit. Delta can't decide if it wants to fly to Hawaii or not. Plus, JetBlue pays nearly sixty million bucks for old Spirit slots, and United's new app feature quietly makes life miserable for airline employees flying standby.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Tuesday, September 1st. Let's get into it.

So, United. Get this — they bought hundreds of lie-flat business class seats. For a plane that doesn't exist yet.

This is for the 737 MAX 10, the big one Boeing still hasn't certified. It's been "almost done" for years now, kind of like that home renovation your neighbor keeps talking about. United ordered the seats early, expecting the jet, and now they're just sitting in a warehouse somewhere.

Here's the kicker, reported by View from the Wing — those seats might need structural changes before they even fit once the plane shows up. Right now the only real backup plan is sticking some of them on Hawaii routes. So United spent real money on premium seats for a plane with no delivery date, and the backup plan is "well, maybe Hawaii." That's not a fleet strategy, that's a garage sale waiting to happen.

Speaking of Hawaii — Delta, what are you doing.

Two stories today, same theme. Delta is cutting short a brand-new seasonal Hawaii route before it even really gets going, running mid-December to early January and then, poof, gone. And separately, they just killed the Boston to Honolulu flight — again. This is the third time in two years Delta has launched or relaunched this route and then quietly walked it back.

Remember, Delta's whole pitch lately is "we're becoming the next Singapore Airlines." Ed Bastian's been saying that publicly. But you can't build a premium global carrier while your Hawaii network looks like an Etch A Sketch. Either the demand's not there or the scheduling's a mess — probably both. Either way, I'd hold off booking anything Delta to Hawaii more than a few months out.

Okay, more useful news. The FAA has tentatively signed off on JetBlue buying Spirit's old slots at LaGuardia. Price tag: fifty-eight and a half million dollars.

This matters because LaGuardia slots are basically the aviation version of Manhattan real estate — there's a hard cap, nobody's building more, and the only way in is buying someone out. Spirit's been circling bankruptcy for what feels like forever, so this is JetBlue picking through the wreckage of a merger that never happened, one slot pair at a time. Good move for JetBlue's Northeast footprint. Rough footnote in Spirit's slow fade.

Now, United's app has a new feature, and depending who you ask, it's either genius or a nightmare. If your flight gets messed up, you can now list yourself for standby on up to three earlier flights while keeping your confirmed seat on the one you got rebooked to. The app watches those flights and texts you if a seat opens up. Genuinely smart, and it's rolling out right in time for Labor Day chaos.

But — and Paddle Your Own Kanoo pointed this out — this is terrible news if you're a non-rev employee flying standby. Those open seats used to be your shot. Now paying passengers with a safety net are hoovering them up first. So a great customer feature for one group just became a much harder Tuesday for another.

Last one, and it's small but it made me laugh. JetBlue has quietly changed its boarding announcement. For years flight attendants said "you've got the most legroom in coach." Not anymore.

Why? Because JetBlue's rolling out a new domestic first class recliner section, and to make room they're shuffling around the Even More seats and shrinking things up in back. JonNYC caught the new seatmap on X. So the airline that built its whole economy-class brand on legroom is now quietly backing away from that claim the same week they're adding a first class cabin. Nothing's free, folks — least of all your knees.
