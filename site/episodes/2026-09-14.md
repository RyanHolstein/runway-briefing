Episode Title: airBaltic Files Chapter 11, American Eyes Singapore Again, and a Cargo Plane's Owner Gets the Boot

Episode Summary: Latvia's flag carrier heads to a New York bankruptcy court while insisting flights keep flying — turns out betting big on a single aircraft type has a dark side too. Plus, American Airlines might return to a route it once ran away from, Amazon cuts ties with the cargo airline behind last week's Miami runway overrun, and a shouting match on the tarmac in Atlanta raises real questions about who actually has the final word before takeoff.

Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Monday, September 14th. Let's get into it.

So, airBaltic. The Latvian flag carrier just filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in New York, and yeah, that's now the second European airline this year headed to an American courtroom, because apparently that's just how you restructure debt now if you're a mid-size carrier in trouble.

Here's the thing though — flights are still operating normally. Nobody's stranded, nothing's grounded. This is airBaltic getting ahead of a cash crunch before it becomes a crisis.

And the numbers are rough. They've got about 583 million dollars in debt and lease obligations, plus another 106 million euros in unpaid payroll taxes. Before filing, they actually tried to raise new debt at a 25 percent interest rate. Twenty-five percent. That's not financing, that's a payday loan with wings.

They've now secured 405 million dollars in bankruptcy financing instead, at a comparatively reasonable 12 percent. Small mercies.

What's interesting is airBaltic bet everything on being an all-Airbus A220 operator — one aircraft type, super efficient, great story on paper. But when you've got zero fleet diversity and the bills come due, there's no cushion. This is basically that strategy on trial.

Okay, American Airlines. So get this — there are rumors swirling that American might bring back nonstop service from LA to Singapore. Which is wild, because American already tried the whole LA-to-Asia thing and basically ran away from it.

Beijing, Hong Kong, Shanghai, Sydney, Tokyo — American had all of it out of LAX and slowly gave up on nearly every route because, in their own words, every Asian carrier feels like it has to serve LA, so the competition ate their margins alive.

Now, with more 787-9s finally showing up, American's commercial chief is talking about rebuilding longhaul flying out of LA. Nothing's filed, nothing's on sale, this is still just rumor and vibes. But if it happens, American would be going head-to-head with Singapore Airlines on their own turf, with a shiny new business suite and, notably, no local partner on the other end to connect anybody past Singapore. Ambitious. Possibly reckless. We'll see.

Next — Amazon has suspended operations with 21 Air, the cargo carrier behind that runway overrun in Miami last week. Flight 7598 ran off the end of the runway, and Amazon apparently didn't wait around for a full investigation to decide it was time to part ways.

This is Amazon protecting its own brand more than anything — they contract with a bunch of small cargo operators to move packages overnight, and when one of them has a very public, very bad incident, the fastest move is to just cut the cord and sort out the details later.

And finally, a weird one out of Atlanta. An American Airlines pilot and an air traffic controller basically got into it on frequency this weekend over takeoff separation behind a 757. The pilot wanted more space, the controller said it wasn't required, and things got heated enough that the controller reportedly threatened to report him.

Here's my take — the controller might be technically right that extra spacing wasn't mandated. But the pilot is the one responsible for that airplane, and if he doesn't feel safe going, he doesn't have to go. Threatening a pilot for making a safety call is exactly the wrong instinct, and it's the kind of thing that makes headlines for the wrong reasons.
