Southwest's Rough Week, Airlines Accused of Price-Fixing, and Qatar's Hans Zimmer Boarding Music
Episode Summary: The NTSB drops a scary report on a Southwest 737 MAX that flew 44 times with undetected tail damage — and then, as if on cue, another Southwest jet loses an engine on takeoff. Plus, airlines get hit with new price-fixing lawsuits, the industry's top lobbyist says everyone's happy while his own survey says otherwise, British Airways trains crews on urine-soaked seats, and Qatar Airways gets Hans Zimmer to score your boarding music.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Saturday, November 7th. Let's get into it.

So, Southwest. Not a great week.

The NTSB came out with a report on a 737 MAX that had a Dutch roll event — that's when the tail starts swinging side to side, not fun — and the backstory is way worse than the incident itself. The plane flew through a storm, got tail damage, and should've had a mandatory inspection afterward. It didn't happen.

So the jet just... kept flying. Forty-four flights with undetected damage. Then it actually failed a maintenance test later — the rudder was only moving in one direction, which, yeah, rudders should probably move in both — and that got missed too. This is a paperwork and process failure stacked on top of a maintenance failure, and that's the scarier story than any single bad flight.

And then, like the universe wanted to pile on, a different Southwest 737 — this one nineteen years old — lost an engine right after takeoff from John Wayne Airport on Friday. Squawked 7700, diverted to LAX, landed safe, everyone's fine. But combine that with the NTSB report and you've got a rough 48 hours for Southwest's maintenance reputation, right as they're also trying to launch new premium products and chase a different kind of customer. Not the week you want.

Okay, next one, and this one's almost funny if it weren't so annoying. Two new lawsuits accuse American, Delta, United and other majors of sharing pricing and shopping data to avoid undercutting each other. And get this — fares are up over 22% in the past year.

Basically the claim is airlines built a system to spot when a competitor's about to discount a route, so they just... don't discount either. Nobody has to call anybody. The data does the collusion for you. If this sticks, it's a much bigger deal than a normal fare complaint — this is the same legal theory that's taken down landlords and gig companies recently for algorithmic price coordination. Worth watching.

Speaking of airlines telling you one thing while the numbers say another — the head of the US airline lobby group went out this week and said 98, 99% of passengers are, quote, "very happy." Cool. Except his own organization's survey says 72% are satisfied, which means 28% aren't, and only 31% say they're "very" satisfied. That's not 99%, that's not even close to 99%. I don't know what math he's doing, but I'd like an invite to whatever software he's using.

Alright, lighter stuff. British Airways is training flight attendants on how to handle urine-soaked seats. I wish I was kidding. A TikTok creator sat down on a Miami to Heathrow flight, discovered — the hard way — that the seat was soaked from the previous passenger, and the crew's big solution was a tiny bottle of antibacterial spray and some paper towels. For nine hours. No spare seat, no blanket, nothing. Video's got over 600,000 views, so now there's an actual training module getting built around this. Genuinely wild that it took a viral video to get there.

And finally, because aviation never stops being a little absurd — Qatar Airways just released thirty minutes of original boarding music composed by Hans Zimmer. Yes, the Lion King and Dune guy. It's called the Burgundy Symphony, five tracks, premiered at a concert in Doha. Track titles include "Dawn," "Dusk," and my personal favorite, "An Ode to Qatar." Look, if you're gonna sit on the jet bridge for twenty minutes, I guess you could do worse than an Oscar winner scoring your boarding experience. Though I have to wonder what the budget was on this compared to, you know, fixing a seat.
