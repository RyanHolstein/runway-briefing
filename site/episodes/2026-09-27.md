Episode Summary: Boeing lands the biggest order of the year and still can't catch a break on the 737 MAX. American Airlines is quietly boxed into a corner on its next widebody jet, Ryanair shareholders stage a revolt that doesn't actually change anything, and Heathrow's third runway slips yet again — this time to 2039. Plus, Boston airlines are flying with extra gas in the tank because of a nor'easter.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Sunday, September 27th. Let's get into it.

So, Boeing. Good week, bad week, somehow both at once. Korean Air just signed off on a hundred and three jets — triple sevens, 787s, MAX 10s — thirty-six billion dollars, the biggest order of the year, with the US ambassador and South Korea's trade minister literally standing there for the photo op. This is trade-deal aviation, not just an airline buying planes it needs.

Then two days later, Boeing's own CEO stood up at an investor conference and said the 737 MAX production ramp is running "a little bit longer" than planned. Translation: still stuck below the target of 47 jets a month, and the bottleneck is wing production — which, sure, that's an important part of the airplane. Stock dropped six percent that day, worst single day since May.

And it gets worse. The Air Current is reporting the FAA is now digging into a software glitch on the MAX 10 that could make pilots' workload spike during a missed approach. This bug's been sitting around for almost two years, and now it's surfacing right as Boeing's trying to finally certify the last two MAX variants. So you've got record orders rolling in the front door while the actual airplanes are stuck in a very expensive holding pattern out back.

Okay, American Airlines. Quietly this is a huge story. Every other legacy carrier — Delta, United — has already committed to a next-gen widebody bigger than the 787-9. American hasn't. And Simple Flying's reporting Boeing just made a move to close that gap, which, if you're keeping score, means American's fleet strategy for the next twenty years is about to get decided in the next few months. This matters because long-haul jets are a decade-plus commitment. Whichever plane American picks locks in its international route map basically through 2045.

Ryanair. Michael O'Leary's pay package — up to a hundred fifty million euros in stock options — got approved by shareholders. But get this: almost forty percent of investors voted no. That's a real revolt, even if it didn't actually go anywhere, since a simple majority is all you need. Both major proxy advisers told shareholders to reject it. To be fair to O'Leary, he doesn't get a dime unless Ryanair hits four billion in annual profit or the share price holds above forty-two euros through 2032 — so it's not a free lunch. But when two out of five shareholders of Europe's most profitable airline say "not like this," that's worth noting.

Heathrow's third runway — remember we've talked about this before — got delayed again. New target is 2039. Not because of a court fight, not because of protesters chaining themselves to fences, but because Heathrow's own bosses told the Prime Minister the timeline they'd been using was just "too ambitious." This runway has been "coming soon" since basically the Blair government. At this point I think the pyramids will get a fourth side before Heathrow gets a third runway.

Quick one to close. Boston Logan had airlines tankering extra fuel into flights this weekend — meaning planes flew in heavier than they needed to — because fuel barges couldn't get up the coast ahead of a nor'easter. Airport says it wasn't a crisis, just being careful. But it's a good reminder that a lot of what keeps airports running smoothly is just boats showing up on schedule, and when they don't, airlines start doing math with jet fuel weight instead of, you know, passengers.
