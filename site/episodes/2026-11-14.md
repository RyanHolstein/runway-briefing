Episode title: Ground Stops, Grudges, and a Very Angry Power Bank
Episode Summary: A nationwide ground stop — only the second since 9/11 — shows just how fragile America's air traffic system really is. Plus, American doubles down on LAX, Alaska threads a single 737 across the Pacific to Tokyo, Singapore Airlines can't shake its yellow seat problem, and KLM's former CEO becomes the reason his old airline had to file a police report.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Saturday, November 14th. Let's get into it.

So Friday afternoon, the FAA basically hit pause on the entire country. A nationwide ground stop — every departure, everywhere — because of a communications failure across air traffic control centers. This has only happened once before since 9/11. Let that sink in for a second.

It started around 3:30 at the Kansas City center and just spread. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy came out that night and basically said the quiet part out loud — old switches, old routers, a system that got overloaded with data and couldn't handle it. Homeland Security's Markwayne Mullin confirmed it wasn't a cyberattack, which, fine, good, but also almost worse? Because this wasn't an attack. This was just... our infrastructure being old.

They lifted the stop around 8:15 that night, but here's the thing — lifting a ground stop doesn't put six thousand canceled flights back where they belong. Planes and crews were stranded in the wrong cities overnight, and airlines are saying the disruption bleeds into this weekend and probably Monday too.

And get this timing — literally hours before this happened, the White House was already asking Congress for 30 billion dollars to upgrade aviation infrastructure, trying to get it done before Democrats take the House in January. So, yeah. Point proven, I guess. Nothing like a nationwide meltdown to make your own budget request for you.

Okay, American Airlines. They're making a real play at LAX again. Nine route changes — some new, some restored, two routes getting cut. The bigger story is strategy — American's flights at LAX are down 32% from 2019. They went all in on Sun Belt hubs, Dallas, Charlotte, Miami, and basically let LAX wither while Delta quietly became the biggest airline at the airport. Now American's saying they want to "gradually build" back. LAX is the only airport where all three legacy carriers run a hub, so this is American walking back into a fight they kind of abandoned.

Alaska Airlines is doing something genuinely weird — launching nonstop service from Anchorage to Tokyo Narita. Eight hours and fifteen minutes, which is a long flight already. But here's the twist — they're doing it with a single Boeing 737 MAX 8. A narrowbody. Across the Pacific. That's the kind of route that used to require a widebody, but modern single-aisle jets have the range now, and airlines are realizing they can test thinner long-haul markets without committing a 787. It's a bet that Anchorage-Tokyo demand exists, just not enough to fill a bigger plane.

Singapore Airlines, meanwhile, is still dealing with the fallout from that new business class seat everyone hated — the black and yellow one for the 777-9. The CEO has now publicly said they're open to rethinking the colors, which, for an airline that built its whole brand on obsessing over details, is kind of a big deal. People are out there generating AI mockups of better designs just to rub it in. Honestly the comfort matters way more than the color scheme, but when your entire reputation is "we sweat the small stuff," a yellow stripe becomes a very big deal.

And last one, because I can't not tell you this — KLM has filed an actual police complaint against its own former CEO. Camiel Eurlings ran the airline back in the early 2010s, still had travel privileges, and allegedly fell asleep while charging a power bank in business class. The thing overheated, caught fire, and the crew had to put it out with water mid-flight over the North Atlantic. Plane diverted back to Amsterdam. KLM banned in-seat power bank charging earlier this year for exactly this reason, and now they're going after the guy who used to run the place. You cannot write that.
