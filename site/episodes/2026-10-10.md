Episode title: Flydubai Fallout Spreads, JetBlue's Bonds Get Shaky, and American Kills First Class
Episode Summary: The flydubai cockpit attack keeps sending shockwaves through the industry — United restricts crew by nationality, a new confession reveals years of planning, and an Egyptian pilot's video raises eyebrows at the worst possible time. Plus, JetBlue's loyalty program debt is getting nervous looks from lenders, Delta posts record revenue but slashes its profit outlook, and American officially kills off first class on its biggest plane.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Saturday, October 10th. Let's get into it.

So let's start with something heavy, because the flydubai attack is still rippling outward, and it's touching crews everywhere.

Quick recap if you're just catching up — a first officer attacked his captain with a crash axe, trying to fly the plane into Ben Gurion's terminal. New reporting out now says he was radicalized years ago in Melbourne, Australia, at a mosque tied to a Muslim Brotherhood-linked group, and that he specifically chose aviation as a career so he could pull off a 9/11-style attack. He scouted Tel Aviv twice beforehand. This wasn't impulsive. This was years in the making.

Here's the fallout. United just sent crews a memo saying if you hold dual citizenship with any of 29 countries — Iran, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, Syria, places without diplomatic ties to Israel — you can't work flights into Tel Aviv anymore. Doesn't matter if you're also a US citizen. Israeli authorities are drawing the line at nationality, not where you were born. That's an extraordinary ask for an airline to make of its own workforce, and it tells you exactly how seriously cockpit access is being treated right now.

And then, because the timing could not be worse, an Egyptian pilot posted a video this week of an El Al jet in Milan, soundtracked with Anwar Sadat saying "an eye for an eye, violence for violence." He says it was just patriotic pride for Egypt's October war anniversary, nothing to do with flydubai. Maybe. But posting that, with that song, over an Israeli plane, days after a cockpit axe attack? Even if his intentions were innocent, his judgment clearly wasn't.

Next — remember JetBlue grabbing Spirit's old LaGuardia slots recently? Here's the less fun JetBlue news. The bonds backed by its TrueBlue frequent flyer program, two billion dollars worth, just dropped to 72 cents on the dollar. That's bond-market speak for "investors think there's real risk here." TrueBlue is supposed to be JetBlue's crown jewel, the asset you borrow against cheap because loyalty programs print cash. When lenders start demanding a huge return just to hold debt against your best asset, that's not a great sign. JetBlue still has cash, and the big payments aren't due for a while. But this is the market quietly raising an eyebrow, and loyalty-program debt getting shaky is usually one of the early warning signs.

Then there's Delta, which had a weird quarter. Record revenue, seventeen point six billion dollars, up sixteen percent. But they cut their full-year profit forecast, from six-fifty to seven-fifty a share down to five-ten to five-sixty. Why? Fuel. Up sixty-two percent year over year, costing them an extra six billion dollars this year. The interesting part is premium revenue grew eighteen percent — people are paying up for the fancy seats, so Delta's whole strategy is working. It's just that fuel is outrunning it. Basically the story for the whole industry right now.

Quick one on American — they flew their first trip with the retrofitted 777-300ER, and it has zero first class seats. Seventy business suites, forty-four premium economy, thirty extra-legroom economy, no first class at all. On long-haul routes a really good business seat already feels like first class to most people, and airlines can sell way more of those than old-school suites. Fits right into that first-class fee fight we've been tracking — the writing's on the wall for traditional first class.

And last thing — Qatar Airways flew its final Airbus A330 passenger flight, closing out that chapter after keeping the jets around longer than planned. They're also lending some A330-trained pilots to RwandAir on secondment, basically keeping their skills sharp while regional disruptions have limited flying. Small story, but another legacy widebody riding into the sunset.
