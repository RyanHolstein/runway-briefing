Episode Title: 25 Years Since 9/11, Emirates Finally Gets Berlin, and JetBlue Hits Pause on Growth
Episode Summary: It's the 25th anniversary of 9/11, and we look at how the day still shapes everything from cockpit doors to TSA water bottle rules — plus a wild story about a Korean Air 747 almost getting shot down hours after the attacks. Also today: Emirates wins its Berlin fight while Algeria slams its airspace shut on UAE carriers, and JetBlue quietly pushes 44 new jets off into the future.

Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Friday, September 11th. Let's get into it.

So, obviously, today's the day. Twenty-five years since 9/11, and I want to spend a minute on it because this industry doesn't really exist the same way without that morning. Cranky Flier does this every year — prints the names of the flight crews who were murdered that day — and it's worth just sitting with. Betty Ong, the American flight attendant who made the first call from Flight 11, is being honored today too. She was calm, precise, giving details to the ground the entire time. That's the job.

Here's a wild footnote you probably haven't heard. Hours after the attacks, a Korean Air 747 inbound to JFK got mistaken for a fifth hijacked plane. The captain used "HJK" in a message meaning hijacking elsewhere, ground handlers panicked, F-15s got scrambled out of Alaska, and then air traffic control told the crew to squawk 7500 — the actual hijack code — to test them. They just... did it. Because why wouldn't you follow instructions from ATC? Nearly caused a shootdown over a miscommunication.

And the changes from that day are still moving. TSA's reportedly about to let PreCheck flyers bring bigger water bottles through at airports with the newer CT scanners. Small thing, but it's part of a bigger unwind — shoes are already coming off the "must remove" list at some checkpoints, non-travelers might get back to the gate. Twenty-five years later, we're still recalibrating what 9/11 actually required versus what just became habit.

Okay, moving to actual news. Emirates finally got the green light to fly Dubai to Berlin, after years of Lufthansa lobbying hard against it. This came wrapped inside a UAE state visit where Abu Dhabi pledged forty billion dollars into the German economy — AI, energy, infrastructure. Funny how that works. Emirates was so confident this was coming, they'd already bought landing slots at Berlin Brandenburg months ago.

But here's the whiplash — same week, Algeria is banning Emirates and Etihad from its airspace entirely, over accusations the UAE's been backing secessionist groups in Libya, Yemen, and Sudan. That's not just losing a destination, that's rerouting flights around an entire country, adding real time and fuel to routes through North Africa. Big win in Germany, real headache in North Africa, same airline, same week.

JetBlue's got a story that says a lot about where the industry actually is right now. Back in 2024 they deferred 44 brand-new A321neos, pushing delivery past 2029. Their newest neo came in 2025, and now we're looking at zero new ones in 2027, 2028, or 2029. They've even sold off two undelivered XLRs before those planes were built. This is a carrier that was supposed to be growing hard this decade, and instead they're basically telling Airbus "not yet." When an airline defers planes it already ordered, that's not caution, that's a company admitting the growth math doesn't work right now.

Quick one on British Airways — they're running a record 27 routes between Heathrow and the US this year, and get this, Gatwick's US flying is shrinking down to just 2% of BA's transatlantic schedule, the smallest slice ever outside COVID. BA is basically consolidating all its transatlantic muscle at one airport. Makes sense — Heathrow slots are gold, and there's no point splitting your best product across two terminals.

And last thing — Delta had a 757 turn back to JFK yesterday after a pressurization warning, landed safely, nobody hurt. Also worth flagging: DFW got hit with a ground stop during Thursday's storms and ended up with more delays than any other airport on earth that day.
