Episode title: United Shuts Out the Robots, Frontier Floods a Cabin, and Everyone Wants First Class
Episode Summary: United bans AI agents from booking your flights, and the real reason is more interesting than the official one. Plus, a Frontier bathroom leak turns into a mayday, Alaska passengers sleep on Red Cross cots, easyJet gets swallowed by private equity, and every airline in America suddenly cares about first class again.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Thursday, November 12th. Let's get into it.

So, United just picked a fight with robots. The airline is banning third-party AI agents from booking flights or checking you in — full stop. They've added it to their contract of carriage, so if a bot books you a seat, United can literally cancel it.

Officially, this is about safety. United says letting an AI check you in could violate FAA rules, because someone actually has to confirm your bag doesn't have a bomb in it. Fair point, honestly.

But listen to what CEO Scott Kirby actually said — he doesn't want, quote, "another middleman." That's the real story. Airlines spent twenty years clawing distribution power away from travel agents and booking sites. Now AI agents are shaping up to be the next middleman, and United wants to kill that before it even gets going. Delta's already doing the same thing. So are JPMorgan and Amazon. This is turning into the next big fight in tech — not robots taking jobs, robots taking the booking screen — and companies are not here for it.

Now, one of the wilder stories of the week. A Frontier flight from Denver to Detroit had a bathroom sink spring a leak mid-flight — and I mean really spring a leak. So much water came out it started flooding the cabin, and the pilots declared a mayday.

Not because of wet carpet. There's an avionics bay right under that part of the plane, and water plus sensitive electronics is a genuinely bad combination. The pilot told air traffic control, quote, "we're worried about electronics" — not a sentence you want to hear at thirty-three thousand feet over Iowa. They diverted to Chicago O'Hare and landed fine. But this is like the third weird mechanical scare we've covered on Frontier in recent weeks. At some point that stops being bad luck and starts being a pattern.

Speaking of diversions gone wrong — Alaska Airlines passengers got stuck in Yakima, Washington, and the airline wouldn't put them up in hotels. So the Red Cross showed up with cots.

Yakima's tiny, hotel rooms are probably scarce, I get that. But Alaska has a customer commitment that promises hotel help for disruptions within its control, and their defense here is basically "not our fault." Maybe! Try explaining that to someone sleeping on a cot in a terminal. This is exactly the kind of story that makes those passenger-rights conversations we keep having feel a lot less theoretical.

Big one out of Europe — easyJet shareholders just voted, overwhelmingly, to sell the airline to Apollo Global Management, a US private equity firm. We're talking ninety-seven percent of shares in favor. The deal values easyJet at about seven and a quarter billion dollars.

Apollo says it'll keep running easyJet as a low-cost carrier, just with bigger planes and a beefed-up loyalty and holidays business. Which is private-equity speak for "we're going to squeeze more revenue out of the same seats." Apollo's already deep in aviation — they financed Virgin Atlantic's Heathrow slots, they're tangled up in Air France-KLM's loyalty program too. This is part of a bigger trend: private equity quietly buying up pieces of the airline industry, one slot portfolio and one loyalty program at a time.

Last thing — everybody suddenly wants first class. JetBlue just showed off its first-ever first-class cabin, recliners with a mattress-topper cushion, though legroom's actually kind of tight at 36 inches. Frontier — yes, Frontier — isn't just adding bigger seats up front, they're rolling out real first-class service standards starting next March. American's bringing back actual food, smoked salmon and burrata, on first-class flights between 700 and 899 miles. And Qantas just delivered an A321XLR with three fewer economy seats to make room for a fourth bathroom.

Put it together and it tells you where the industry's head is. Coach is getting squeezed down to the studs, and everybody's fighting over people willing to pay for the front of the plane. Even the budget guys are chasing it now, which tells you exactly how much money is sitting up there.
