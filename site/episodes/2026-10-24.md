Episode Title: JetBlue's Creditors Call a Lawyer, Lufthansa Buys TAP, and a 737 Fleet Gets Grounded for Inspections
Episode Summary: JetBlue's bondholders just hired bankruptcy advisers while the stock sits at an all-time low — we break down what that actually means. Plus, Lufthansa beats Air France-KLM for a chunk of TAP Air Portugal, nearly 2,000 Boeing 737s need emergency tail inspections, Riyadh Air literally can't fly out of Riyadh anymore, and a flight attendant who was once told she wasn't "American material" retires after 63 years with the receipts to prove them wrong.

Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Saturday, October 24th. Let's get into it.

So, JetBlue. We've mentioned the premium turnaround a few times, and the story keeps getting worse, not better. This week, a group of JetBlue's biggest creditors hired restructuring advisers — we're talking PJT Partners and a law firm that specializes in, well, bankruptcies. That's not a subtle move.

Here's the number that got my attention: JetBlue's frequent flyer debt, the stuff backed by the TrueBlue loyalty program, is trading at 42 cents on the dollar. That's bond market speak for "we don't think you're paying this back in full." The stock, meanwhile, just hit an all-time low.

Now, hiring advisers doesn't mean bankruptcy is imminent tomorrow. Companies do this defensively all the time, just to have options on the table. But creditors organizing like this is usually a sign they've stopped believing the turnaround story and started planning for what happens after. And here's the part that should worry JetBlue shareholders specifically — United has been circling. If this does end in a restructuring, United could cherry-pick the good parts, like slots and gates, and today's JetBlue stock could end up worth close to nothing. Not great for a brand that used to be the plucky, beloved underdog of U.S. aviation.

Big one in Europe. Lufthansa just won the bidding war for TAP Air Portugal, beating out Air France-KLM for a 44.9% stake. They were literally the only two bidders, so this was always going to go one way or the other.

Why does this matter? TAP controls a ton of valuable slots at Lisbon, which is basically the gateway between Europe and Brazil. Lufthansa already runs four hubs — Frankfurt, Munich, Zurich, Vienna — and now they're adding a fifth flavor to the mix with Lisbon's South America connections. This is consolidation, plain and simple. Europe's airline map keeps shrinking down to basically two or three giant groups, and if you're a smaller flag carrier anywhere on the continent, you should probably be nervous right now.

Safety story. The FAA just ordered inspections on nearly 2,000 Boeing 737 Next Generation jets after multiple incidents where the nose was literally pitching up and down mid-flight. The issue is in the hinges and jackscrew that move the horizontal stabilizer — basically the part of the tail that controls pitch.

That's not a small thing to have acting up. The good news is this is the older NG generation, not the MAX, so Boeing's latest headache isn't the cause this time. But nearly 2,000 airplanes needing tail inspections is still a massive logistical headache for every airline running them, and it's one more reminder that Boeing's quality control questions didn't start, and won't end, with the MAX.

Weird one out of Saudi Arabia. Riyadh Air — the brand-new, multi-billion-dollar airline literally named after the capital — is no longer flying out of the capital. After missile attacks damaged the terminal at King Khalid Airport earlier this month, the airline evacuated its brand-new 787 Dreamliners and is now flying out of Al Qassim, a city 400 kilometers away.

Passengers get a complimentary three-and-a-half-hour shuttle through the desert to catch their flight. I don't know how you spin that in a press release, but that's rough for an airline that's trying to launch itself as a premium global player. It's also a pretty stark reminder that the Houthi threat to Saudi aviation isn't theoretical anymore — real airlines are rerouting real 787s because of it.

Okay, last one, and it's a good palate cleanser. Carol Fair is retiring from American Airlines on Monday after sixty-three and a half years as a flight attendant. She started at Piedmont back in 1963 — that's Kennedy-era, Boeing 707 and DC-8 era — and worked her way through mergers into US Airways and then American.

Here's the kicker: decades ago, someone at American told her she wasn't "American material." She's now retiring as the airline's most senior active flight attendant. Her last flight fittingly comes into Charlotte from London, echoing a Piedmont route she worked back in 1987. That's a pretty great way to end an argument.
