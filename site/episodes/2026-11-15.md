Episode title: Wingtips, White Paint, and a Very Confident Scott Kirby
Episode Summary: A UPS freighter and a Copa jet trade paint on a Miami taxiway, and somehow nobody got hurt. Plus, the fallout from Friday's ground stop keeps getting weirder, Emirates and Qantas take completely opposite bets on the A380, and United's CEO says a merger with American is a lock — which is a wild thing to say out loud.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Sunday, November 15th. Let's get into it.

So first up — Miami, Saturday night. A UPS 767 freighter is taxiing out for departure, and a Copa Airlines 737 clips it with its wingtip. Right into the nose of the 767.

Nobody was hurt, which, genuinely, is the only reason this is a fun story and not a terrifying one. The FAA says the Copa jet taxied too far — basically didn't give the UPS plane enough room to swing its tail around. One witness account floating around is just "somebody hit us," which, yeah, that about covers it.

Here's why this matters beyond the fender-bender vibes. Taxiway and ramp collisions are actually way more common than runway incursions, they just don't make headlines because nobody dies. But every one of these is a reminder that the most dangerous part of flying usually isn't the flying — it's the fifteen minutes on the ground when two jumbo jets are trying to share pavement built for propeller planes in the 1950s. MIA's going to have a long week of paperwork, and both planes are out of service getting wingtip and nose damage checked out.

Okay, update on the ground stop mess from Friday — because it is not getting better, it's getting weirder. Delta told customers this weekend to just... not fly if they can help it. Twenty-one percent of Saturday's departures canceled, hundreds more delayed, and Delta straight up said rebook for Monday.

And then there's the explanation for why any of this happened in the first place. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said old routers overloaded with data. Homeland Security's Markwayne Mullin said, actually, it happened while they were installing new equipment to patch the old system. Those are two different stories, and neither one is "here's exactly what broke and here's how we're fixing it."

Meanwhile, a leaked ATC schedule shows controllers pulling ten-hour shifts, mandatory overtime, fourteen days straight with no day off. So when people ask why the system cracked, I don't think it's a mystery. You don't run air traffic control like a sweatshop for years and then act shocked when it breaks.

Now, something genuinely fun — Emirates and Qantas just took the exact opposite bet on the same airplane. Emirates is stripping its A380s down to bare metal and spending billions rebuilding the interiors from scratch. Qantas is retiring its A380s four years early.

Same plane, totally different math. Qantas flies the A380 mostly to LA and London, routes where a 787 or A350 can do the job on two engines instead of four, burning way less fuel. Emirates flies it into slot-constrained airports like Heathrow and JFK, where you physically cannot get more seats in the sky without a bigger plane. When the airport won't give you another landing slot, the only move left is to make the plane you've already got bigger inside. So Emirates keeps betting superjumbo, Qantas is done with it, and honestly both of them are right, for their own networks.

Quick one on strategy — United's Scott Kirby says he's "100% confident" a merger with American would get approved, which is a hell of a thing to say with full confidence, given that these deals have a long history of getting blocked or taking years, if they go through at all. Remember, the DOJ killed the JetBlue-Spirit deal and fought the Northeast Alliance. Kirby betting the house on a yes shows you how much the competitive landscape might be shifting under this administration.

And it's funny, because at the same time, American's chief commercial officer is out there talking about becoming a "premium global airline" and doubling down on local customers in cities like New York. So you've got American trying to win on its own, and United's CEO publicly musing about just buying them. Those two stories don't exactly point the same direction.

Last thing, quick — the 737 MAX 10 is finally certified. WestJet gets the first one in early 2027, Alaska and United right behind, Delta later that year, American not until 2029. Four airlines, 550 jets on order combined. This plane's been stuck in certification limbo for years, so airlines finally get the bigger cabin, more first class seats, better economics per seat — they've just been waiting a long, long time for it.
