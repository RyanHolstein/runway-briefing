Episode title: Engine Fire Over Phoenix, Delta's Wi-Fi Flip-Flop, and the FAA Meltdown Just Got Worse
Episode Summary: An American Airlines jet declares Mayday after another crew spots flames shooting from its engine over Phoenix. Delta's CEO does a stunning about-face on how much Wi-Fi actually matters, new reporting makes the FAA's ground stop look even messier, and one Southwest passenger learns the hard way what happens when your plus-one doesn't show up.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Friday, November 20th. Let's get into it.

So, American Airlines. An A319 takes off out of Phoenix Thursday, and shortly after departure, the crew declares Mayday. Not because they noticed anything themselves — because a SkyWest flight behind them radioed in and said, hey, your engine's on fire.

Think about that for a second. The pilots flying the plane didn't know. It took another crew, watching from behind, to spot flames coming out of the engine and warn ATC. That's actually how this is supposed to work, by the way — pilots look out for each other in the air, it's happened before. But it's still a little unsettling that the first warning came from a totally different airline's cockpit.

The jet turned around, landed safely back at Sky Harbor, nobody hurt. But I'll be watching for what the NTSB or FAA says caused it, because "flames visible from another aircraft" is not a phrase you want in an incident report, even when the ending is fine.

Okay, Delta. This one's a little rich. Remember when Ed Bastian stood up in 2023 and said, and I'm paraphrasing barely at all, Wi-Fi is essential to daily life, your Delta flight should feel just like being at home? That was the whole pitch for free Wi-Fi.

Well, now that Delta's stuck waiting on Amazon's satellite internet instead of just doing a deal with Starlink like everybody else, Bastian's suddenly saying customers don't actually choose airlines based on Wi-Fi. Which — come on. You spent three years training us to care about this. You don't get to un-ring that bell just because SpaceX wouldn't give you the business terms you wanted. Honestly this is what happens when you bet against Starlink — you end up defending Viasat with a straight face.

Quick update on the FAA mess we've been following all week. New CNN reporting digs into what actually happened before that nationwide ground stop, and it's not flattering. Turns out a technician in Arkansas let a contractor into a radio facility, and his job — by his own understanding — ended at unlocking the door. No monitoring after that.

The FAA administrator claimed the installation was supervised. The agency's own spokeswoman then said, quote, the escort is not there while the technician is doing work. Those two things cannot both be true. And there's a 2025 policy change that turned contractor maintenance from "needs approval" into "just notify us," handled by a tiny team buried under thousands of notifications. So when people ask how one router install in Arkansas grounded flights nationwide — this is how.

Lighter one. A Southwest passenger in Boise got marched off her flight home from Vegas because her husband, who she was flying on his Companion Pass, didn't show up. She even offered to just pay for her own ticket on the spot. Didn't matter — rules are rules, and apparently Southwest enforces them even when there's an empty seat sitting right there.

Here's the thing about the Companion Pass — it's one of the best deals in American aviation, basically a free seat for a year. But it's tied to that specific person flying with you, not a general "plus one" pass. Southwest's been cracking down on this exact loophole, so if your companion flakes, you're not flying as them. Rough way to learn that lesson at 9pm at the gate.

Last one, and it's just funny. American Airlines passengers in Atlanta watched their checked bags come off the belt covered in brown sludge. Naturally, everybody feared the absolute worst. American's official explanation: peanut butter. One bag leaked and got everywhere.

Which, sure, maybe! But one passenger pointed out you'd need an unreasonable amount of peanut butter to coat that many suitcases. I don't have an answer for that. I just think "defend yourself with an inspection report about peanut butter" is a sentence no airline wants to say out loud, and American had to say it anyway.
