Episode Title: Norse Atlantic's Planes Get Repossessed, Qantas Picks a Side, and Americans Barely Fly

Episode Summary: Norse Atlantic's collapse gets uglier as lessors try to literally take the planes back mid-crisis. Qantas is about to hand Boeing a massive long-haul win over Airbus, and a new survey suggests America's "air travel obsession" might be a myth. Plus, Scott Kirby makes a bold Thanksgiving prediction.

Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Friday, November 27th. Let's get into it.

So, update on something we've been following all week. Norse Atlantic. Remember, the low-cost long-haul airline that grounded its entire fleet Wednesday night because it couldn't even make payroll? Well, it just got worse.

The lessors came for the planes. AerCap and BOC Aviation, who own all twelve of Norse's 787s, sent termination notices Friday morning saying, basically, give us our jets back. Norse is contesting it, saying the terminations aren't valid, but two aircraft are already scheduled to fly to a storage facility in Spain today. That's not a storage facility for planes that are coming back, by the way. That's where planes go to get parted out.

Here's the thing about leasing versus owning in this industry — when you don't own your metal, you've got no leverage in a crisis. Norse never owned these Dreamliners, it leased them, and the second the music stopped, the owners moved fast. And on top of that, Norway's aviation regulator is fining Norse eight hundred bucks a day for not paying passenger compensation it already owes. This airline owes money in basically every direction at once. If a short-term rescue doesn't materialize in the next day or two, I think we're looking at bankruptcy, not a "maybe."

And honestly, the flight attendants' union put it well — fuel hedges are running out across the industry, and Norse is just the first domino. Worth watching who's next.

Okay, switching gears — Qantas. Big fleet news. Sources say Qantas is about to place a widebody order for around 20 new jets, and Boeing is set to win the majority of it with the 787 Dreamliner, with Airbus's A350 picking up the rest.

This matters because Qantas has actually been pretty split loyal between the two manufacturers for years. A clean Boeing win here is a real signal — especially right after Malaysia Airlines just ordered up to 16 of the stretched 787-10s this week too. That's two major Asia-Pacific carriers picking Boeing widebodies in the same week. Boeing's had a rough few years, so landing back-to-back wins like this, especially against Airbus's hugely popular A350, is a genuinely big deal for them.

Next — there was a YouGov survey out today that honestly kind of blew my mind. Only one percent of Americans fly multiple times a month. Only thirty percent fly even once a year. We spend so much time talking about ground stops and meltdowns like the whole country is living at the airport, and it turns out the vast majority of Americans barely fly at all.

And when you ask why, it's not mysterious. Sixty percent said high fares are the problem, forty-four percent blamed hidden fees. People also don't trust the system — only fifteen percent think air traffic control is actually getting better despite Sean Duffy's twelve and a half billion dollar rebuild plan, and he's already asking for fifty billion more. I think this is the real story under the story — flying feels like a given in this industry's own bubble, but for most Americans it's genuinely a once-a-year, white-knuckle, "I hope nothing goes wrong" kind of event.

Last one, quick hit. Scott Kirby sent a Thanksgiving message to United employees, and tucked inside the usual thank-you-to-staff stuff was this line: "the lead that we have is going to grow versus all of the other airlines."

Not just American, not just Delta — all of them. That's Kirby being Kirby, pretty confident guy running an airline that's genuinely been performing well. But it's worth remembering United still has huge variation in its product — Starlink on some planes, ancient seatback screens on others. Confidence is good. Consistency still needs some work.
