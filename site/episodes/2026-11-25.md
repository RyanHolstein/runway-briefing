Episode Title: Norse Atlantic's Critical Condition, the Army's Return to DC Airspace, and Boeing Beats Airbus in Malaysia
Episode Summary: A low-cost long-haul airline that never learned from Norwegian's collapse is now begging for a lifeline, the Army wants back in DC airspace less than two years after the deadliest US air disaster in decades, and Malaysia Airlines just handed Boeing a win over Airbus. Plus, two airlines are quietly trimming their route maps and the ICE jet bridge fallout keeps growing.

Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Wednesday, November 25th. Let's get into it.

So, Norse Atlantic. If you don't know the name, here's the quick version. Oslo-based, low-cost, long-haul, flying 787s across the Atlantic for cheap. Great idea. Terrible business model, historically.

Norse told the Oslo Stock Exchange this morning its finances are, quote, "critical," and its survival depends on finding financing fast. They're literally entertaining takeover talks right now, today, with no guarantee anything comes of it.

Here's the thing that kills me. Norse was built from the wreckage of Norwegian, which tried this exact playbook and collapsed spectacularly right before COVID. Norse's founder swore up and down they'd learned the lessons. They haven't turned a profit in a single year since launching in 2022. Over half a billion dollars in losses.

They tried wet-leasing planes to IndiGo to make ends meet, but IndiGo pulled back on long-haul flying and ripped that revenue away. The founder got pushed out a year ago. And now here we are. Long-haul, low-cost has basically a one-hundred-percent failure rate at this point — Laker, Wow Air, Norwegian, and now maybe Norse joins the list. The math just never works when fuel and crew costs eat a discount fare alive over an eight-hour flight.

Okay, next one. This one's heavier. The US Army wants to restart helicopter training flights near Reagan National as soon as December 6th. That is less than two years after a Black Hawk and a regional jet collided over the Potomac and killed sixty-seven people.

Sara Nelson, who runs the flight attendants' union, is furious, and honestly, I get it. The NTSB called that training corridor an "intolerable risk" after finding over fifteen thousand close calls between helicopters and planes near DCA in just a few years. The Army says conditions have changed. Nelson's basically saying, slow down, we buried sixty-seven people because of this exact flight path. This is going to be a fight, and I don't think it's over.

Speaking of that same union — quick update on something we talked about Saturday. Remember the masked ICE agents on the Alaska Airlines jet bridge in Seattle? The flight attendants' union just escalated. They're now warning that if crews can't tell a real federal agent from an impersonator, that's an actual security hole — someone could use a fake badge to get onto a jet bridge. American's flight attendants union piled on too, saying their own airline hasn't given them any real guidance on what to do when ICE shows up. This one isn't going away.

Alright, let's do some fleet news. Malaysia Airlines just signed for up to sixteen Boeing 787-10s, and they're using them to replace their Airbus A350s. That's a real loss for Airbus, who thought they had that relationship locked up.

And over at British Airways, not a great week for the 787 program in general — a Boston-bound 787-10 had to declare an emergency over the Atlantic Tuesday night with an oil leak. Second oil leak emergency in ten days for BA's Dreamliner fleet. Nothing catastrophic happened, planes landed fine, but that's the kind of pattern that gets regulators asking questions.

Last thing, quickly — two airlines quietly shrinking their maps this week. Air Canada is cutting eight international routes, and Allegiant is dropping twenty-six nonstops. Different reasons — Air Canada's trimming underperforming long-haul, Allegiant's leaning harder into its merger with Sun Country and just being pickier about leisure markets. But it's the same story under the hood: airlines are done chasing growth for growth's sake. They want routes that actually make money, not just routes that look good on a map.
