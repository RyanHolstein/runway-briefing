Episode title: Boeing Tears Open 30 777Xs, American Flirts With The A330neo Right As Its Engine Gets Flagged, and a Drone Shuts Down San Diego
Episode Summary: Boeing's 777X problem gets weirder — finished jets now have to be partially disassembled before they can ever fly a passenger. American Airlines is circling the exact same plane whose engine just got an FAA safety order. Plus, a drone and a weather balloon shut down San Diego for hours, and we cover a genuinely tragic ramp accident in Montreal.

Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Sunday, September 6th. Let's get into it.

So Boeing's 777X saga just got a new chapter, and it's a rough one. Turns out some of the finished jets sitting in storage — built, painted, ready to go — now need to be partially torn apart before they can ever carry a passenger.

Here's why. The certification flight test program dragged on so long that Boeing kept building airframes the whole time, and the design kept changing underneath them. So now there's a "change incorporation" process — older jets need structural rework, newer ones just need software and systems updates.

Think about that for a second. They built the plane before they finished designing the plane. That's not a typo, that's the actual timeline. And it matters because customers like Emirates, Cathay, and Lufthansa have been waiting years for this jet already — the 777X was originally supposed to fly commercially back in 2020. Every one of these teardowns costs time and money on a program that's already burned through both.

Okay, so American Airlines. Update on something we talked about earlier this week — American's shopping for up to 65 widebodies to replace its aging 777-200ERs, and the picture's getting clearer. It's reportedly leaning toward the Airbus A330neo over the Boeing 787-10, mainly because Boeing's Dreamliner backlog is so packed that American wouldn't see planes until the 2030s anyway.

Which, remember, American retired its entire A330 fleet during the pandemic. Prematurely, a lot of people would say. Now they might be buying the newer version back. That's a pretty expensive way to say "actually, never mind."

But here's the funny timing part. The FAA just ordered an emergency fix for the exact engine that powers the A330neo — the Rolls-Royce Trent 7000 — after reports of in-flight shutdowns tied to a faulty oil pump. Delta flies these on its A330neos, and the rule kicks in October 2nd. So if American does sign on the dotted line, they'd be buying a plane whose engine just made news for shutting itself off mid-flight. Not exactly a great sales pitch, but hey, the fix is apparently pretty targeted, so no need to panic.

Next up, the DOT. We mentioned this rule was coming, and now it's final. Starting October 19th, airlines get to stop covering hotels and meal vouchers for ten specific delay causes they're calling outside their control. Some of these make sense — sabotage, extreme weather damage. But others are wild. "Government system shutdowns." "Overheated brakes during an emergency." "Cybersecurity attacks," as long as the airline says it followed the rules.

Basically, airlines just got handed a pre-approved excuse list. And remember, the U.S. doesn't even have a legal duty-of-care requirement like Europe does — this was all voluntary goodwill to begin with. Now there's less of it.

Quick one — San Diego airport got shut down for hours on Friday after a drone and, somehow, a weather balloon were spotted in the airspace nearby. Four hundred plus flights got delayed, and when they finally cleared departures again, planes were sent out "at their own risk." That phrase should not exist in commercial aviation, and yet here we are. This keeps happening at major airports and there's still no great system for actually stopping it — just grounding everyone and hoping.

Last story, and it's a hard one. A ramp agent at Montreal-Trudeau was struck and killed by an Airbus A350 during pushback back on August 24th, after reportedly stumbling on the tarmac. He passed away from his injuries. Ramp operations are one of the most dangerous jobs in this entire industry, and it almost never gets the attention it deserves until something like this happens. Our thoughts go out to his coworkers and family.
