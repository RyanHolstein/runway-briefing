Episode title: FAA's Fiber Line Fiasco, America Threatens to Ground Iran's Airlines, and Air New Zealand Pilots Throw Hands in San Francisco
Episode Summary: A cut fiber cable takes down the Northeast's airspace and cancels over a thousand flights in one day. Plus, the US threatens to cut off fuel to any airport that services Iranian planes, two Air New Zealand pilots get into it on a layover and strand a planeful of passengers for 14 hours, and American's building a four-and-a-half-billion-dollar terminal in Phoenix.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Tuesday, September 22nd. Let's get into it.

So yesterday was a mess. A primary circuit failed at the FAA's Philadelphia TRACON — that's the facility that handles approaches for Newark, JFK, LaGuardia, Philly, Teterboro, basically the whole Northeast corridor. Normally, no big deal, you flip to the backup line.

Except the backup line had literally just been cut. By construction crews. In New Jersey. A fresh fiber-optic backup, severed before it ever got used.

The result? Around 7,000 flights delayed or cancelled nationwide, more than 600 of those just at Newark, where no plane landed for almost eight hours. And the timing was brutal — 130 world leaders were flying into New York for the UN General Assembly at that exact moment.

FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford called the break "massive," 600 feet of cable gone, 13 hours to fix. Everybody's pointing fingers — Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy blamed a rail crew, Amtrak's involved somehow too. But here's the real story: this is what happens when critical infrastructure runs on one aging circuit and one backup, and nobody double-checks the backup actually works. Delays are still lingering into today. Buckle up.

Okay, next. This one's wild. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent is threatening to cut off dollar access to any foreign airport that refuels Iranian airlines. As soon as tomorrow. We're seven months into the US and Israel's campaign against Iran, and now Washington's going after it from a completely different angle — basically telling airports worldwide, service Iran's planes, lose access to the US financial system. That's an enormous stick. It could effectively ground Iranian aviation entirely if airports comply, and airports usually comply when the alternative is getting cut off from dollars.

Now, something that's just... a lot. Air New Zealand had two pilots reportedly get into an actual fight during a layover in San Francisco. Off duty, not on the plane, thank god. The airline's calling it "pilot illness," which — sure, if punching your coworker counts as a symptom.

Here's the kicker though. Because of mandatory crew rest rules, they couldn't just swap in another pilot immediately. They had to fly a replacement out, let them rest, then finally operate the 13-hour flight to Auckland. Total delay: 14 hours. One disagreement on a layover, and hundreds of passengers eat an entire extra day of their trip.

Alright, something more upbeat. Cranky Flier had a great piece on Phoenix Sky Harbor, which just named American as the primary carrier for its new west terminal — a four-and-a-half-billion-dollar project. American and Southwest already carry nearly three-quarters of the airport's passengers, and this move frees up space in the old terminal for other carriers too, plus more international gates. Sky Harbor's gone from one sleepy terminal in 1952 to basically an arms race. It won't open till 2033, so don't hold your breath, but it tells you Phoenix is betting big on staying a real hub, not just a Southwest and American town.

Quick hits before we go. An American passenger on a Phoenix to Montreal flight reportedly asked Air Canada flight attendants for asylum mid-flight. Pilots said she was calm, they just... continued to Montreal. No further details, which honestly makes it funnier and weirder.

And Paris airport had a translator mix up Switzerland and China — because both start with "C" apparently — and sent Chinese passport holders into the wrong immigration line for who knows how long. Paris Aéroport's response on social media was actually pretty good: "our signs decided to take a trip around the world a little faster than expected."

Last one — Simple Flying's got a piece on the A380 being called "Airbus's 30 billion dollar failure" that's somehow become one of the most beloved planes flying today. Which tracks. Nobody wanted to buy them, airlines are retiring them early because twin-engine jets are cheaper to fly, but passengers genuinely love flying on them. Sometimes the plane that makes zero financial sense is still the one everyone wants a window seat on.
