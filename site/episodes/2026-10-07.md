Episode title: JetBlue Grabs Spirit's Slots, Emirates Ditches the A380, and First Class Gets Cheaper (And Worse)
Episode Summary: The FAA hands JetBlue the keys to LaGuardia after Spirit's collapse, and we get into why that actually makes sense. Plus, Emirates quietly pulls its flagship A380 from nine cities, Delta and United start nickel-and-diming first class passengers while American sits back and smiles, and a Delta A350 has a very bad morning at LAX.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Wednesday, October 7th. Let's get into it.

So, JetBlue just won LaGuardia. Remember Spirit went under back in May, and they were sitting on 18 slot pairs at LaGuardia, which is one of only three slot-controlled airports in the whole country. That's basically real estate gold.

JetBlue had a deal to buy 22 of those slots, but the FAA had to sign off, and somebody actually lobbied for Frontier to get them instead, arguing JetBlue's fares aren't "ultra-low-cost" enough. The FAA basically said, nah, JetBlue's got a proven low-fare model and giving them more LaGuardia presence helps them actually compete with the big legacy carriers there. Which, honestly, makes sense — JetBlue's biggest problem in New York has always been that it's stuck with a tiny foothold while Delta and American dominate. This is JetBlue finally getting room to breathe in its own backyard. They can't resell the slots until 2028, but they get a grace period on the use-it-or-lose-it rule while they ramp up. Good week for JetBlue. Bad week to be Spirit, still, even in the afterlife.

Speaking of JetBlue, weird one — for a few hours yesterday, every single JetBlue flight for October 13th and 14th just vanished from the booking system. Passengers with confirmed reservations said their trips disappeared, dates got changed without asking. Airline support reportedly called it a "phantom" glitch. Which is not a word you want your airline using about your actual flight.

Okay, Emirates. They've quietly pulled the A380 from nine routes. Emirates is THE A380 airline — they fly more of these things than anyone on earth — so this stings a bit. But here's the thing: those jets cost north of 400 million dollars each, and they only make sense when you can fill all 500-plus seats consistently. On thinner routes, a 777 or a 787 burns way less fuel and still gets the job done. This is the same story we've seen across the industry — the superjumbo era is winding down route by route, even for its biggest champion.

Now, first class. Delta's selling domestic first class tickets now with a 300 dollar change fee and no free seat selection. United's rolling out something similar — fewer included bags, no free changes. Basically, they're selling you the front cabin and then charging you to actually enjoy it without hassle. Which, I don't know, feels like the whole point of first class was NOT having hassle. American's reportedly just... not doing that. If they hold the line, that's a real opening — "we still treat first class like first class" might actually become a selling point instead of the default.

Alaska's having a moment. They just quietly started an interline deal with Breeze Airways — small for now, just a few cities like Charleston and Raleigh — but it's interesting because Alaska still doesn't have a real East Coast presence after buying Hawaiian. Breeze could be the backdoor into that. And separately, Alaska's CCO said they're working on a new Asia route out of Seattle, didn't say where, just teased it. Seattle's one of the best-positioned hubs in North America for transpacific flying, so after a big push into Europe this year — London, Rome, Reykjavik — looks like Asia's next.

Couple quick ones. A Delta A350 clipped an Air Canada 777's tail while taxiing at LAX yesterday — no injuries, but both planes are grounded for inspection, and Delta's Tokyo flight got delayed. And an update on that flydubai hijacking story we mentioned earlier this week — new reporting says the suspect had actually planned to crash the 737 into an Israeli airport, and that he'd been planning it for over a year. Darker than it already sounded.
