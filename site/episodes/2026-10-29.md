Episode Title: United's Basic First Class Nickel-and-Dimes You, Heathrow's Terminal Shuffle Gets Messier, and Two Planes Kiss at LAX

Episode Summary: United rolls out "basic" first class, because apparently the front of the plane needed a bait-and-switch fare too. Plus, SkyTeam publicly rips Heathrow over its terminal reshuffle, a Delta A350 wedges its wingtip into an Air Canada 777 at LAX, and Delta explains why it's the one legacy carrier skipping the hottest plane in the industry right now.

Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Thursday, October 29th. Let's get into it.

So, United. We talked a couple days ago about United's "basic business class" and how CCO Andrew Nocella was basically bragging that customers hate it so much they pay extra to escape it. Well, good news, that strategy's now in first class too.

United just rolled out Base, Standard, and Flexible fares for domestic first class. The cheapest one still gets you a first class seat, technically. But you pay extra to pick your seat, you get one checked bag instead of two, you can't change your ticket without a fight, and you earn zero MileagePlus miles. Zero. You paid seven hundred dollars to sit up front and United's reward is pretending you don't exist.

Here's the math that really gets me: on a Chicago to LA route, the new base fare is about twenty bucks cheaper than the old first class fare used to be. Twenty dollars! For giving up your bags, your seat choice, your flexibility, and your miles. That's not a discount, that's a fee disguised as a discount. This is the exact same playbook as basic economy, just wearing a nicer jacket, and it's working because people see "first class" and stop reading. Smart for United's margins. Rough for anyone who actually flies up front.

Okay, Heathrow. Remember yesterday we talked about Heathrow splitting up Delta and Virgin Atlantic in that big terminal reshuffle? Well, SkyTeam just weighed in, and they are not happy. The alliance put out a statement saying their fourteen member airlines are now scattered across three different terminals at Heathrow, calling it a competitive disadvantage, and pointing out that Delta and Virgin have run integrated operations together for over a decade. Meanwhile, China Southern is moving in right next to British Airways, which, sure, is a much smaller operation, but you can see why Virgin's CEO is using words like "favoritism." Heathrow says it's just about terminal capacity. Maybe! But when you're the airport and you keep making moves that happen to cluster your hometown airline's partners together, people are going to notice the pattern.

Now, something actually went bump at LAX. A Delta A350 clipped an Air Canada 777 while taxiing out for departure earlier this month, and the video that's circulating shows the Delta jet's wingtip literally wedged into the 777's tail. Nobody was hurt, which is the important part, but that's not a fender bender, that's two widebodies that are each worth well over a couple hundred million dollars doing a very expensive high five. Ground collisions like this usually come down to taxiway congestion or a tug operator miscommunication, and LAX has been a pinch point for years with all that construction going on. Both planes are now out of service for inspection and repair, which means two less widebodies flying this week.

Speaking of widebodies, here's a fun contrast. American and United have both brought in the Airbus A321XLR to replace their aging 757s on long, thin transatlantic routes. Delta looked at the exact same plane and said, no thanks. And honestly, I get it. The A321XLR is a single-aisle jet doing a widebody's job, which means smaller cabins, less first class real estate, and a product that's a step down from what Delta currently flies on those routes. Delta's whole brand right now is "we're the premium one," so cramming customers into a narrowbody for a nine-hour flight just doesn't fit the story they're selling. It'll be slower growth on thin routes for Delta, but it protects the thing they've spent a decade building.

Last one, and this is a good one to file under "airlines doing something sketchy." American priced a bunch of multi-city award tickets way too cheap, basically charging customers only for the first leg of the trip. People booked them, completely legitimately. Three weeks later, American canceled the tickets, calling it a mistake fare. Except American's own rules say they have to catch and cancel mistake fares within seventy-two hours. Three weeks is not seventy-two hours. DOT lets airlines walk back genuine pricing errors, but there's supposed to be a clock on it, and American's basically saying the clock doesn't apply to them. That's the kind of thing that erodes trust fast, and it's worth watching if it turns into an actual DOT complaint.
