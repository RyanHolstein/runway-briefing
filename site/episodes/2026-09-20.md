Episode Title: Boeing's Engineers Could Ground the 777X, Delta's Very Bad Day With a Casket, and United's Polaris Lounges Can't Keep Up
Episode Summary: Boeing's engineers might walk out October 6th, and that could freeze the already-years-late 777X right when it needs to get certified. Plus, a heartbreaking story about Delta mishandling a family's loss, United's new 787s are so popular in business class that the lounges can't handle the crowd, and Finnair is about to fly one of the longest scheduled routes in the world.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Sunday, September 20th. Let's get into it.

So, Boeing. Again. I know, I know.

Here's the deal. About 17,000 engineers — the folks at SPEEA, the union that actually designs and certifies this stuff — are working under a contract that expires October 6th. They already voted down one offer, and 88% of them authorized a strike. Boeing sweetened the deal on September 11th with a 10% raise, and we'll see if that's enough.

Why should you care? Because the 777X — Boeing's next big widebody, years late, billions over budget — is sitting right at the finish line waiting on certification. CEO Kelly Ortberg basically admitted this week that a strike would freeze certification completely until the engineers come back. Not slow it down. Stop it.

And here's the thing — this isn't Boeing's first rodeo with a labor stoppage. Remember two years ago, 33,000 machinists walked out for seven weeks and shut down 737, 767, and 777 production? That cost Boeing billions and took months to recover from. Now imagine that happening to the one plane Boeing has already sold to airlines but literally cannot deliver yet. If these engineers walk, the 777X doesn't just get delayed again. It basically goes back into a freezer with no defrost setting.

Okay, next one, and this one's rough. A family in Tennessee says Delta left their father's body unrefrigerated overnight after a missed connection through Atlanta.

The body ended up arriving in Memphis too late for cargo pickup — Delta's own rules say human remains can't be held overnight and can't arrive after 7pm — and by the time the family could see him, he was too decomposed for a final viewing.

I don't really have a joke here. This is just Delta's hub-and-spoke system failing at the one job you absolutely cannot get wrong. Airlines move human remains all the time, it's a whole logistics operation behind the scenes, and when it breaks down, there's no refund or voucher that fixes what a family loses. Delta hasn't said much publicly yet, but I'd expect this one to get louder.

Alright, something a little more first-world-problems. United has a Polaris business class crunch on its hands.

United's getting 66 new 787-9s, and the business class cabins on these are noticeably bigger than what they've been flying. Which sounds great until you realize more Polaris passengers now means more people trying to cram into the same lounges. United's already looking at expanding at LAX and O'Hare because the math just doesn't work anymore.

This is honestly a good problem to have — it means United's premium bet is paying off, people are buying up business class, and the international network is growing. But it's also a reminder that airlines love announcing shiny new planes way more than they love building the lounges to go with them.

Last one, and this is just a fun route. Finnair is launching a new flight October 25th — Helsinki to Melbourne, with a stop in Bangkok, same plane, same flight number the whole way. It's an A350, and because of fifth-freedom rights, they can actually sell tickets just for the Bangkok-to-Melbourne leg too.

This is old-school long-haul flying, the kind of routing you don't see much anymore now that planes can fly halfway around the world nonstop. It's basically a 24-hour scheduled journey door to door. Kind of wild that in 2026, the most efficient way from Northern Europe to Australia still involves a layover in Thailand — but hey, at least you get two meal services.
