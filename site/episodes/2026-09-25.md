Episode Title: Air France Gets Bumped From Denver, Spirit Sells Its Last Plane, and a Senator Discovers Furries
Episode Summary: United's new Denver-Paris route just pushed Air France out of the market entirely — a perfect case study in what happens when a hub carrier flexes. Plus, Spirit Airlines sells off its very last aircraft for $668 million, Airbus finds a corrosion defect in hundreds of A321neos, Dubai and Iran trade airspace bans, and a U.S. senator delivers the most bizarre aviation safety warning we've heard all year.

Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Friday, September 25th. Let's get into it.

So, Air France just got run out of Denver. Quietly, no press release, nothing dramatic. JonNYC spotted that Air France zeroed out inventory on its Denver to Paris route starting next spring, which in airline-speak means "we're done, we just haven't said it out loud yet."

Here's why this is such a clean story. Air France launched Denver-Paris back in 2021, three times a week, and honestly it never really found its footing — it went seasonal, then year-round, then back to seasonal again. That's not a route that's thriving, that's a route on life support.

And then United announced daily, year-round Denver to Paris starting next May on a 787. Denver is United's fortress hub. They control the connections, the loyalty base, the gates, everything. Air France was basically trying to sell a one-stop-shop item in a store where the landlord also sells it, cheaper, with more parking. This is basically the transatlantic version of what we've seen for years — when a legacy hub carrier decides it wants a route, foreign carriers without a local base usually just can't compete long-term. Lufthansa, British Airways, they've all had to make similar retreats over the years. Denver was never going to be Air France's fight to win.

Next, some closure on a story we've been following for months. Spirit Airlines just sold off its very last 27 Airbus jets for six hundred sixty-eight million dollars. This is it — the actual, literal end of Spirit as an operating airline. The lenders are taking most of the planes, Fortress Transportation grabs four more, and the cash goes straight to Spirit's creditors as the liquidation wraps up.

It's kind of wild to think that five years ago Spirit was fighting off a takeover bid from JetBlue. Now it's down to an asset sale spreadsheet. The ultra-low-cost model that Spirit basically pioneered in the U.S. just could not survive rising costs and heavier competition from the majors matching their basic economy fares. Consider this the final chapter.

Now, an Airbus story that matters more than the headline suggests. The Air Current is reporting that Airbus found a quality defect that could reduce corrosion resistance inside the fuselage of the A321neo — and it affects more than five hundred aircraft, some already flying, some still on the factory line.

To be clear, Airbus says this isn't a safety issue right now. But when you're talking about corrosion resistance in the fuselage structure, that's the kind of thing that turns into expensive inspections and rework down the road, on top of a delivery schedule that's already stretched thin. Airbus has been racing to hit production targets for years. This is exactly the kind of quiet manufacturing headache that quietly eats into that timeline.

Quick geopolitical one. Dubai just banned all Iranian civilian aircraft from its airspace and airports, not because they wanted to, but because the U.S. slapped secondary sanctions on anyone doing business with Iranian carriers. Refuse to comply, risk getting cut off from the dollar system. Dubai obviously said yes to that request. Iran retaliated immediately by banning UAE-registered planes — that's Emirates, Etihad, flydubai — from its airspace. Which means longer, more expensive routings for some of the busiest carriers in the world. This is one to watch, because airspace access in that region has already been a mess since the spring.

Couple quick hits before we go. EasyJet announced eighteen new UK routes in a single day, heavy focus on Spain, because of course it is. Delta's adding Boston-Venice and Detroit-Athens for summer 2027, doubling down on that southern Europe demand every airline's chasing right now. And East Coast flyers, heads up — airlines are waiving change fees this weekend ahead of an early Nor'easter, so if you're flying through the Northeast, check your app.

And finally — Senator Tommy Tuberville stood up on the Senate floor this week and warned Americans that their pilot might secretly be a furry. I wish I was making that up. He did not offer any actual data, any incident, anything. Just vibes and a warning that "Lord help us." Genuinely one of the stranger moments in aviation policy discourse this year, and that's saying something.
