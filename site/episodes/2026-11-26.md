Episode title: Norse Atlantic Grounds for Good, FedEx Overshoots at Miami (Again), and Trump's Embraer Tariff Threat
Episode Summary: The long-haul budget airline experiment finally runs out of road as Norse Atlantic suspends all flights and stares down bankruptcy. A FedEx freighter overshoots the same Miami runway area that saw a fatal crash just months ago, and Washington's new tariff plan could make your next regional flight a lot more expensive. Plus, United finally starts retiring a 30-year-old workhorse on one of its flagship routes.

Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Thursday, November 26th. Let's get into it.

So remember two nights ago when we talked about Norse Atlantic being in "critical condition"? Well, critical turned into flatlined. The airline suspended every single flight Wednesday night, and Thursday morning they told the Oslo Stock Exchange that if they can't find emergency cash, they're filing for bankruptcy. Full stop.

Here's how bad it got. Their stock dropped 85% in a single day. Employees are reportedly being told there's no money for payroll. And the way we found out wasn't even a formal announcement — it was two flights they were running for Pakistan International Airlines just... not happening. PIA had to scramble its own 777s to cover London and Manchester routes because their wet-lease partner basically vanished.

Honestly, I've been watching this one for a while, and it's not surprising. Norse launched in 2022 trying to do what Norwegian tried and failed at years earlier — cheap, no-frills long-haul on 787s. The math never worked. Fuel's expensive, long-haul low-cost has a brutal cost structure, and they'd already pivoted to leasing out half their fleet just to survive. That IndiGo deal fell apart after eighteen months. The PIA deal didn't even last a month. At some point you run out of plans B, C, and D.

Next up, Miami had another bad night. A FedEx MD-11 freighter coming in from Memphis overshot the runway Wednesday during a storm, crosswinds and all, and ended up in the grass right next to the perimeter fence. Both pilots walked away fine. But get this — it came to rest just meters from the wreckage of the Amazon cargo plane that crashed in that exact same spot back in September. That one was fatal.

Two overruns, same airport, same general area, less than three months apart. That is not a coincidence anyone wants to explain away. Investigators are already opening a probe, and I'd bet Miami's runway safety areas and approach procedures for 26R get a hard look very soon.

Okay, switching gears — tariffs. The Trump administration wants to slap a 10% tariff on Brazilian aircraft, which means Embraer jets. And this matters because Embraer basically IS the US regional jet fleet at this point — there are about 800 E-Jets flying for American, Delta, and United's regional partners right now, with 220 more on order. A 10% tariff on those planes adds up to hundreds of millions of dollars, and that cost doesn't just disappear — it gets passed somewhere, probably to the regional carriers already running on thin margins.

This is part of a bigger trade fight — Brazil already got hit with a 25% tariff back in July on other goods, but aircraft were exempted. Now Washington's trying to use planes as leverage to get Brazil to the negotiating table. Public comment period runs through December 6th, so nothing's final, but if you fly a CRJ or E-175 regularly, keep an eye on this.

Last one, a smaller story but a good one. United is finally retiring its Boeing 767-300ERs on the Chicago to London Heathrow route. These planes are pushing 30 years old on average — that is genuinely ancient for a widebody still flying transatlantic passengers. They're getting replaced by the 787-9, the same "Elevated" configuration United's rolling out from San Francisco, and Houston's getting added to that Heathrow network in January too.

This is United quietly building out a real transatlantic fortress hub by hub, and honestly it's about time those 767s retired. Thirty years is a long career for any airplane, let alone one still doing eight-hour ocean crossings every day.
