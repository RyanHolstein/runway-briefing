Episode title: Norse Atlantic's Planes Get Snatched Mid-Air, Two American Jets Swap Paint at Reagan, and Delta's Ghost Airline Tails
Episode Summary: Norse Atlantic's collapse gets a wild final chapter as lessors repossess planes literally mid-flight. Two American Airlines 737s collide on a DCA taxiway and force a go-around, Italy almost guts its busiest airport's schedule over EU border chaos, and we explain why some Delta jets are still wearing a dead airline's name. Plus, an update on the Qantas-Boeing widebody drama we've been watching all week.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Saturday, November 28th. Let's get into it.

So, Norse Atlantic. We've been watching this one fall apart all week, and Friday gave us the most dramatic ending possible. The Norwegian arm filed for bankruptcy, and literally as that news was breaking, two of their 787s were mid-flight, heading to a storage facility in Spain.

Here's the part that sounds like a movie. Both planes suddenly diverted to Shannon, Ireland, which happens to be a base for AerCap, the world's biggest aircraft lessor. The lessors had already told Norse hours earlier they were terminating the leases on the entire fleet. So basically, the planes took off as Norse Atlantic property and landed as somebody else's property, mid-air, while passengers presumably had no idea.

Why this matters beyond the drama: this is now the second long-haul budget carrier to collapse in just a few years, after Norwegian's own long-haul arm folded back in 2018. The model keeps promising cheap business class and cheaper economy across the Atlantic, and it keeps running out of cash before it works. Fuel costs, thin margins, and lessors who will absolutely repossess your plane mid-flight if you miss a payment — turns out that's not a great combination.

Okay, next up, and this one's just chaos. Two American Airlines 737s collided on a taxiway at Reagan National on Friday evening, clipping wingtips while lining up for takeoff. One plane actually lost its winglet. The pilot's radio call is the best part — he tells the tower, quote, "we swapped paint with the American next to us, not sure whether we are attached to them."

Which, I mean, incredible way to deliver bad news. The collision also sent debris onto the runway, forcing an arriving regional jet to go around at the last second. Nobody was hurt, both flights got rebooked a few hours later. But DCA has had a rough stretch — remember, this is the same airport that saw the deadly midair collision less than two years ago — so any incident there, even a minor taxiway fender-bender, is going to get extra scrutiny from the FAA.

Over in Italy, Milan Malpensa almost had its schedule gutted. Italian regulators drafted a plan to cut at least one flight an hour next summer, because they're worried the EU's biometric border system, called EES, is going to create total gridlock at immigration. And honestly, that fear is not crazy — this system has been a mess since it rolled out last year, with four-hour lines at some airports during peak summer.

But airlines found out about the plan basically through a newspaper leak, and the industry lobby went nuclear, pointing out carriers had already locked in their Summer 2027 schedules. Within hours, Italy's aviation authority backed off and said nothing's actually changed yet. So, crisis paused, not solved. This is the tension to watch across Europe next year — biometric border security versus airports that physically cannot process it fast enough.

Quick update on something we've been tracking — Qantas and that widebody order. Bloomberg's sources now say Qantas is leaning toward giving Boeing the bulk of its upcoming order, mostly 787s, to help replace the A380 fleet. Officially it's a 10-and-10 split up for grabs between Boeing and Airbus, but if this holds, it's a real gut punch for Airbus, who's dominated Qantas's recent orders with A350s and A321XLRs. Nothing's signed yet, so there's still room for a last-minute swing, but the smoke is pointing one direction.

Last one, just a fun fact to send you into the weekend. Delta is flying around 38 Airbus A320s today whose tail numbers still trace back to Northwest Airlines — an airline that stopped existing in 2010. The planes have been fully modernized, new screens, new WiFi, new seats, but they're 23 to 35 years old and still legally carrying a dead airline's registration history. Kind of a nice reminder that when airlines merge, the ghosts stick around longer than you'd think.
