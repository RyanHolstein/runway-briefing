Amazon's Deadly Miami Crash: Cargo Aviation's Safety Blind Spot

RYAN: Partly that, yeah. No survivors doing tearful interviews on the news, no angry passengers tweeting about their canceled connection. But it's also structural. RYAN: Exactly. And regulators have historically treated cargo differently too. This is the part that I think people would be shocked to learn. Cargo pilots are exempt from the flight and duty time rules that passenger pilots operate under.

RYAN: Literally the same aircraft type in some cases. Same 767, same cockpit, same physics. But back in 2011 the FAA overhauled pilot fatigue rules after the Colgan Air crash in Buffalo, this is Part 117 if you want the regulation number, and cargo operations got carved out of it.

HOPE: Why would they do that? That seems insane on its face.

RYAN: The official reasoning was cost. FedEx and UPS lobbied hard, argued that applying the same rest requirements to cargo operations would cost the industry something like five hundred million dollars a year, and the FAA sided with them, decided the cost outweighed the safety benefit for an industry that doesn't carry passengers.

HOPE: But it carries people on the ground, apparently.

RYAN: Right, and that's the sick irony sitting right in front of us this week. This crash didn't just kill the crew. It went through a fence into a parking lot. There were people down there who had nothing to do with this flight and never made a choice to accept any risk.

HOPE: So the entire cost benefit calculation that justified exempting cargo pilots from fatigue rules didn't even account for that.

RYAN: It really didn't, because the framing was always passengers versus no passengers. Nobody built the models around what happens when an overrun turns a runway safety area into ground zero for people who live or work near the airport.

HOPE: Miami International is not exactly out in the middle of nowhere either.

RYAN: No, it's surrounded by dense commercial and residential development. This is actually a pattern you see at UPS's Birmingham crash in 2013, similar story, cargo 767 wait no it was an A300, comes in short of the runway at five in the morning, both pilots killed, and fatigue was a major factor the NTSB flagged. HOPE: So this has happened before and the fix never actually got fixed.

RYAN: That's the frustrating pattern. Every few years there's a cargo accident, fatigue or training gets flagged as a contributing factor, there's a round of hearings and op-eds, and then the exemption survives because the lobbying money and the cost arguments win out again.
