Episode Title: 777 Partners' Founder Pleads Guilty, Southwest Blows Up Rapid Rewards, and a Flight to Nowhere
Episode Summary: The man behind the collapse of two budget airlines admits to a $500 million fraud scheme — and it's wilder than you'd think. Plus, Southwest overhauls its entire loyalty program and makes the Companion Pass harder to get, a Singapore Airlines flight spends nine hours going absolutely nowhere, and Lufthansa finally reveals where its brand-new A350-1000 is headed first.

Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Friday, October 16th. Let's get into it.

So, remember Bonza, the Australian airline that collapsed in 2024? And Flair, the Canadian ultra-low-cost carrier that's been limping along for years? Turns out there's a reason both of those airlines were disasters waiting to happen, and his name is Joshua Wander.

Wander co-founded 777 Partners, the private equity firm behind both airlines, and this week he pled guilty to wire and securities fraud. Prosecutors say he defrauded investors and lenders out of more than 500 million dollars — using the same collateral to secure multiple loans, basically pledging assets he didn't actually own. And when lenders asked for proof? Staff allegedly doctored bank statements. With Microsoft Paint. Not even good Photoshop. Paint.

Here's why this matters beyond the fraud itself — this guy was placing massive 737 MAX orders for Flair while, according to prosecutors, running the whole operation like a personal piggy bank. Bonza folded in 2024 owing money everywhere. Flair's still flying, barely. And it's a reminder that a lot of these flashy low-cost airline launches over the last decade weren't backed by real money — they were backed by financial engineering. Wander's looking at up to 20 years. I'd call that a rough landing.

Okay, next up — Southwest. Big day for loyalty nerds, bad day for anyone chasing a Companion Pass on points alone.

Southwest's blowing up Rapid Rewards. A-List and A-List Preferred are gone, replaced by Silver, Gold, and a brand new top tier, Platinum. They're also finally adding partner awards, so you can redeem Rapid Rewards points internationally for the first time ever. That's actually a huge deal — Southwest's been a domestic-only loyalty island forever.

But here's the catch. Starting in 2027, the Companion Pass — one of the most beloved perks in all of travel — becomes something you can only get through Platinum status. Which means those juicy credit card welcome bonuses that used to get you most of the way there? They don't count anymore. You actually have to fly. This is part of a bigger pattern we've talked about — Southwest ditching open seating, adding bag fees, and now building actual lounges that look like a Chase Sapphire lounge had a baby with a Westin. The airline we grew up with is turning into a completely different product, and honestly, the direction is pretty clear: premium, premium, premium.

Moving on — Singapore Airlines had, and I mean this literally, a flight to nowhere. Flight SQ31 left San Francisco Wednesday night headed for Singapore. Three hours in, a passenger reportedly started screaming, kicking seats, and tried to get to an emergency exit. Crew couldn't get him under control, so the pilots turned the A350 around.

Total flight time: almost nine hours. Destination: San Francisco. The exact airport they left from. Passengers got dinner, a movie, maybe a nap, and then landed right back where they started, FBI and police waiting on the jet bridge. And they still had the actual flight to Singapore ahead of them after that. Rough day to be literally anyone on that plane except maybe the guy who got a free eight-hour tour of the Pacific.

Quick one — Lufthansa finally named the first route for its new A350-1000, the bigger sibling to the -900 they already fly. The jet enters service in December, and it's a sign Lufthansa's betting heavily on long-haul widebodies as it modernizes a fleet that's honestly gotten pretty old in spots. Worth watching where else this plane ends up next year.

And last thing — there's a really good piece out from The Air Current about something we touched on earlier this week with the Saudi evacuations. Basically, hundreds of daily flights between Europe and Asia squeeze through this narrow 85-mile sliver of airspace near Azerbaijan, because conflict zones are closing off the alternatives on either side. With Houthi strikes still hitting Riyadh's airport, that corridor just got even more critical, and even more fragile. This is the kind of story that doesn't make headlines until something goes wrong — but it's exactly why airlines are quietly rerouting and re-planning fuel stops right now.
