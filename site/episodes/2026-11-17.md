Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Tuesday, November 17th. Let's get into it.

So remember Friday's nationwide ground stop — the longest since 9/11? Turns out it was even worse than we thought. Simple Flying's got reports of United passengers stuck on the O'Hare tarmac for up to eight hours. Eight hours. No gate, no explanation that makes you feel better, just you, a bag of pretzels, and your own thoughts.

And then — I'm not kidding — United had ANOTHER outage on Monday. A completely separate tech meltdown, ten internal systems down, pilots couldn't get takeoff numbers, flights stuck at the gate. They fixed it in about an hour and said it wasn't a cyberattack, just bad timing stacked on worse timing.

Here's the thing that ties it together. NATS — that's the UK's air traffic control provider — had their chief exec give a speech this week about system resilience, and it's basically describing exactly what happened to us. His line was that "returning a complex system to normal is not like flicking a switch." Highly efficient systems are also highly sensitive ones. That's America's air traffic system in one sentence — so optimized, so maxed out, that one router update turns into eighteen thousand cancellations over three days. AirlineGeeks is now estimating that ground stop alone could cost airlines hundreds of millions of dollars. And Trump's blaming Biden, the union's blaming the contractor who apparently did maintenance mid-day without proper oversight. Nobody's blaming the actual 50-year-old system, which, I mean — maybe start there?

Okay, switching gears. Update on a story we haven't talked about in a bit — the flydubai terror plot. Quick recap: an Omani first officer tried to crash a packed flydubai jet into Ben Gurion Airport back in September. Failed, thank god, but Israel banned flydubai from flying to Tel Aviv afterward. Now Israeli transportation officials have actually flown to Dubai to meet with Emirati officials about restarting flights. Worth remembering there are currently zero direct flights between Israel and Dubai — Emirates hasn't flown Tel Aviv since October 7th, and Israeli carriers won't fly there over security disputes. So this meeting is actually a pretty big deal. It's the first real sign anyone's trying to thread that needle back open.

Next — Delta's satellite Wi-Fi bet just got a lot riskier. Delta passed on Starlink and picked Amazon's Leo network for their next-gen inflight internet. On Sunday, one of the rockets carrying 40 Amazon satellites blew up ten minutes after launch. Total loss, all 40 satellites. This is United Launch Alliance's first total mission failure in twenty years, which is not nothing. Amazon's still got almost 400 satellites up already and more ready to launch, so Leo isn't dead. But Delta's install timeline wasn't even starting until 2028, and every delay like this pushes that further out — meanwhile Starlink's already flying on other airlines today. Betting against the market leader is a choice, and right now it's not looking great.

Last one, and it's actually good news for once. Philippine Airlines just finalized an order for fifteen Boeing 787-10s, with rights for five more — that's up to twenty widebodies, call it $3.4 billion. This is the largest widebody order in PAL's history, and their first direct Boeing deal since 2007. For context, they already committed to Airbus A350s back in July, so Manila's building out a serious long-haul fleet from scratch on two different manufacturers. That's a flag carrier betting big that it can actually compete on routes to the US and Australia instead of just connecting everyone through a jammed-up Manila hub — which, by the way, Simple Flying's review this week says is still kind of a mess. Fixing the planes is the easy part. Fixing the airport is the real test.
