Episode Title: Riyadh Airport Takes Another Hit, Israel Locks Out 31 Nationalities, and United's Windscreen Scare Over the Atlantic
Episode Summary: Another missile hits Riyadh's airport, this time while it was already shut down, and we get into why closing the airport hasn't stopped the attacks. Plus, Israel bans aircrew from 31 countries after the Flydubai attack, a United 777 makes an emergency diversion to Dublin after its windscreen cracks at cruising altitude, and Alaska Airlines shows you exactly how airlines are coping with brutal fuel prices right now.

Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Sunday, October 11th. Let's get into it.

So Riyadh. We've been following this all week, and it just keeps getting worse. Saturday's missile attack on King Khalid Airport killed twelve people, hurt over three hundred, and it hit Terminal 3 while it was packed with passengers. That's already brutal.

But here's the part that should scare everyone in the industry. GACA shut the whole airport down after that attack. Grounded everything. And a few hours later, on Sunday, another missile hit Terminal 4. Thankfully empty this time, no flights were running, but think about what that means. Closing the airport didn't make it safe. The Houthis are still finding their targets even with zero air traffic.

This is the fourth or fifth hit on this airport in about a week. Airlines pulled out after the first one destroyed a parked Saudia A320 and killed a captain. The ones still flying in are mostly Saudi carriers with nowhere else to go. At some point you have to ask what reopening even looks like here, because a functioning runway doesn't mean the threat is gone. It just means the missiles haven't landed yet.

Connected to that story, Israel just made a pretty dramatic move. Remember the Flydubai cockpit attack we talked about, the first officer from Oman who tried to crash a plane into Tel Aviv's terminal? Israel's response just got a lot bigger. They're now telling foreign airlines that any pilot or flight attendant holding dual nationality with one of 31 countries can't work flights into Israel. We're talking Pakistan, Indonesia, Lebanon, Iran, even Malaysia.

The thing is, Israel technically already had this rule. It just wasn't enforced. That Omani first officer flew into Tel Aviv twice before the attempted hijacking, and his info should've been flagged in advance. So this isn't really a new policy, it's Israel finally checking IDs at the door after leaving it unlocked for years. United's already sent crew memos about it. Expect more airlines to quietly reshuffle who they schedule on Tel Aviv routes.

Switching gears, a scary one from Friday night that's still making news. A United 777 flying Newark to Milan had its cockpit windscreen crack at 37,000 feet over the Atlantic. Pilots made a steep descent and diverted to Dublin. Nobody was hurt, and look, 777 windscreens are layered, so one cracked pane isn't a depressurization event. But when your first officer's window splinters at cruising altitude on a 29-year-old jet, you're landing that plane immediately, no debate.

Last one, and it's actually kind of a good story for once. Alaska Airlines just posted ugly first-half numbers, fuel alone added six hundred million dollars in costs this year, enough to wipe out their entire profit. But instead of just complaining about oil prices like everyone else, they used their investor day to double down on premium cabins, specifically these new lie-flat Aurora suites coming to their 737 MAX-10s.

And honestly, the math backs them up. Delta's premium revenue is already growing twice as fast as coach. American's retrofitting planes with business class. United's cutting economy seats to make room for better ones. When fuel prices are this unpredictable, squeezing more money out of fewer, pricier seats is starting to look like the only strategy that actually works. Alaska's betting the whole industry is headed there, and right now, nobody's proving them wrong.
