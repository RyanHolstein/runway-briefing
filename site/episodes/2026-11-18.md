Episode title: FAA's Router Mess Gets the FBI Involved, Lufthansa Pilots Dig In, and a 787 Takes a Mud Bath
Episode Summary: The FAA's meltdown gets weirder — now the FBI is involved and Sean Duffy wants $50 billion. Lufthansa pilots give management a one-week ultimatum before more strikes hit. Plus, a British Airways Dreamliner ends up nose-down in the grass at Heathrow, Ryanair builds its own engine shop in Spain, and James Blunt actually goes through with the Ryanair thing.

Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Wednesday, November 18th. Let's get into it.

So, remember that ATC meltdown we talked about last week — the one that stranded people for eight hours and sent three women to buy a Ford Bronco instead of waiting for a flight? Turns out the plot thickens. The FBI is now involved.

Not because anyone thinks it was a hack — Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy was very clear about that. But the router that caused the whole mess apparently turned on a function that was supposed to be disabled, and nobody at the FAA can explain how. That's... not reassuring. When your own safety officials say "we thought we turned that off" about the system that controls national airspace, that's a sentence that should scare you a little.

And here's the real headline: Duffy is now asking Congress for fifty billion dollars to rebuild ATC infrastructure. He started this year asking for thirty. The number keeps climbing every time something breaks. Meanwhile, as View from the Wing pointed out, a chunk of that fifty billion isn't even for air traffic control — it's airport infrastructure riding along on the crisis. Which, fine, towers and runways need money too. But using a system failure to fund a wishlist is a very Washington move. Either way, I don't think this is the last router story we tell you this year.

Okay, Lufthansa. This has been simmering since last September, and it is not getting better. The pilots' union and management just wrapped eighteen rounds of mediation — eighteen — and still couldn't close the gap on pensions and job security.

Here's the kicker: the mediator actually put forward a deal the union was willing to accept. Lufthansa said no and countered with its own plan instead. The union's response was basically "we don't get it, why would you reject your own mediator's compromise?" Fair question. They've given Lufthansa until November 23rd to come back to the table, or it's strike season again — the third round since this all started. For a flag carrier that's already dealing with a messy short-haul business class rollout, a pilot walkout right before the holidays would be rough timing.

Speaking of Lufthansa having a day — a Lufthansa flight attendant went into anaphylactic shock from sesame over Bosnia on Sunday, the EpiPen failed, and they had to divert to Belgrade and get saved by a doctor who happened to be a passenger. Wild story, glad he's okay, but good grief, Lufthansa, pick a different week.

Now, the one that's just... chef's kiss embarrassing. A British Airways 787 Dreamliner, 256 people on board, pushes back at Heathrow, starts taxiing toward the runway, takes a left turn — and ends up nose-first in the grass. Stuck in the mud. The pilot's announcement was, and I quote, "the nosewheel has ended up in the grass." Passengers couldn't even get stairs to the plane until it stopped sinking. They never made it to Lagos. They got a bus back to the terminal instead. A multi-million-dollar widebody, beaten by a grass verge.

On the "actually smart business move" side of today — Ryanair is building its own engine maintenance shop, in Seville, half a billion euros, opening in 2029. This is Michael O'Leary doing what Michael O'Leary does: cutting out the middleman. Right now Ryanair pays outside shops to maintain its CFM engines. Bring that in-house, and you control your own costs and turnaround times instead of waiting in line behind every other airline's engines. With Ryanair planning to grow to 800 aircraft by 2034, this is them building the plumbing to support that growth now, rather than scrambling later.

And last thing, because you need this today. James Blunt promised fans that if his new album hit number one, he'd legally change his name after Ryanair. It hit number one. He actually filed the paperwork. His new legal name, if approved: Ryan Bel Air. Say it fast. Ryanair's social team, naturally, couldn't resist, telling him "at least people would know you then." Honestly the best marketing Ryanair didn't even have to pay for.
