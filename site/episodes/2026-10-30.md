Episode title — Solo Travelers Get Taxed, Airlines Fight the CDC, and Boeing's Backlog Stretches Into the 2030s
Episode Summary: American and United are quietly charging solo flyers more than groups on the same flights — we dig into who's doing it and who isn't. Plus, airlines are furious about a new CDC Ebola rule landing next week, the Airbus and Boeing order backlog is now so deep that a plane ordered today won't show up until the mid-2030s, and South African Airways can't make payroll even after selling off its Heathrow slots.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Friday, October 30th. Let's get into it.

So, Thrifty Traveler did some digging, and here's the headline: American and United are charging solo travelers more than people booking as a pair. Same flight, same day, same seat — if you're flying alone, you might just pay more for it.

And look, this isn't some weird glitch. It's apparently widespread enough that Thrifty Traveler is calling it out by name. Here's the part that actually stings though — Alaska, Delta, and Southwest don't do this. So it's not an industry-wide pricing quirk, it's a choice. Two of the big four decided solo travelers are a softer target, and the other two decided not to bother.

I don't love this trend because it's so quiet. Nobody announces "hey, we're going to charge single travelers a premium." It just shows up buried in the fare logic, and most people never notice because you don't exactly A/B test your own ticket price against a stranger's. If you're flying solo on American or United, it might genuinely be worth pricing out a fake second passenger before you book, just to see what happens.

Okay, next one, and this is a bigger deal than it sounds. The CDC just told airlines that starting November 3rd, every single international passenger flying into the US has to sign an attestation saying they haven't been in the Democratic Republic of Congo in the past 21 days. Because of Ebola concerns. Fine, reasonable goal.

Here's the problem — airlines got two weeks notice. Two weeks to build a new document collection and retention system, train gate agents to enforce it, and figure out how to deny boarding to anyone who won't sign. IATA is calling it "inconsistent with a risk-based approach," which is airline-speak for "this is a mess and you didn't ask us first." Nobody's arguing against public health screening here. They're arguing that dumping an entire compliance system on airlines with two weeks' notice, right before a holiday travel surge, is just operationally unworkable. Watch this one — I'd bet money the enforcement looks very different in week one than what's on paper today.

Alright, here's a stat that stopped me. Airbus and Boeing combined have 12,420 single-aisle jets sitting in their backlog right now. That's not a typo. At current production rates, that's over a decade of work already booked. So if an airline orders a 737 MAX or an A321neo today, they're not getting it this decade. Try the mid-2030s.

This is why you're seeing airlines hang onto old planes way longer than they'd like, why Embraer's E2 — which builds in thirteen to fifteen months — suddenly looks a lot more attractive for regional fleets, and honestly, why used aircraft leasing has become such a lucrative little corner of this industry. When the two biggest manufacturers on earth are sold out until the next decade, everybody downstream has to get creative.

Now, South African Airways. They delayed payroll for a second month in a row. And get this — they already sold off a pair of Heathrow slots, which are genuinely one of the most valuable assets an airline can own, since slots at a congested airport like Heathrow are basically gold. They also reported a "profit" on paper. And they still can't cover payroll.

That combination tells you everything. When you're liquidating your best assets and still posting cash flow problems, the issue isn't one bad quarter, it's structural. This is a Star Alliance member we're talking about, not some regional startup. Worth watching whether Star Alliance partners start quietly distancing themselves the way we've seen happen with other struggling flag carriers.

Couple quick ones before I let you go. WestJet is cutting two more US destinations, on top of the Chicago, Seattle, San Francisco, and Boston cuts we talked about this week. Fuel costs are clearly forcing some real soul-searching in Calgary right now.

And Southwest hired a flight attendant who, it turns out, had an active warrant out for elder fraud. He finished training, flew for a few weeks, then turned himself in. Rough one for the background check department.
