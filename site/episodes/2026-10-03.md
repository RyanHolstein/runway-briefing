Episode title: Flydubai's Vetting Scandal, Frontier's Fake Boarding Pass Guy, and the UK's Half-Baked Fee Ban
Episode Summary: The flydubai cockpit attacker was reportedly banned from flying at a previous airline over extremism concerns — so how did he end up in a 737 cockpit anyway? Plus, a Frontier flight gets deplaned over a guy with a fake boarding pass, and the UK promises to ban "rip-off" airline fees while quietly keeping some of the highest aviation taxes on Earth.

Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Saturday, October 3rd. Let's get into it.

So, update on the flydubai story we've been following all week. New reporting from the Wall Street Journal and ABC News says the first officer who allegedly attacked his captain with a crash axe had already been flagged as a security risk. Before flydubai, he was a trainee co-pilot at Oman Air. In 2024, they found extremist material on him. Oman Air's response? Ground him from flying, but keep him employed in an admin role.

Somehow, from there, he gets hired by flydubai. As a pilot. Flying to Tel Aviv.

That's the part that should bother everyone in this industry. This isn't really a story about a crash axe in a cockpit anymore, as awful as that was. It's a story about vetting. Remember, flydubai pulled every single Israel route the day of the attack, not just that flight. That's not what you do if you think it's one bad apple. That's what you do when you're not sure your background checks mean anything.

Airlines hire internationally, pilots move between carriers across borders, and apparently a red flag raised in one country just... doesn't follow you to the next job. If this reporting holds up, I think we're going to see regulators asking some very uncomfortable questions about how pilot vetting actually works across borders. Because right now it sounds like it doesn't.

Okay, lighter story, but kind of the same theme — how does anyone get past the gate who's not supposed to be there. A Frontier flight out of Atlanta had to fully deplane this week because a guy allegedly boarded with a fake boarding pass and then just... refused to leave. Police had to come get him off.

Everyone else on board had to grab their bags and get off too, which, I get the safety logic, airlines don't want another viral dragging-a-guy-off-the-plane video. But also — how did he get through the gate scan in the first place? Nobody's explained that part yet. Kind of a wild little mystery for a Tuesday flight to San Francisco.

Over in the UK, the government announced it's banning what they're calling "rip-off" airline fees. No more charging parents to sit next to their own kids, no more fees for fixing a typo in your name on a booking. Prime Minister Burnham framed it as putting money back in people's pockets.

Which, sure, fine, those fees were annoying and kind of predatory. But here's the thing nobody at that press conference wanted to mention — the UK still has Air Passenger Duty, which is already one of the highest aviation taxes in the world. We're talking up to 253 pounds tacked onto a long-haul premium ticket, and it's going up another 3% next spring. That tax alone is about 10% of an average ticket price in the UK, compared to like 4% in Belgium. So the government's handing you back maybe a hundred quid on name changes while quietly taking way more at the register. Nice gesture, wrong hand doing the giving.

Quick hit from Cranky Flier's weekly roundup — Alaska Air Group is formally joining the transatlantic and transpacific joint ventures alongside its oneworld partners, bringing Hawaiian along too. That's a big structural move, because it means American, Alaska, and Hawaiian all end up under one big antitrust-immune umbrella for long-haul flying, something none of the other three major alliances currently have with more than one US carrier. If regulators approve it, and that's a real if, this reshuffles who's got leverage on transatlantic pricing and scheduling for years.

And finally, Southwest announced its biggest summer schedule ever for 2027 — eleven new routes, big expansion out of Austin, Nashville, and San Diego, including new international spots like Costa Rica and the Bahamas. Nothing scandalous here, just Southwest continuing to push beyond its old all-domestic identity. Worth watching whether that international push actually sticks or gets trimmed back like some of their past experiments.
