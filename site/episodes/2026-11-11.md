Episode title: O'Hare's FAA Excuse, Frontier's Fake Recline, and Emirates Still Waiting on the 777X
Episode Summary: United's CEO says O'Hare's taxi time problem isn't United's fault — it's the FAA's. Plus, Frontier finally starts selling first class seats that don't actually recline, Emirates get a trickle of 777-9s seven years late, and Alaska joins the exclusive club of airlines with secret elite status you can't apply for.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Wednesday, November 11th. Let's get into it.

So Scott Kirby's out here doing a bit of a victory lap, except the victory is blaming someone else. He says O'Hare's infamous taxi times — where you land and then just... sit there, slowly dying inside — are the FAA's fault, not United's. His comparison was that taxiing at O'Hare is basically like driving to Milwaukee.

Which, fair, I've felt that. But here's the actual story underneath the joke. The FAA's control towers, especially at big hubs like O'Hare, have been understaffed for years — this isn't new, it's the same staffing crisis that's been dragging down the whole system since well before this year. Kirby's argument is that getting towers back to full staffing could shave minutes off every single taxi, and when you multiply that across thousands of daily operations, it adds up to real fuel savings and real schedule reliability.

Here's what bugs me about it though. United controls a huge chunk of O'Hare's gates and schedule density. Part of why taxi times are brutal is that United and American have jammed so many flights into the same banks of time that ground control is basically directing rush hour traffic with a walkie-talkie. So yes, blame the FAA for being understaffed. But it's not the whole story, and Kirby knows that.

Next up, Frontier. Tickets for Frontier's first-ever first class seats are officially on sale now, for flights starting in March. Two rows, 2-by-2, black seats with a little green emblem, very sleek looking in the renderings.

Except — and I promise I'm not making this up — the seats don't recline. Frontier's official language is that they're "ergonomically reclined." Which is corporate-speak for "we built the shape of a recline into the cushion so we didn't have to build an actual reclining mechanism." And you'll still pay for snacks and drinks. So it's first class in the sense that you board first and sit in a slightly different chair. Honestly, good for Frontier for finding a new way to charge more for basically the same seat.

Emirates, meanwhile, is still waiting on its 777-9s, and the wait just got a little less terrible but still pretty terrible. Tim Clark says Emirates will get somewhere between three and five of the jets by the end of next year. For context, Emirates ordered 270 of these things, and Boeing originally promised certification back in 2020. So this plane is now more than seven years late, and the airline that bet biggest on it is getting single digits.

Clark also said he doesn't expect Boeing to deliver more than ten 777-9s total in all of 2028, across every airline that ordered one. That's Lufthansa, Singapore, Qatar, everybody, fighting over a production rate that still barely exists. This is the story of the 777X in one sentence — it's not a delivery schedule anymore, it's a waiting list with no clear end.

Alaska Airlines just launched something called Atmos Global Concierge, which is their new invite-only top-tier status, basically the Alaska version of American's ConciergeKey or United's Global Services. You can't apply for it, you just get tapped, and nobody will say what triggers the invite — though if I had to guess, it rhymes with "spend an absurd amount of money."

This matters because it tells you where Alaska sees itself now. A few years ago this would've been laughable — a west coast carrier having a secret ultra-elite tier like it's Delta or American. But Alaska's been pushing hard into international and premium travel since folding in Hawaiian, and this is them saying, we want to play in the same room as the legacy carriers.

Quick one before we go — Airbus just got Guinness to officially certify that wild 24-hour, 24-minute flight from Melbourne to Toulouse back in July, the long way around the planet. That plane's actually a test aircraft for the version Qantas will fly nonstop from Sydney to London starting late next year. So that ridiculous marathon flight record we keep talking about? It's getting closer to being a real ticket you can buy.
