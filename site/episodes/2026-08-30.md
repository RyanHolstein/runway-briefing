Episode Title: Icahn Bails on JetBlue, Everyone's Cutting Routes, and Your Engine Oil Might Be Lying to You
Episode Summary: Carl Icahn dumps his entire JetBlue stake at a loss after his board seats went up in smoke — so was this activist investing or just a really expensive shrug? Plus, Allegiant, Alaska, Frontier, and WestJet all take a hatchet to their fall schedules on the same day, a flight attendant union sounds the alarm on engine oil designed to NOT smell bad, and a Delta flight strands its own passengers after a TSA checkpoint closes for the night.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Sunday, August 30th. Let's get into it.

So, Carl Icahn and JetBlue. Remember back in early 2024 when he built a stake, said the airline was undervalued, and basically forced his way onto the board? Classic Icahn move — same playbook he ran at places like Southwest.

Well, he's out. Filings show he sold over 8 million shares across four straight trading days last week, and get this — the price dropped every single day he sold. August 17th he got $5.28 a share. By August 20th he was down to $4.73. He bought in near $6.

His two board appointees already resigned last week, which tells you this wasn't a graceful exit, it was a retreat. Now, forty million dollars is nothing to Icahn — the guy's worth billions — but the symbolism matters. When the activist investor who was supposed to fix your airline quietly slips out the back at a loss, that's not exactly a vote of confidence in the turnaround.

Okay, route cuts. It's like everybody got the same memo this week. Allegiant is dropping service to 11 airports, though to be fair, most of those come back in October, so call it seasonal thinning, not a bloodbath.

Alaska is cutting 7 routes but bringing back a popular intra-California hop. Frontier is slashing 8 routes out of O'Hare — which, when you're the low-cost alternative at the world's busiest airport, getting squeezed by United and American isn't shocking. And WestJet just cut 27 US routes. Twenty-seven.

That one's the real story here. Canadian leisure travel to the US has been cratering — you can blame the tariff fights, the "51st state" jokes, whatever you want, but Canadians are just not booking American vacations right now. When your neighbor's airline pulls almost thirty routes at once, that's not a scheduling adjustment, that's a market voting with its wallet.

Now here's one that should bother you. A union representing flight attendants is demanding an investigation into a new engine oil that's specifically designed to not smell bad.

It's called Turbo Oil 2330, made by Eastman Chemical, and it's meant for the auxiliary power unit. Here's the problem — when that oil leaks and gets sucked into the cabin air system, it normally smells awful, like a wet dog, and that smell is actually the warning sign that something's wrong. Take away the smell, and you take away the early warning for what the industry likes to call an "odor event" and what everyone else calls a toxic fume event.

Eastman's pitch is that airlines save money because they don't have to ground planes to investigate every time. Which — I get the business case, but engineering out the warning light instead of fixing the leak feels like exactly the kind of decision that looks great in a spreadsheet and terrible in a lawsuit five years from now.

Quick one on the ICE situation. The flight attendants union, AFA-CWA, sent guidance to its members this week reminding them they don't have to answer questions from ICE agents about coworkers or passengers, and they can't be forced to help with an arrest.

This comes right after a Southwest flight attendant was detained by ICE at his own home base last month, still in uniform. When flight attendants need legal guidance just to do their jobs safely, that tells you how tense the airport environment has gotten.

And finally, if you want a perfect little snapshot of how airlines treat you when things go sideways — a Delta flight from LA to Tampa diverted to Fort Myers Saturday night. A flight attendant reportedly told passengers to grab their bags from the carousel. Some did. Then found out TSA had already closed for the night, and the plane left without them.

I don't even need a joke here. That's just rough.
