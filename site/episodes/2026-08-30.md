Episode Title: United Bets Big on Small Planes, Boeing's Wings Have a Shim Problem, and a Guy Bomb-Threats His Own Flight
Episode Summary: United is going all-in on single-aisle jets for long-haul flying while Delta calls the idea a bad product — and we dig into why that split matters. Plus, the FAA drops a wing-crack directive on Boeing's 787 that American doesn't love, American finally admits its cabins are held together with duct tape, and a guy in Detroit phones in a bomb threat because he was running late for his own flight.

Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Sunday, August 30th. Let's get into it.

So United just announced ten new international routes for next summer — Luxembourg, Slovenia, the Azores, Sicily, places like that. And here's the thing that actually matters: most of these are flying on the A321XLR. A single-aisle plane. Across the Atlantic. For eight hours.

United's chief commercial officer basically said nobody else is doing this at scale, and he's right. Fifty of these jets are on order, decked out with real business class and premium economy, and United's betting travelers would rather fly nonstop to some under-the-radar city than connect through London or Paris on a widebody.

Delta thinks this is a bad idea. Their commercial chief straight up told The Points Guy, quote, "I don't know if it's the best product." Delta's sticking with big planes only on international routes, full stop.

Honestly, I get both sides. The economics of a single-aisle jet flying thin routes that could never support a 787 are great — you open up markets that were never viable before. But eight hours in a plane built for a three-hour domestic hop is a real ask, premium cabin or not.

And speaking of markets nobody's serving — Brett over at Live and Let's Fly asked a good question this week. If everyone's chasing new narrowbody routes, why is nobody betting on Manchester? Aer Lingus just pulled its long-haul operation out of there, and Manchester still moves over a million passengers a year to and from the US. That's real, proven demand just sitting there. With the XLR's range, someone could grab it cheap. Nobody has yet. Weird.

Okay, next. The FAA dropped a new directive on the Boeing 787 this week — wing skin splice plates, seventeen US-registered jets, inspections starting October 1st. The issue is shim gaps that exceeded engineering tolerances during manufacturing, which over time could let fatigue cracks grow into the wing structure. Not great, but caught early, which is the system working as intended.

What's actually funny here is American's complaint. The FAA set inspection intervals using an equation based on flight length instead of a flat number of hours, and American pushed back saying their tracking software can't handle equation-based compliance. The FAA's response was basically, that's your problem, not ours. Total cost to fix the fleet: four hundred thirteen thousand dollars, and parts are free under warranty. So really, this was never about safety — it was American trying to get an easier paperwork lift and getting told no.

Sticking with American — they're finally admitting something everyone who's flown them already knew. Cabins are falling apart. Broken seats, busted tray tables, entertainment screens that don't work. Their own chief customer officer said it out loud earlier this year: quote, "duct tape where we should not have duct tape." Now they're hiring over sixty new maintenance techs and rolling out a program called OneCabin starting September 14th at six hubs. Look, hiring people to fix seats is good. But you also need planes sitting on the ground long enough to actually do the work, and spare parts in stock. Hiring the crew is the easy part. We'll see if this actually moves the needle by next spring.

Alright, a palate cleanser. A guy in Detroit overslept, missed his Spirit flight to LA, and instead of just waiting twelve hours for the next one, he called Spirit's reservation line and said there was a bomb on board. Sixteen minutes on the phone, cops rushed the gate, dogs swept the plane, passengers got pulled off and searched. All so he wouldn't have to sit in the terminal. He was just sentenced — and got probation. No jail. For a fake bomb threat that grounded a commercial flight. I have thoughts about that sentence and none of them are positive.

And last one — Elon Musk is out here picking a fight with Delta again, claiming people are avoiding the airline because it doesn't have Starlink WiFi yet. Given his track record with Ryanair's CEO, I wouldn't expect this to stay civil. Delta hasn't really responded, which honestly might be the smartest move here.

One more thing before we go — Azriel Blackman, an American Airlines mechanic, passed away this week at 100 years old. He'd worked for the airline for over eighty years, which means he was literally older than American itself. That's a wild stat and a nice life. Rest easy.
