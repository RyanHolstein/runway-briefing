Episode title: Flydubai's Captain Breaks His Silence, American Starlink Goes All-In, and Southwest's Bag Fee Lawsuit Gets Dodged
Episode Summary: The flydubai captain who survived a mid-air stabbing describes fighting to open a bulletproof cockpit door with 184 lives on the line. Plus, American may be about to out-wifi Delta on every single plane, United keeps chipping away at what "first class" even means, Southwest wiggles out of a bag fee lawsuit with a very convenient new rule, and Thai Airways learns the hard way that lost luggage can cost you your job.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Friday, October 2nd. Let's get into it.

So remember that flydubai story from earlier this week — the cockpit attack on the flight from Dubai to Tel Aviv? We're getting new details, and honestly they're even more intense than what we knew.

Captain Smit Machchhar spoke from his hospital bed, and he described being stabbed, collapsing, and realizing the plane was descending. He's been flying 17 years, 11 as a captain, and he said he knew exactly what was happening just from the feel of the aircraft. So injured, bleeding, barely conscious — he forced open a bulletproof cockpit door. That's the only reason passengers and other off-duty pilots could get in and save the flight.

Israeli officials now believe the first officer was planning a 9/11-style attack into a building in Tel Aviv. A crash axe from the cockpit was the weapon. 184 people are alive because one badly wounded man pushed open a door he probably shouldn't have been able to move. Also, separately — Trump posted that the MAX "exceeded its design limits," which, sure, maybe, but we don't actually have an engineering report confirming that. Let's let the investigators finish before we hand out gold stars.

Okay, onto something lighter-ish. American Airlines might be about to leapfrog Delta on wifi. Back in May, American said Starlink was coming — but only to its Airbus planes, which is like a third of the fleet. Everybody raised an eyebrow at that.

Now JonNYC is reporting American's quietly expanding it to the 737s, the 777s, and the 787s too. Basically the whole operation. If that's true, it's a big deal, because Delta isn't getting fast satellite wifi — Amazon Leo — until 2028 at the earliest. So American could leapfrog Delta on the one thing everybody complains about on long flights. I'll believe it when American actually confirms it, but if this is real, that's American finding religion on product for once.

Update on something we touched on yesterday — United's "Base" fare thing. Turns out it's not just economy and Polaris anymore. United's rolling Base, Standard, and Flexible tiers into domestic first class and shorthaul international business too.

Here's the part that bugs me: Base first class strips out seat selection and flexibility — stuff that used to just come with buying a first class ticket. You're paying first class money and United's saying, actually, pay a little more if you want to pick your seat. I get tiered fares in economy. Basic economy is a trade — cheap seat, fewer perks, fine. But if someone's already paying for first class, nickel-and-diming them feels like it undercuts the whole premium pitch. Honestly, feels like United's trying to squeeze every fare bucket they can find.

Southwest's got a fun one. Years ago they said charging for bags would cost them 300 million dollars a year. Then they started charging anyway, and a shareholder sued, saying the board caved to an activist investor just to save their own board seats.

So how'd Southwest get out of it? Texas passed a law letting companies require a 3 percent ownership stake before a shareholder can even sue. Southwest adopted that rule immediately. At the time, 3 percent was worth over half a billion dollars, so basically nobody could afford to sue them anymore. Convenient, right? That's not really winning the argument, that's just changing who's allowed in the room.

And last one — if you're job hunting, Thai Airways is hiring a CEO. They just fired Chai Eamsiri, and this is actually kind of a bummer story because the guy had done real work — pulled the airline out of bankruptcy, got it back on the stock exchange. But a baggage meltdown at Bangkok's airport, stranded luggage, cargo chaos, cancellations — that was apparently the final straw. Reminds you that no matter how good your balance sheet looks, if bags are piling up on the tarmac, the board stops caring about the spreadsheet.
