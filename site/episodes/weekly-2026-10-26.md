Weekly Deep Dive: Is United About to Buy JetBlue?

Episode Summary: JetBlue's stock has cratered to all-time lows, creditors have brought in bankruptcy lawyers, and United's CEO is already talking about "the deal of a generation." Ryan and Hope dig into whether this is really the first big airline acquisition in over a decade, what United would actually be buying, and why antitrust regulators already killed a smaller version of this exact deal.

RYAN: Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing Weekly. I'm Ryan, and I've got Hope with me.

HOPE: Hey! So this week we're digging into JetBlue, and specifically, whether it's actually going to survive the year as an independent company.

RYAN: Yeah. And I want to be careful here because we touched on this in the daily a couple times already. Creditors hiring bankruptcy advisers, stock at three bucks. But today we're going all the way back. Why is JetBlue actually in this position, what does it mean if United buys them, and why this already almost happened once and got blocked.

HOPE: Right, because this isn't the first time somebody tried to merge JetBlue into something bigger.

RYAN: Not even close. So let's set the table. JetBlue stock closed under three dollars this week. That's a company that, at its peak in like 2018, was trading around twenty bucks. We're talking an eighty-five percent decline from the high.

HOPE: Eighty-five percent. That's not a bad quarter, that's a company falling apart.

RYAN: And the thing that really spooked people isn't even the stock price, it's that bondholders hired restructuring advisers. That's a specific signal. Companies don't do that for fun. When creditors bring in a restructuring shop, they're preparing for the possibility that this company can't pay what it owes.

HOPE: So walk me through that. What does "restructuring adviser" actually mean in practice? Like, what are they doing day one?

RYAN: Basically they're lawyers and bankers whose entire job is figuring out, if this company goes into Chapter 11, how do we get our money back, in what order, and do we force a sale instead of a reorganization. JetBlue has about two billion dollars tied up in frequent flyer debt, the stuff backed by the TrueBlue loyalty program.

HOPE: And that debt is trading at forty-two cents on the dollar right now.

RYAN: Forty-two cents. Which means the market is pricing in that if you hold a dollar of JetBlue's loyalty-backed debt, you're only getting forty-two cents back. That's not "this company is having a rough quarter" pricing. That's "we think this company might not make it through in its current form" pricing.

HOPE: Okay, but here's my question as the person who doesn't trade bonds for a living. Why loyalty debt specifically? Why would that be the thing creditors are worried about?

RYAN: Because loyalty programs are actually the most valuable asset most airlines have. Delta's SkyMiles program alone has been valued at more than the entire airline at various points. United did a loyalty-backed bond offering during COVID worth like six billion dollars. It's basically the airline borrowing against its own frequent flyer database, because that database prints money through credit card partnerships.

HOPE: So if that paper is trading at forty-two cents, the market is saying even JetBlue's best asset is in trouble.

RYAN: Exactly. And that's new. Three years ago nobody was talking about JetBlue going bankrupt. They were the plucky number five carrier that flew with free Wi-Fi and extra legroom and made everybody else look cheap.

HOPE: Right, that was kind of their whole brand for twenty years. "We're the good guys of air travel."

RYAN: And it worked! JetBlue basically invented the idea that a low-cost carrier could also have a decent product. Before JetBlue, your options were basically Southwest, which was great but no seatback TVs, or the legacy guys, who were miserable in economy. JetBlue shows up in 1999 and says, you can have leather seats, personal TVs, and still pay less than American.

HOPE: And then Mint came along, right? The lie-flat business product?

RYAN: Mint launched in 2014, and that was honestly one of the smarter moves in the industry that decade. JetBlue takes transcon routes, JFK to LA, JFK to San Francisco, Boston to LA, and offers lie-flat suites for way less than what American or United or Delta charge. For a while that was a genuine threat to the legacy carriers on their most profitable domestic routes.

HOPE: So what happened? How do you go from "genuine threat to the legacy carriers" to "creditors are calling lawyers" in a decade?

RYAN: A few things stacked on top of each other. First, the Northeast Alliance. JetBlue partnered up with American in 2020, basically code-sharing and coordinating schedules out of New York and Boston. The idea was, combine forces against Delta and United in the Northeast. DOJ sued to block it, a federal judge ruled in 2023 that it violated antitrust law, and JetBlue had to unwind the whole thing.

HOPE: So that's a few years of strategy and legal fees just gone.

RYAN: Gone. And right as that's collapsing, JetBlue pivots to plan B, which is buying Spirit outright for about thirty-eight hundred million dollars, call it three point eight billion. The idea being, instead of partnering with American, just absorb a competitor and get bigger that way.

HOPE: And that one also got blocked.

RYAN: Blocked even harder. DOJ sued in 2024, argued that killing Spirit as an independent ultra-low-cost carrier would raise fares for budget travelers, a federal judge agreed, and the deal died. JetBlue had already spent enormous money on legal fees, had committed financing, had basically bet the strategy of the company on this merger going through, and then it just evaporates.

HOPE: So two strategic bets in a row, both blocked by the same federal government, both over the span of about four years.

RYAN: And in the meantime, while JetBlue is fighting regulators instead of running the airline, Delta and American are pouring money into premium cabins, into their loyalty programs, into international partnerships. Southwest starts actually assigning seats and adding extra legroom options for the first time in company history, which directly eats into JetBlue's "we're the nice airline" positioning. JetBlue gets squeezed from both directions.

HOPE: Squeezed how, exactly? Like, where specifically did they lose the customer?

RYAN: On the low end, ultra-low-cost carriers like Frontier and Spirit, even in their weakened state, are still cheaper for the price-first traveler. On the high end, Delta and American have better international networks, better lounges, better loyalty redemption value. JetBlue's stuck in the middle without the scale to really win either fight.

HOPE: And that's before we even get to the engine problem.

RYAN: Right, the Pratt and Whitney geared turbofan issue. JetBlue flies a lot of A220s and A320neo family jets with those engines, and the fleet-wide inspections tied to a contamination issue in the metal powder used to manufacture certain engine parts have grounded a meaningful chunk of their fleet for extended stretches. You're paying crews, paying leases, and the airplane's sitting on the ground.

HOPE: So it's not even just competitive pressure, it's literally planes they can't fly.

RYAN: Right. And that's expensive in a way that's really hard to plan around, because Pratt and Whitney is compensating operators for it, but compensation doesn't replace the revenue of a flying airplane sitting full of paying customers.

HOPE: Okay, so now bring me to this month. Stock at three dollars, bondholders calling lawyers. And then on top of all that, JetBlue starts giving away free first class upgrades.

RYAN: Which, by the way, is a really telling move. When an airline starts loosening up premium inventory and handing out perks like that, it's usually one of two things. Either they're trying to build loyalty during a tough stretch, or they're trying to generate buzz and keep cash flowing through ancillary spend while the core business struggles.

HOPE: And this week they also quietly changed some of the loyalty perks into one-time passes instead of repeatable benefits, right? Like status perks that used to renew every year now you only get once.

RYAN: Yeah, which is a cost-cutting move dressed up as a feature. It's a way of saying, we're still giving you something nice, without committing to the recurring cost of giving every elite member that benefit every single year forever.

HOPE: So desperate but trying not to look desperate.

RYAN: Pretty much. And that's the backdrop for the big question everybody's asking now, which is, does United swoop in?

HOPE: Right, because Scott Kirby didn't exactly hide the ball on this one. He's literally called it the deal of a generation.

RYAN: He did, and Kirby is not a guy who's shy about saying the quiet part out loud. We talked about this a little in the daily, but let's actually get into why United specifically, and why now.

HOPE: Okay, so why United and not, say, Delta or American?

RYAN: A few reasons. One, United doesn't have a strong Northeast position the way American and Delta do. Delta's dominant at LaGuardia and JFK. American's strong in Philly and still has a real presence in Boston and New York despite the failed JetBlue alliance. United's always been the weakest of the big three in that corridor.

HOPE: So JetBlue's slots and gates at JFK, LaGuardia, Boston Logan, that's the prize.

RYAN: That's a massive part of the prize. Slots at those airports are basically impossible to get any other way. The FAA caps the number of takeoffs and landings, and almost all of them are already owned by existing carriers. You can't just decide to grow at LaGuardia. You either buy somebody who already has the slots, or you don't grow there, period.

HOPE: It's like trying to get an apartment in Manhattan where the only way in is to know somebody who already has a lease.

RYAN: That's actually a pretty good analogy. And then beyond the Northeast slots, JetBlue's got a really strong Caribbean and Latin America network out of Fort Lauderdale and Orlando, which complements United's stronger international long-haul network. And JetBlue's got Mint, a premium product with actual brand recognition, which fits Kirby's whole pitch about United being the premium alternative to what he calls Motel 6 service at other carriers.

HOPE: Oh, the Motel 6 line, I remember that from earlier this week.

RYAN: Right, where he compared competitors to a Four Seasons room bolted onto a Motel 6. If United buys JetBlue, they get Mint's premium transcon brand, TrueBlue's loyalty base, a fleet of A220s and A320 family jets that could slot into United's existing Airbus operations, and instant scale in the one region of the country where United's always been an afterthought.

HOPE: Okay, but here's where I get stuck. Didn't the government just spend years blocking basically this exact idea? Like, JetBlue plus American was blocked. JetBlue plus Spirit was blocked. Why would JetBlue plus United be any different?

RYAN: This is the single biggest question hanging over the whole thing, and I don't think there's a clean answer yet. The Northeast Alliance case and the Spirit case were both about reducing competition while all the companies involved were healthy. The legal argument is totally different if JetBlue is actually failing.

HOPE: You mean like a "failing firm" defense?

RYAN: Exactly, that's the actual legal term. Under antitrust law, if a company is genuinely failing and would otherwise liquidate, a merger that would normally be blocked can sometimes be allowed, because the alternative isn't "JetBlue stays independent and keeps competing," the alternative is "JetBlue disappears entirely and all those jobs and routes vanish anyway."

HOPE: So if JetBlue's distressed enough, the government's logic might flip from "this reduces competition" to "there was no competition left to protect."

RYAN: Right, and that's the bet Kirby's making, I think. He's watching the stock price and the credit markets do the government's argument for him. If JetBlue actually files Chapter 11, the whole conversation about whether this merger reduces competition gets a lot murkier, because you can argue the realistic alternative is JetBlue getting broken up and sold off in pieces to a private equity group, not JetBlue staying independent and healthy.

HOPE: Okay, I want to push back a little bit though. Isn't there something kind of convenient about an airline CEO publicly talking about buying a struggling competitor right while that competitor's stock is tanking? Like, does that talk itself make it worse for JetBlue?

RYAN: That's a totally fair point, and honestly I think that's part of the game here. When Kirby says "deal of a generation" publicly, that's not an accident. It puts pressure on JetBlue's board, it signals to the market that United's serious, and it potentially discourages other buyers from getting into a bidding war, because why compete with United when everyone assumes they're already circling.

HOPE: So it's a little bit of a flex and a little bit of actual deal strategy.

RYAN: I think so. And look, we've seen this movie before in this industry. Go back to 2010, United and Continental merge. That deal took a while to actually show results, there were real integration headaches, but ultimately it created the United we know today. Go back further, America West essentially takes over US Airways in 2005 but keeps the US Airways name because it had more brand value, and that whole combined entity ends up merging into American in 2013.

HOPE: So every major airline today is basically a Frankenstein's monster of three or four older airlines stitched together.

RYAN: Pretty much. Delta's a combination of the old Delta and Northwest. American absorbed US Airways, which had already absorbed America West and the old US Airways and Piedmont and PSA. United absorbed Continental. This is just how the industry has worked for twenty years, consolidation after consolidation, and we haven't really had a big one since 2013. If this happens, it's the first true big merger wave moment in over a decade.

HOPE: Okay, so let's say this actually happens. Who loses?

RYAN: Consumers in the Northeast corridor, probably. JetBlue was the one carrier consistently pushing fares down on routes like Boston to Florida, New York to the Caribbean, transcon flights to LA and San Francisco. If United absorbs them, you lose an independent low-fare competitor on a bunch of those routes.

HOPE: So the thing DOJ was worried about with the Spirit deal, basically happens anyway, just with a different buyer.

RYAN: That's the irony of it, yeah. The DOJ blocked JetBlue from buying Spirit specifically to protect low fares. If JetBlue instead gets absorbed into United, you still lose an independent low-fare brand in a bunch of these markets, it just happens through bankruptcy and acquisition instead of through JetBlue doing the buying.

HOPE: What about JetBlue employees? What happens to them?

RYAN: Historically, in every one of these mergers, there's significant overlap and significant pain. Pilots get integrated into a single seniority list, which is always a fight, always takes years, always ends up with lawsuits from somebody. Flight attendants, gate agents, there's usually some consolidation of positions where routes overlap. It's never clean.

HOPE: And what about actual JetBlue customers? Like, somebody listening right now who's got a TrueBlue account with points sitting in it, should they be worried?

RYAN: Not worried exactly, but it's worth knowing the precedent. When Alaska bought Virgin America in 2016, Virgin America's loyalty program eventually got folded into Alaska's Mileage Plan, and there was a conversion process, some people felt like they lost value in the translation, some felt like they gained it depending on how they used points. When American and US Airways merged, Dividend Miles became AAdvantage, again with a conversion formula.

HOPE: So history says, yes eventually your miles probably become United miles, but the exchange rate on that conversion is where people get grumpy.

RYAN: Exactly. And given that we just established JetBlue's loyalty debt is trading at forty-two cents on the dollar, you'd have to assume whatever program United inherits, they're not going to value that at full face value when they design the conversion.

HOPE: Right, so even if your miles survive, they might survive at a discount.

RYAN: That's historically been the pattern, yeah.

HOPE: Okay, let's talk timeline, because I think that's what everybody actually wants to know. Is this a this-year thing? Is this a next-year thing?

RYAN: My honest read, and this is speculation, is that bondholders hiring restructuring advisers usually means you're looking at months, not years. Companies typically try to negotiate a prepackaged bankruptcy with creditors before they ever file, which means a lot of this negotiation is probably already happening behind closed doors right now, this week, this month.

HOPE: What's a prepackaged bankruptcy exactly, for people who don't know?

RYAN: It's where the company negotiates the terms of the restructuring with its major creditors before it actually files Chapter 11, so that when it does file, it moves through court quickly because everybody already agrees on the plan. It's way faster and way less chaotic than a free-fall bankruptcy where creditors are fighting each other in court for months.

HOPE: So if I had to bet, what's your actual prediction? Does JetBlue end up as part of United, or does something else happen?

RYAN: If I had to bet, I think JetBlue files some form of Chapter 11 within the next six to twelve months, and I think whether United ends up buying them depends entirely on whether other bidders show up. Alaska's already digesting Hawaiian, so I don't think they've got the appetite for another big integration right now. Southwest's dealing with its own activist investor pressure and just started flirting with widebody aircraft for the first time ever, so their hands are kind of full. American already got burned twice trying to partner with JetBlue.

HOPE: What about private equity? Could somebody just buy the pieces and run JetBlue as a standalone company, leaner?

RYAN: It's possible, that's actually a real path in these situations, somebody comes in, strips out the parts that don't work, keeps the Northeast slots and the Caribbean network, and runs a smaller, meaner JetBlue. But private equity running an airline has a mixed track record historically, and slots in particular are hard for a financial buyer to monetize the same way an existing airline could.

HOPE: So you think it's more likely United ends up with at least a big chunk of this than somebody else.

RYAN: I'd put United as the favorite, yeah, mostly because Kirby's already out there talking about it publicly, and because United's the strategic buyer with the clearest reason to want exactly what JetBlue has. But I wouldn't be shocked if it ends up partial, like United takes the slots and the premium routes and somebody else, maybe a financial buyer, takes the rest.

HOPE: That's kind of a wild thought, that JetBlue as a brand might just not exist in two years.

RYAN: It really might not. And that's the bigger story underneath all of this. This isn't really about JetBlue specifically, it's about whether the US airline industry is heading into another multi-year consolidation wave, the same way it did from 2008 to 2013. Four legacy carriers became three big ones plus Southwest. If this JetBlue situation plays out the way it looks like it might, we could be looking at four major players again in a slightly different shape, just with United bigger than it's ever been.

HOPE: And that affects every single person who flies, not just JetBlue customers.

RYAN: Right, because less competition generally means less pressure on fares over time. It's worth watching really closely over the next few months, because I think this is the first domino, not the only one.
