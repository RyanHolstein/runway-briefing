Episode Title: An Independent Reporter Loses His Home, Etihad Bets Big, and United's CEO Wants Chicago All to Himself
Episode Summary: The founder of Aviation Herald, one of the most trusted names in aviation safety reporting, just lost his home fighting a defamation lawsuit for reporting something that turned out to be true. Plus, a Russian smuggling ring gets caught funneling spare parts to sanctioned Aeroflot, Etihad announces a massive $20 billion expansion plan, and United's Scott Kirby has some very convenient thoughts about American's struggles in Chicago.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Monday, August 31st. Let's get into it.

So first, a story that honestly made me kind of angry. Simon Hradecky, the guy who runs Aviation Herald — if you follow aviation safety at all, you know this site. Since 2008, he's documented over 32,000 incident reports. No clickbait, no drama, just facts and updates when facts change.

Here's what happened. Last June, he reported a Go2Sky 737 had an engine problem based on a passenger report. Next morning, he got better information — it was actually a stabilizer trim issue, not the engine — and he corrected it. That's... literally the job. That's what good reporting looks like.

Go2Sky sued him anyway. In Austria. Over a report he'd already fixed. And the legal costs got so bad that Hradecky had to sell the apartment he'd lived in for 41 years and move into his office.

This matters because independent aviation reporting runs on people like Hradecky — no big media company backing him, no legal team on retainer. If airlines can bankrupt someone for correcting a mistake within 24 hours, that's not defamation law working. That's a chilling effect. And it should scare anyone who cares about who's actually watching the skies.

Okay, next. A Florida man just got convicted on all twelve counts for smuggling nine hundred thousand dollars worth of aircraft parts to Aeroflot. Remember, Russia's been under sanctions since 2022 specifically to keep Western spare parts out and ground their fleet.

Alexander Mamonov, former Aeroflot employee, set up a shell company literally named "Sky Gift" — subtle — and routed parts through a UAE middleman so U.S. suppliers had no idea where they were really headed. Oil filters, engine parts, the works.

Four and a half years into the war, Aeroflot's Western jets are still flying, and this case is basically a window into how. Sanctions only work if nobody's willing to be the guy in Florida with a shell company. Turns out, someone always is.

Alright, switching gears — Etihad had some big news. They're launching eight North American routes this year, their busiest lineup in the airline's 23-year history, including their first-ever route to Western Canada with Calgary. But the bigger number is the twenty billion dollars they're putting into next-gen aircraft and new routes, with a stated goal of hitting a hundred fifty destinations.

That's an airline that's done playing regional player and wants a real seat at the global table with Emirates and Qatar. Worth watching whether Abu Dhabi can actually pull traffic away from Dubai and Doha, because right now, that's a very crowded neighborhood for connecting passengers.

Next — Scott Kirby's at it again. United's CEO says American should just de-hub Chicago O'Hare, and that it'd actually be great for Chicago because, in his words, more destinations. Brett at Cranky Flier had a great breakdown of this — turns out Kirby's not wrong that American's losing over a billion dollars a year in Chicago. But here's the thing Kirby conveniently leaves out — if American folds in Chicago, United gets a hub running a thousand daily departures, basically unchallenged. Every other airline in this country has a vested interest in NOT letting that happen. So yeah, Kirby's right about the math. He's just also the guy who benefits the most from saying it out loud.

Last one, and this is just wild. Two Airlink Embraer jets did a flyover of a packed stadium in Cape Town before Saturday's Springboks-All Blacks match — at forty-five feet. Forty-five feet above the roof, at a hundred fifty knots, with pyrotechnics going off underneath them. Airlink says it was all approved and rehearsed with three senior pilots in each cockpit.

Look, I get the tradition — this is literally recreating the 1995 Mandela flyover that's in the movie Invictus. But that was a 747 at a stadium, way higher up. Forty-five feet over a packed roof feels like something a safety board is going to have opinions about eventually.
