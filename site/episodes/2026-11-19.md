Runway Briefing: Burbank's Tower Goes Dark, United Bets Big on Cleveland, and Qantas Paints a Dog on a Superjumbo
Episode Summary: Pilots at Burbank had to play air traffic controller themselves when the tower just... emptied out. Plus, United makes a surprising comeback at a hub it abandoned over a decade ago, Boeing's two oldest jets head in completely opposite directions, and Qantas slaps Bluey on an A380 because of course it does.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Thursday, November 19th. Let's get into it.

So first up, and this one's a little unsettling. Hollywood Burbank Airport's control tower went completely dark Wednesday night. Not short-staffed — empty. Zero controllers. That's officially called "ATC Zero," and it means pilots have to coordinate landings and takeoffs themselves, like it's a grass strip in the middle of nowhere.

There's actual audio of Southwest and Alaska pilots radioing each other — "Southwest's on final, runway 8," that kind of thing — doing the tower's job for them. And look, pilots train for this. It's not unsafe, exactly. But Burbank is not some sleepy regional field. It's a real commercial airport in the middle of Los Angeles.

We've been talking about the FAA's staffing mess all week — the floppy disks, the ground stop meltdown, Sean Duffy asking for fifty billion dollars. This is what that crisis actually looks like on the ground. It's not an abstract budget fight anymore. It's two airliners talking to each other on an open channel because nobody's in the tower. Mullin over at AirlineGeeks even floated that some of this month's tech failures might be AI-related. Either way, the system is held together with duct tape right now, and Burbank's just the latest crack.

Okay, something more upbeat. United is going big on Cleveland.

Remember, United de-hubbed Cleveland back in like 2014. Just walked away. Now they're quietly rebuilding it — adding routes all year, and Simple Flying's calling it United's biggest domestic bet in years. Why now? Honestly, it's about filling gaps between the bigger hubs — Chicago, Newark, DC — and scooping up leisure traffic that Spirit and Frontier used to fight over before half of them started struggling. Cleveland's cheap to operate, there's local demand, and nobody else really wants it. Sometimes the smart move isn't the flashy international route — it's just quietly owning a city nobody's paying attention to.

Next, a genuinely interesting contrast out of Boeing's own backyard.

In Renton, they just put brand new LEAP engines on the 737 MAX — a jet whose airframe traces back to the 1960s. Meanwhile, thirty miles away in Everett, Boeing is shutting down the 767 line in 2027. And the 767 is actually the newer design — 1980s engineering. So you've got the older plane getting a second life, and the newer one getting retired. Why? Simple: demand. Airlines want narrowbodies that sip fuel on short and medium routes, and the MAX fits that perfectly after an engine swap. The 767's niche — mid-size widebody, cargo conversions — just isn't big enough anymore. Age doesn't matter in this business. Relevance does.

Last one, and it's just fun. Qantas is putting Bluey on an Airbus A380.

Yes, the cartoon dog. If you don't have small kids, Bluey is basically Australia's Peppa Pig, and apparently it's popular enough that Qantas is wrapping a superjumbo in it, plus two other planes, Bluey lounges in five cities, and themed kids' kits starting January. Honestly? Smart move. Qantas flies some brutally long routes — Perth to London, Sydney to LA — and anything that keeps a toddler occupied for fifteen hours is worth its weight in gold to every single adult on that plane. Cynical marketing, sure. But I'd take a Bluey coloring kit over screaming for fifteen hours any day.
