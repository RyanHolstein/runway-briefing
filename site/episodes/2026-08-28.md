So here's the deal. American Airlines rolled out its summer 2027 long-haul network plans, and on paper, it looks like a nice little expansion. Three "new" destinations, seven new routes, tickets going on sale August 31st. But — and this is a big "but" — the timing could not have been worse for American, because just 48 hours before this announcement, United dropped what it's calling the largest international expansion in its history. Ten new cities. A real swing-for-the-fences moment. And American followed that up with... this.

Let's break down what's actually new, because as Cranky Flier pointed out, American did some creative bulking-up of this list. Of the three "new" destinations — Vienna, Porto, and Reykjavik — only Vienna is genuinely net-new. American already announced Porto back in February, and Reykjavik is really a return, since American flew there before the pandemic and just hasn't gone back until now. All three will connect through Philadelphia, which remains American's transatlantic stronghold.

Beyond that, you've got some real route additions worth mentioning. JFK to Amsterdam is a notable one — that makes New York the third American hub connected to the Netherlands, joining Dallas/Fort Worth and Philadelphia. JFK is also picking up Nice, giving New Yorkers a second gateway to the French Riviera after Philly got that route back in 2024. And Charlotte is finally getting Barcelona back, a route American hasn't flown since 2019, plus a fourth daily Charlotte–Paris frequency.

Now here's the part that actually explains why this announcement feels underwhelming, and it's not really American's fault. Cranky Flier dug into this, and it turns out American expected to have thirteen new A321XLRs ready to deploy for summer 2027. Instead, thanks to delivery delays from Airbus, they're only getting five. Five! That's a massive shortfall, and it fundamentally caps what American could roll out this cycle. So a lot of this announcement ends up being padded with things that were already in the schedule — frequency bumps, seasonal date shifts moved up a few weeks, stuff that sounds like news but really isn't. Chicago to Tokyo starting in March, for example, was already booked. Miami to Milan starting a few weeks earlier — already happening.

So is this a disappointing announcement? Kind of, yeah. But it's less about American lacking ambition and more about American literally not having the airplanes to be ambitious with. The real story here might be what this means for Airbus's XLR production line, because if American's only getting five out of thirteen promised jets, that's a supply chain conversation that's bigger than just one airline's summer schedule. And it means American's genuinely big swing — whatever that looks like — probably doesn't happen until 2028, once more of those XLRs actually show up on the ramp.

Meanwhile, the elephant in the room is obviously United's expansion, which we covered here recently — ten new international cities in one of the most aggressive route announcements from any U.S. carrier in years. Put side by side, American's move this week just doesn't have the same gravitational pull. It's not that American did something wrong, it's that United set a bar this week that made everyone else's announcement look small by comparison. Timing really is everything in this business, and American either didn't realize United was about to steal its thunder, or didn't have a choice given its own internal deadlines. Either way, the optics aren't great.
