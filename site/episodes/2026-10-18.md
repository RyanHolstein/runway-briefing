Episode Title: Air India's $2.3 Billion Problem, a Wild Night of Emergency Landings, and the Vending Machine That Sold Death Insurance
Episode Summary: Air India loses billions and brings in the guy who fixed Ethiopian Airlines — can he do it twice? Plus, three different planes had very bad days in the last 24 hours, Virgin Atlantic finally launches its Phuket route, American wants flight attendants selling credit cards or else, and the weirdest piece of aviation history you'll hear this week: airport vending machines that sold flight insurance.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Sunday, October 18th. Let's get into it.

So, Air India. They lost two point three billion dollars last year. Not revenue — that's the loss. And it basically doubled from the year before.

Revenue actually went down too, almost nine percent, so this isn't one bad quarter, this is a trend going the wrong direction. Tata's chairman called it a quote "five to ten year journey," which is corporate speak for "don't expect this to get better anytime soon."

Here's the move though. They just hired the guy who rebuilt Ethiopian Airlines to come run the turnaround. That's a serious hire — Ethiopian is one of the only African carriers that's actually profitable and well-run, so if anyone's got the playbook, it's him.

But here's what bugs me. In the chairman's whole list of reasons for the losses — old aircraft, supply chain issues, legacy systems, culture — nowhere does he mention Flight 171. That's the 787 that crashed in Ahmedabad last year and killed 260 people. I'm not saying the crash caused the balance sheet problem. But when your flagship airline has a disaster like that, it affects bookings, it affects confidence, and leaving it out of the conversation entirely is a choice.

Okay, buckle up, because it was a rough twenty four hours for emergency landings. Three separate incidents.

First, an American Eagle flight out of St. Louis — oxygen masks dropped, cabin filled with haze, and passengers evacuated right onto the runway. Then a LATAM 767 flying Lima to São Paulo lost cabin pressure and dropped twenty three thousand feet in an emergency descent. And a United 737 MAX had to divert to Chicago because a passenger got physically trapped in the lavatory at thirty five thousand feet.

None of these are connected, they're just three unrelated bad days that happened to land in the same news cycle. But it's a good reminder — the oxygen mask drill they do before every flight? There's a reason for that, and planes are actually built to handle this stuff. Everyone's fine. It's just a lot for one Saturday.

On a happier note — Virgin Atlantic finally launched that Heathrow to Phuket route today, after basically a year of people asking "is this actually happening." Fourteen hours nonstop, first time Virgin's flown that route from Heathrow. Phuket's a huge winter sun destination for Brits trying to escape the gray, so this is Virgin going after leisure demand in a market that's mostly been owned by Thai carriers and one-stop connections. Smart timing, right as everyone starts booking their January escape.

Now, something that's going to annoy a lot of flight attendants. American Airlines just made credit card sales training mandatory for all thirty thousand of their cabin crew. Here's the thing — actually selling the card is still optional, you get a commission if you do it. But now everyone has to sit through the training module whether they want to sling credit cards or not, and skip it, you can get disciplined.

The flight attendants' union is not happy, and honestly I get it. You're putting a sales pitch in the same training bucket as human trafficking awareness and de-escalation training. That's a weird hierarchy of priorities. But airlines make absurd money off these co-branded cards — way more than ticket sales in some cases — so this isn't surprising, it's just tone-deaf.

Last one, and it's a weird piece of history. View From the Wing dug up old photos of airport vending machines — literally vending machines — that sold flight insurance. Pay eight bucks, get three million dollars if your plane crashes. This was a real thing for decades.

And here's the dark part — some people actually bought the policy and then sabotaged the plane themselves for the payout. Which, yeah. That's exactly why that business model doesn't exist anymore.
