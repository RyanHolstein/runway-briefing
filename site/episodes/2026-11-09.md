Episode title: Miami's Near-Miss, Frontier's Executive Exodus, and KLM's Big Fuel Bet
Episode Summary: A Lufthansa 747 nearly gets clipped by an American 787 in Miami — the third close call we've covered in two weeks. Plus, Frontier brings in a Wizz Air exec as two of its own leaders quietly walk out, KLM takes delivery of its first A350 in a long-haul fleet overhaul, Ryanair explains why it'll never touch an Airbus, and American tries to stiff a bumped passenger out of 650 bucks.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Monday, November 9th. Let's get into it.

So remember Friday, when we talked about that Air France A350 turning toward an American jet right after takeoff at LAX? Well, add another one to the pile. Last week at Miami, a Lufthansa 747-8 was taxiing out for departure when an American 787 turned straight into its path — the Dreamliner crew apparently just didn't hold short like they were supposed to.

The 747 had to slam on the brakes. ATC audio of the whole thing is floating around online now, and it is tense. Controllers describing it afterward said it was, quote, "very close to an accident."

Here's what bugs me about this one. That's a jumbo jet and a widebody converging on the ground, not in the air, which honestly should be the easier problem to prevent. Taxiway incursions are supposed to be the boring, solvable part of aviation safety — paint lines, hold-short instructions, basic procedure. And yet this is the third serious near-miss we've talked about in like a week and a half, counting LAX and that Cessna getting flipped by jet blast at John Wayne. Three isn't a coincidence, that's a trend, and it's usually a staffing or workload story underneath it.

Okay, Frontier. They just hired a new Chief Operating Officer, Michael Delehant, coming over from Wizz Air, starts November 30th. Normally fine, except two senior Frontier leaders are walking out the door at the same time — the head of operations and the head of customer experience. No explanation given for either.

Think about the timing here. The ops chief and the customer chief, both gone, right as the new COO they'll be reporting to shows up. That's not a retirement party, that's a reorganization, or a falling-out, or both. And it's happening while Frontier's trying to launch first class, Spirit's basically gone, Allegiant and Sun Country just merged, and JetBlue is struggling. Frontier's betting big on going upmarket right as its own front office is getting reshuffled. Rough time to lose institutional knowledge.

KLM just took delivery of its first Airbus A350, with 21 more on the way, and it's replacing the airline's older widebodies — we're talking 25 percent less fuel burn and CO2 per flight. That's a real number, not marketing fluff.

This is the same math we've talked about before with Qantas and the A380 — new twin-engine jets just torch less fuel than the old quads and early-generation widebodies they're replacing. What's interesting is how split the industry is on which plane gets to be the new flagship. Air Canada's about to fly its first Airbus widebody in thirty years with the A350. Emirates, meanwhile, just added 65 more orders for the Boeing 777-9, bringing their order book to 270. Same decade, same industry, completely opposite bets on what the future widebody looks like.

Quick one on Ryanair, because I get asked this a lot — why do they only fly 737s? It's not brand loyalty, it's just spreadsheet logic. One aircraft type means one set of pilots, one set of mechanics, one spare parts inventory, and way lower training costs. Alaska had to give up that model once they merged with Virgin America and Hawaiian, both Airbus shops. Ryanair's avoided that trap by just never merging with anybody who flies something else. Boring strategy, extremely profitable strategy.

And last one, because it's just annoying. American Airlines bumped a passenger off a Cleveland flight, and a gate agent reportedly promised her 650 dollars. American's now saying that doesn't count, because the plane got swapped for a smaller one, which apparently gets them out of federal bumping rules. She got 250 in credit, then another 192 after a reporter started asking questions, so she's sitting at 442 out of a promised 650. Not a huge story, just a good reminder that "the gate agent said" means nothing once corporate gets involved.
