Episode Title: Air Canada's A321XLR Face-Plants on London, Delta and United Want to Be Singapore Airlines, and Frontier Guts Elite Status
Episode Summary: Air Canada's shiny new A321XLR couldn't even make it through its debut season on the Toronto-London route before getting yanked. Plus, Delta's Ed Bastian and United's Scott Kirby are both suddenly dropping Singapore Airlines and Emirates in interviews, Qantas is retiring the A380 early, and Frontier's elite program gets stripped for parts. Grab a beer, this one's got some good absurdity in it.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Saturday, August 29th. Let's get into it.

So, Air Canada. They had this whole plan — brand new A321XLR, single-aisle jet, flying Toronto to London Heathrow. One of the flagship transatlantic routes in North America. The pitch was basically "look how efficient we are, we don't even need a widebody for this."

Great story. Except it lasted, what, a few months? They already pulled it. Widebodies are back on that route — 777s, 787s — because it turns out when you swap a big jet for a skinny one on your busiest corridor, you lose seats and you lose cargo space. Shocking, I know.

Look, the XLR is a legitimately cool plane. It's just not a private jet dressed up as a widebody. Air Canada tried to use a scalpel where they needed a sledgehammer, and London corrected them real fast. United's about to fly the same plane on some domestic routes out of Dulles before it goes transatlantic in December, so, everybody's watching this experiment. Air Canada's just the first one to eat it.

Okay, next thing. This one's kind of funny in a "great minds think alike" way. Delta's CEO Ed Bastian went on a podcast this week and said he wants Delta mentioned in the same breath as Singapore Airlines and Emirates. Not just America's best airline — one of the world's best.

Here's the thing — United's Scott Kirby said basically the identical thing, in Denver, the same week. Both CEOs, out of nowhere, reaching for Singapore Airlines as the measuring stick. Which tells you something. Neither airline thinks American carriers are actually competing with each other anymore for the top spot — they've decided the real competition is Dubai and Singapore. Ambitious? Sure. Are either of them there yet? No. But it's interesting that the two guys running America's biggest airlines suddenly want the same trophy.

Qantas made news too — they're retiring the A380 four years early, starting in 2028 instead of 2032. The superjumbo's basically becoming the industry's expensive houseguest nobody wants to keep feeding. Four engines, huge weight, brutal fuel costs — and Qantas has 48 new A350s and 787s inbound to replace it.

But here's the fun part — they also unveiled new business suites for the A321XLR and the 787. Lie-flat seats. On a narrowbody. Which, fine, everyone's doing that now apparently. Qantas is trading in the world's biggest passenger plane for something the size of a regional jet with a fancy front cabin. Bold trade.

Frontier's back with more bad news for its loyal customers, which, at this point, is basically a personality trait for Frontier. They just gutted their elite program — cutting mileage earning up to 90 percent for anyone who doesn't buy up from a basic fare. Elite seat selection, gone unless you pay more. Change fee waivers, gone unless you pay more. Platinum members lose unlimited companion travel entirely.

They are making Diamond status a little easier to reach in 2027, which sounds nice until you realize the benefits attached to it are worth way less than they used to be. It's the airline equivalent of promoting you to manager and then taking away your parking spot. Frontier giving with one hand and picking your pocket with the other — name a more iconic duo.

Last one — this is a weird one. Spirit Airlines, which is going through bankruptcy, is planning to sell a giant data archive to Google as part of a ten million dollar AI deal. We're talking payroll records, tax documents, medical leave files, disciplinary records, and get this — five hundred million Microsoft Teams messages from employees.

The flight attendants' union is fighting it, and honestly, good for them. They probably can't stop the sale outright — bankruptcy court doesn't really care about your feelings — but they've got a real shot at forcing safeguards so Google doesn't just get a raw dump of everyone's private medical history along with the spreadsheets. Wild that "going bankrupt" now comes with a bonus round of "also your HR file gets auctioned off to a tech company."
