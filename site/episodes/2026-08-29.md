Episode Title: United and American Play Chicken Over Routes, Air Canada's Fancy New Jet Flops, and Frontier Guts Its Elites
Episode Summary: United and American both dropped their summer route announcements this week, and things got a little spicy when United's CEO predicted American will eventually get run out of Chicago entirely. Plus, Air Canada's brand-new A321XLR couldn't hack its highest-profile route, Frontier takes another swing at its loyalty program, and Etihad has some explaining to do after a mid-air medical emergency.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Saturday, August 29th. Let's get into it.

So United and American both did their big summer route reveals this week, and it turned into a bit of a flex-off. United announced ten new international destinations, eight of which no other U.S. airline flies at all. Most of them are landing at Newark, which — if you've ever flown through Newark in July — is United basically saying "please, come suffer with the rest of us." Luxembourg, Ibiza, Marseille, all riding in on the new A321XLR.

American countered with seven new routes, but only three of those are actually new cities — Porto, Reykjavik, and Vienna. The other four are just more flights to places American already flies. Still, a solid answer.

But here's the spicier part. United CEO Scott Kirby is out there again predicting American will eventually retreat from Chicago O'Hare completely, saying American's losing something like a billion dollars a year there. His vision afterward? United turns O'Hare into a fortress hub with a thousand daily departures. Kirby's basically saying "get out of my house" without saying it. Whether he's right or just manifesting it, it's not exactly subtle.

Next up, Air Canada just learned an expensive lesson. They put their brand-new A321XLR on Toronto to London Heathrow — one of the most premium routes on their whole map — swapping a widebody for a single-aisle jet. Sounds efficient on paper. They pulled it within weeks. Turns out yanking all that first-class cargo space and premium seating off your flagship transatlantic route doesn't go great, so the 777s and 787s are back. Meanwhile United is doing the smart version of this — starting its own A321XLR domestically, on routes like Dulles to San Diego, before sending it overseas in December. Same plane, wildly different confidence levels.

Frontier also had a week, and not a good one. They're cutting mileage earning by up to 90 percent for anyone who doesn't buy the pricier bundles, stripping free seat selection and change fee waivers from elites who stick with base fares, and Platinum members are losing unlimited companion travel. The silver lining, according to Frontier, is that it's now easier to reach Diamond status. Which is a nice way of saying "we took away the free stuff, but here's a shinier ladder to climb for whatever's left." Classic Frontier.

Not a great week for Etihad either. Two female doctors say a male cabin manager refused their help during a mid-flight medical emergency, with a passenger down in the aisle, citing "protocol." That doesn't sound like protocol. That sounds like a bad call in the moment that's now going to follow the airline around for a while.

And last one — Spirit's bankruptcy has a new subplot. As part of Google's ten million dollar deal to buy up Spirit's old data archive, flight attendants are fighting to keep their own personal records out of it. We're talking payroll, tax files, medical leave, disciplinary records, on top of five hundred million internal messages. The union probably can't stop the sale, but they're pushing hard for safeguards before any of that lands in Google's hands. Fair enough — nobody signs up for their HR file training somebody's AI model.
