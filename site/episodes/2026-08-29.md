Episode Title: Air Canada's A321XLR Face-Plants on Its Big Debut, Kirby Wants Chicago All to Himself, and Frontier Finds New Ways to Take Your Miles
Episode Summary: Air Canada's shiny new narrowbody couldn't hack it on the London route it was built for, Scott Kirby is already measuring the drapes for a United-only O'Hare, and two airline CEOs suddenly want to be mentioned in the same breath as Singapore Airlines. Plus, Frontier's elite program gets the machete treatment, and Spirit's flight attendants are fighting to keep their medical records out of Google's hands.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Saturday, August 29th. Let's get into it.

So first up, Air Canada just had a rough one. They rolled out the brand new A321XLR — this fancy long-range narrowbody that's supposed to be the future of skinny jets flying big routes — and put it on Toronto to London Heathrow. Their flagship transatlantic corridor. The big leagues.

It lasted weeks.

They quietly pulled it and put the widebodies back — the 777s and 787s. Turns out a single-aisle jet, even a really good one, can't match the seat count or the cargo capacity of a widebody on the busiest route in their whole network. Which, yeah, makes sense in hindsight. The XLR is great for thin routes nobody else can justify flying. It is not great for replacing your best-selling flight of the year. Air Canada basically test-drove the future of transatlantic flying and immediately traded it back in.

Next — Scott Kirby is still out here talking about Chicago like it's already his. United's CEO told Crain's that American is losing over a billion dollars a year at O'Hare, and that "economic gravity" is eventually going to force American out entirely. His vision for what comes next? United running about a thousand daily departures out of Chicago and turning it into a fortress hub, like what Delta has in Atlanta.

Look, Kirby's not wrong that the math is ugly for American in Chicago. But he's also the guy who benefits the most from saying it out loud, repeatedly, on the record, to a Chicago business outlet. That's not just an observation. That's a guy trying to talk a competitor into giving up.

Speaking of ambition — funny thing happened this week. Delta's Ed Bastian went on a podcast and said he wants Delta mentioned in the same breath as Singapore Airlines and Emirates. Global elite tier, not just best-in-the-US. And apparently, hours later in Denver, Kirby said basically the same thing to a reporter.

Two rival CEOs, same week, both suddenly comparing themselves to Singapore Airlines. That's not a coincidence, that's a trend. And look, Delta's made real progress — the lounges are genuinely great, the Wi-Fi's free now, the ops are solid. But comparing yourself to Singapore Airlines is a little like a garage band saying they're basically Radiohead. Nice goal. Long way to go.

Okay, Frontier. They just took a machete to their elite program, and not in a subtle way. Mileage earning is getting cut by up to 90 percent for non-cardholders, seat selection and change fee waivers are getting stripped from elites unless you buy up from your fare, and Platinum members are losing unlimited companion travel. The one nice thing they're doing is making Diamond status easier to reach next year — dropping the threshold from 100,000 points to 75,000.

So the pitch is basically: it's easier to get the status, and the status is worth less once you have it. Which, if you've followed Frontier at all the last few years, tracks perfectly. They give with one hand, and the other hand is already in your pocket.

Last one, and it's a weird one. Remember Spirit's bankruptcy sale to Google, the ten million dollar AI data deal? Turns out that archive isn't just old boarding passes — it includes payroll records, tax documents, medical leave files, disciplinary records, on top of 500 million internal Teams messages. Spirit's flight attendant union is fighting to keep employee-specific stuff out of Google's hands before any of it changes ownership.

They probably can't stop the sale itself — bankruptcy sales are bankruptcy sales. But they've got a real shot at forcing some actual privacy protections before Google gets the keys. Which, considering nobody asked these flight attendants if they wanted their sick leave paperwork feeding an AI model, feels like the bare minimum.
