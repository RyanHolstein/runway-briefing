Episode title: Missiles Hit Riyadh Airport, American Goes All-In on Starlink, and Europe's Transatlantic Land Grab Continues
Episode Summary: A Houthi ballistic missile actually hits a parked Saudia jet at Riyadh's main airport, and we get into why that's a scary new chapter in this conflict. Plus, American Airlines doubles down on Starlink for its entire fleet, ten airlines pile into the transatlantic market with 29 new routes, and we revisit the first-class fee fight between American, Delta, and United.

Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Thursday, October 8th. Let's get into it.

So we need to start with Riyadh, because this one's serious. A Houthi ballistic missile attack on King Khalid International Airport actually hit a parked Saudia A320 today. Thankfully empty, nobody on board, but there are now images of a destroyed commercial jet sitting at the gate of one of the busiest airports in the Middle East.

Here's the context you need. The Houthis have been lobbing missiles at Saudi oil infrastructure for years, but in just the past week they've pivoted hard to civilian aviation. Three people killed, dozens hurt across attacks on Abha and King Khalid airports. The US Embassy actually warned citizens about this exact scenario less than 24 hours before it happened.

Think about what King Khalid Airport is. It's the home base for Riyadh Air, Saudi Arabia's splashy new airline that wants to go toe-to-toe with Emirates and Qatar Airways. Hard to sell the world on "come fly through our shiny new hub" when there's a smoking fuselage on the ramp. This is the same playbook Iran ran back in February, hitting Dubai and Bahrain airspace. Regional aviation just got a lot more dangerous, and insurers are absolutely paying attention to that.

Okay, lighter stuff. American Airlines confirmed today it's putting Starlink on its entire mainline fleet, over a thousand jets. That includes the 777s and 787s, not just the Airbus narrowbodies it announced back in May.

And look, this is American admitting its original plan was kind of embarrassing. They announced a Starlink partnership, then said "actually, just half the fleet, the other half keeps the old slow Panasonic wifi." Which, if you're trying to convince people you're a premium airline, is not the flex you think it is. Now United's racing to get Starlink on a thousand planes, Delta's got its own timeline, and even KLM jumped in today, though just for long-haul. Air France, KLM's sister airline, has had this for two years already. Free, fast wifi just became table stakes, and the laggards are going to feel it.

Speaking of premium versus cheap, there's a real fight brewing over first class. United and Delta are both bringing basic-economy-style restrictions into their cheapest first class fares now. No free seat selection, no changes, reduced mileage earning. You pay more for the cabin but still get nickel-and-dimed on the extras.

American hasn't done this yet, and honestly, this is their moment. They don't need some revolutionary new seat. They just need to NOT be annoying, which apparently is a competitive advantage in this industry now. Low bar, but here we are.

Quick one on routes, because there are a lot today. Simple Flying counted 29 new US-Europe routes launching for next summer across ten airlines. Air France alone is adding Nashville and San Diego, while quietly dropping Denver, handing that market straight to United. Icelandair's heading to St. Louis to connect with Southwest. WestJet's launching its first-ever route to South America, Calgary to São Paulo, in December.

The bigger picture: the transatlantic market just keeps getting more crowded, everybody's chasing secondary cities now because the big ones are saturated, and United in particular looks like it's trying to grab every leftover slot before the joint ventures lock things down for good.

Last thing — British Airways finally showed off its new A380 cabins, giving business class the entire upper deck. A hundred and six Club Suites, direct aisle access for everyone, lie-flat beds. Sounds great until you see the fine print: economy loses 88 seats to make room. Nice if you're up front, rough if you're in the back.
