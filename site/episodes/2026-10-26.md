Episode title: Boeing's MAX 10 Finally Certified, United Has a Scary Moment Over Nevada, and Southwest Flirts With Going Widebody
Episode Summary: Boeing finally gets the MAX 10 certified after years in FAA purgatory — we break down why this one actually matters. Plus, a United flight from Vegas to Newark turns back over a hijacking text, Southwest inches closer to buying its first-ever widebody jet, and American and United both just bought their way onto Azul's board.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Monday, October 26th. Let's get into it.

So, Boeing. The MAX 10 just got certified, and honestly, this is bigger than it sounds.

Quick refresher — the MAX 8 and MAX 9 got certified back in 2017, 2018. Then they started falling out of the sky, literally, and the FAA grounded everything until late 2020. After that, the FAA basically put Boeing in timeout and froze all new certifications. The MAX 7 finally got out a little while back, but the MAX 10 — the big one, the one airlines actually want — has been stuck since its first flight over five years ago.

Why does this matter? Because the MAX 10 is the plane that lets airlines like United and Delta compete seat-for-seat with Airbus's A321neo on domestic routes, and that's the most profitable real estate in the industry right now. Every year this thing sat in certification purgatory was a year Airbus ate Boeing's lunch on narrowbody orders. So this isn't just a paperwork win — it's Boeing finally getting a product back that it can actually sell.

Sticking with fleet stuff — Southwest might be about to do something it has never done in 55 years. Reuters is reporting Southwest is leaning toward ordering the Boeing 787 as its first-ever widebody jet.

Think about that. Fifty-five years of nothing but 737s. Same crews, same maintenance, same everything — that's literally Southwest's whole business model. One widebody order means new training, new maintenance contracts, new international infrastructure, basically rebuilding half the airline from scratch. No order's been placed yet, but if this happens, it's the biggest strategic shift at Southwest since, well, ever.

Okay, a scarier one. A United flight from Las Vegas to Newark turned back Saturday night after a flight attendant reportedly saw a passenger texting about hijacking the plane. Pilots locked the cockpit down — which is exactly the protocol you want — but air traffic control was told the passenger apparently had no idea anything was happening around them. So, a weird, murky situation. Glad it ended with the plane just turning around and nobody hurt, but not exactly a relaxing Saturday for anyone on board.

Now, over in Brazil — American just bought its way onto Azul's board. They exercised warrants for about eight percent of the airline and put their alliances chief in the seat. Thing is, United already has a hundred million dollars in Azul and got there first.

Here's what bugs me about this one. American just reported a quarterly loss of 269 million dollars. They made just 111 million dollars in all of 2025, and they basically spent all of it on this Azul stake. So while American's trying to hit its own debt targets at home, it's also racing United for influence over a Brazilian airline's board seat. That's not a great look when your own balance sheet is this tight — but losing South America to United entirely would probably be worse.

Last one, and it's a trend I keep noticing. American's been caught hawking cheap first class upgrades at the gate, minutes before boarding — and selling them to random coach passengers ahead of elites who've been waiting on actual upgrade lists. Meanwhile, United's commercial chief just told investors their new "buy up or lose your perks" fare strategy is working great, and they're expanding it.

See the pattern? Airlines want your loyalty and your credit card spend, but when there's a seat to sell, loyalty takes a back seat to cash in hand. Every. Single. Time.
