Episode title — United's Record Revenue Can't Beat Delta's Math, Lufthansa Invades Columbus, and American's Having a Week
Episode Summary: United posted record revenue but Delta still out-profited them by a mile — we break down why that gap matters. Plus, Lufthansa plants its flag in Columbus, Ohio of all places, American pays flight attendants for a catering disaster while also banning a guy from taking window photos, and a typhoon takes aim at Asian hubs.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Wednesday, October 21st. Let's get into it.

So United dropped Q3 earnings, and on paper it looks great. Record revenue, seventeen point nine billion dollars, premium cabins packed, even Basic Economy is pulling its weight. Sounds like a victory lap, right?

Except Delta reported the same quarter, and here's the thing — Delta made thirty-five percent more profit on basically the same revenue. United's adjusted pre-tax profit actually fell almost nine percent year over year. So United is selling more seats, charging more money, filling more planes... and somehow keeping less of it.

Where's it going? Fuel, mostly, plus rising costs across the board. Delta's margin was nine point four percent. United's was six point nine. That's not a rounding error, that's a real gap. And look, I think United's product is genuinely good right now — Starlink, the new seats, the whole push upmarket. But revenue isn't the scoreboard. Profit is. Scott Kirby's been talking a big game lately, and I do think United eventually closes this gap. Just... not this quarter.

Okay, Lufthansa. They're adding two new U.S. cities for summer twenty twenty-seven, and one of them is genuinely surprising — Columbus, Ohio. First-ever nonstop to Europe for that airport, flying a 787-9 with Lufthansa's new Allegris business class. The other route is Munich to Phoenix.

Why Columbus? Turns out there are something like thirty-eight German companies with operations there, so this isn't charity — there's real corporate travel demand. Plus Ohio's got a subsidy program backing it, which, fine, that's how a lot of these thin routes get off the ground these days. But step back and look at the pattern — Lufthansa, Air France, British Airways, they're all skipping past the usual hubs and going straight for mid-size American cities. Thank the A350 and A321XLR for that. These planes can make secondary markets profitable in a way that just wasn't possible ten years ago.

American's having an interesting week, and not in a good way. First, remember that Heathrow catering meltdown back in February, when they ditched their caterer over reportedly finding a dead rodent in a bread basket? Well, flight attendants who worked those flights and basically did the caterer's job themselves are finally getting paid for it — up to a hundred sixty-nine bucks a trip, for over nine thousand crew members. Good, they earned it.

Then there's this: a passenger on a Rochester to Chicago flight says a flight attendant made him delete a photo of the cabin, and when he asked if he could at least shoot the clouds out the window, she said no. Not boarding photos, not window photos. Just... no photos, apparently, period. Look, I get not wanting strangers in the background of someone's picture. But banning window shots is just not a real policy, it's one flight attendant having a moment. American hasn't confirmed any new photo rule, so my guess is this gets walked back fast.

Quick hits before we go. Typhoon Champi is barreling toward Taiwan and mainland China right now, being called the strongest storm of the year, and it may swing toward southern Japan next — expect preemptive cancellations across those hubs in the next few days if you're flying through there.

And an American 787 blew a tire landing at O'Hare after the crew reported a brake issue. FAA's investigating, nobody hurt, but that's twice this month we've talked about American having a rough landing.
