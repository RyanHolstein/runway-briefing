Episode title: Delta's Riyadh Bet Gets Delayed Five Months, American Recommits to LAX, and a Lufthansa 747 Nearly Kisses the Runway at LAX
Episode Summary: Delta's long-awaited Riyadh launch slips all the way to March 2027 after another round of attacks on the airport. Plus, American makes a bold new play to rebuild LAX into its Pacific gateway, a United 767 makes an engine-related diversion over the North Atlantic, and a drunk passenger gives an Atlanta gate agent a very bad Monday.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Monday, October 12th. Let's get into it.

So, Delta's Riyadh flight. We've been tracking this all week — missiles hitting the airport, Ed Bastian telling reporters "safety will make the call." Well, safety made the call.

Delta announced today they're pushing the launch back to March 2027. That's not a tweak, that's a five-month delay.

This was supposed to kick off October 23rd, with a full week of daily flights before settling into three times a week. All gone now. The airport's still closed through at least this Saturday after attacks that killed sixteen people and wounded over three hundred — and there are photos circulating of a Saudia jet with serious damage sitting on the ramp.

Here's the thing though — Delta isn't walking away. They're calling Saudi Arabia a long-term market, still planning to lean on their partnership with Riyadh Air. But five months is a long time in a region that's currently this unstable. This route was supposed to be Delta's big flex into the Middle East, a market Emirates and Qatar have basically owned for two decades. Instead, it's turning into a lesson in how fast geopolitics can wreck a network plan.

Okay, switching gears. American's talking big again. Cranky Flier sat down with their chief commercial officer, Nat Pieper, and got a real headline out of it — American is making LAX its Pacific gateway again.

Quick history here — American built LAX into a real gateway to Asia before the pandemic, then gutted it when COVID hit. Now they want it back, as part of what Nat calls a push to be "the premier global airline based in the US." Bold statement for an airline that just killed first class on its biggest plane last week.

Here's why it's hard. United has owned the Pacific out of San Francisco for literally decades. Delta's doing something similar out of Seattle with Alaska's help. If American wants in on that fight, good intentions don't cut it — you need widebodies, slots, and a lot of patience. We'll see if they actually follow through.

Couple of scary-looking moments in the sky worth a quick mention. A United 767 flying Dulles to Amsterdam had to divert into St. John's, Newfoundland overnight after what looks like an engine shutdown. Landed safely, everyone's fine, but that's a long way to glide on one engine over the North Atlantic.

And out at LAX, a Lufthansa 747 touched down on runway 24R, then aborted the landing right after touchdown — nose pitched up, tail looking uncomfortably close to the pavement. No tail strike, plane went around and landed fine fifteen minutes later. Dramatic video, less dramatic outcome. Pilots probably just didn't like how much runway they had left to stop. That's exactly what a go-around is for.

Quick weird one for you. Frontier and Southwest both allow carry-ons up to 24 by 16 by 10 inches. The new TSA CT scanners being installed nationwide have tunnel openings of about 24.5 by 16.5 inches.

So do the math — airlines are selling you a bag sized right up to the edge of what fits, and TSA's shiny new scanner gives you literally half an inch of wiggle room. I don't think anyone coordinated on this. It's just a reminder that nobody in this industry is talking to each other.

And finally, because every briefing needs one of these — a Delta passenger in Atlanta got pulled off her flight to Rome for being drunk at the gate, and did not take it well. There's video of her leaning over the counter screaming at gate agents who, credit to them, look completely unbothered. Rome will still be there next week. Maybe sober up first.
