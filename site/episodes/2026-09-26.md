Episode title: Pueblo's $7 Million Empty Flight, Air Baltic Hides the Sick Bags, and American Fights a Refund It Clearly Owes
Episode Summary: A tiny Colorado airport gets a taxpayer-funded flight nobody's flying, Air Baltic's bankruptcy cost-cutting gets weirdly specific, and American Airlines goes to court over an $8,000 refund. Plus Avelo picks a fight at Dallas's newest airport, and a quick update on Spirit's fire sale.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Saturday, September 26th. Let's get into it.

So first up, this one made me laugh and also kind of want to scream into a pillow. There's a federal program called Essential Air Service — it was supposed to be temporary, a stopgap from airline deregulation back in the seventies to keep small towns connected. That was 1978. It is still here. Forty-eight years later, still "temporary," and it now costs about 680 million dollars a year.

Case in point: Pueblo, Colorado is bringing back a 6 a.m. flight to Denver. Taxpayers are subsidizing it to the tune of 6.84 million dollars a year. The airline's own bid predicts it'll fly just 22 percent full. And here's the kicker — Colorado Springs airport is 45 minutes away by car, and Denver itself is basically drivable.

So we're paying millions of dollars a year to fly mostly empty planes to a city you could just... drive to. Gary Leff over at View From the Wing has been all over this program for years, and honestly the math never gets less ridiculous. This is the definition of a government program nobody has the political nerve to kill, so it just quietly grows forever.

Okay, next. Air Baltic filed for Chapter 11 last week, and now the cost-cutting memo has leaked, and it is bleak. They're pulling sick bags out of the seat-back pockets. Not eliminating them — just making you ask for one, from a stash of 50 in the back of the plane. Cosmetic tissues in the bathroom, gone. Disinfectant gel, gone. Trash bags in the cockpit — also apparently a luxury now, also gone.

And it's not just passenger stuff. Crew are losing business class perks between flights, and now have to pick up cabin trash themselves because cleaning crews are getting cut at some outstations. Look, when you're trying to unlock 350 million euros in bankruptcy financing, I get it, every line item matters. But there's something almost poetic about an airline in Chapter 11 rationing sick bags. If this doesn't turn around, the next memo is probably "please bring your own barf bag from home."

Next, American Airlines. A couple named Steven and Pamela Taylor bought an 8,166 dollar business class trip, Nashville to Edinburgh. A delay rerouted them through Boston, and they ended up crossing the Atlantic in premium economy on British Airways instead. Reasonable person thinks: okay, refund me the difference. American offered them under 500 dollars each in expiring trip credits.

They sued. Won 7,158 dollars. American is now appealing, arguing that under the Montreal Convention it actually owes them nothing. Which — they downgraded these people and are in court arguing they don't owe a refund for the seat they didn't get. This is exactly the kind of thing that makes people distrust airline loyalty in general. If you can get bumped from business to premium economy and the airline's legal position is "tough luck," that's not a fare rule, that's just bad faith with paperwork.

Quick one — Avelo is expanding at McKinney National, the brand new third airport for Dallas, way out in the northeast suburbs. Four more routes starting in December, including a new one to Denver. Nobody else has announced service there yet, so Avelo's making a bet that a totally untested airport, 35 miles from both DFW and Love Field, can support standalone traffic. Bold. We'll see.

And a quick update on something from yesterday — Spirit's final aircraft sale, 27 planes for 668 million dollars, has now been formally cleared. That's basically the last chapter of Spirit as we knew it, selling off its own bones to fund the bankruptcy.
