Episode title: NTSB's Grim Amazon Update, United Cancels Its Own 9/11 Flypast, and Hyatt Ditches American for Delta
Episode Summary: The NTSB drops new cockpit voice data from Sunday's fatal Miami crash, and it's about as bad as you'd expect. United quietly scraps a bizarre 9/11 tribute flight over Denver, Hyatt swaps American for Delta as its loyalty partner, and Frontier finally heads to South America. Plus, Air Canada's shiny new A321XLR already flopped on its biggest route.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Thursday, September 10th. Let's get into it.

So, remember the Amazon Prime Air 767 that crashed in Miami on Sunday? We've been following this all week, and the NTSB just released cockpit voice recorder data, and it's rough.

Seven cockpit warnings. In the final 25 seconds. Two "sink rate" alerts, five "too low terrain" alerts. And separately, one of the pilots reportedly told the other they were flying too fast on approach.

So this isn't some mystery mechanical failure. This looks like a bad approach that kept getting flown anyway, straight through a wall of alarms designed to make that exact thing impossible to ignore. The NTSB won't formally assign cause for a while, but from what's public, this is looking like a chain of missed calls, not one broken part. Which is honestly scarier, because that means it could happen again, on any 767, with any crew, if the same pressure builds at the same moment.

Okay, next one — this is just... a lot. United had planned a 9/11 tribute flight for tomorrow's 25th anniversary. A Boeing 777, flying low over downtown Denver, flanked by four F-16 fighter jets.

Read that back. A big commercial jet, flying low, with fighter jets beside it, timed to the exact minute Flight 175 hit the South Tower. United called it a private employee event. Denver officials found out and immediately started prepping 911 dispatchers for a flood of panicked calls, because — shocker — people who see a jumbo jet buzzed by fighter jets tend to assume the worst.

United's now scrapped the whole thing. I get the intent, honoring the anniversary and the crews. But somebody in that planning meeting needed to ask, "hey, will this look exactly like the thing we're trying to commemorate?" Sometimes the flashy idea just isn't the good one.

Speaking of relationships ending — American and Hyatt are done. After seven years of linked AAdvantage and World of Hyatt earning, both sides "mutually decided" to end their enhanced partnership.

And get this, a few hours later, Delta and Hyatt announced they're teaming up, letting SkyMiles and World of Hyatt members double-dip on points for the same spending, flights and hotel stays both.

So basically the second American let go, Delta was standing right there to catch it. This is becoming a pattern — we've talked about Delta quietly picking up routes American's dropped, now it's picking up partnerships too. If you're a Hyatt loyalist who built status around American flights, you've got until November 15th to link accounts before all this shifts over.

Good news if you're a Frontier flyer with a passport — the airline just announced its first-ever flights to South America. Three new routes, which is a real milestone since Frontier's never flown south of the Caribbean before.

Why now? Basically, Spirit's gone. Frontier's been scooping up the ultra-low-cost market share Spirit left behind, and their seat count is up 11% this year to an all-time high. When a competitor disappears, someone else eats the leftovers, and right now that's Frontier.

The real test is whether Frontier's whole bare-fare model, the one that works great on a two-hour hop to Cancun, translates to a five or six-hour flight where people actually want legroom and maybe a snack that isn't $9. We'll see.

Last one, a quick update on something we've been tracking. Air Canada's brand-new A321XLR, the plane meant to replace widebodies on thin long-haul routes, just quietly got pulled off its flagship transatlantic route. Weeks after launch.

This was supposed to be the proof-of-concept, showing you don't need a widebody on a premium long-haul route if you can fill every seat on a single-aisle jet instead. Instead, it flopped almost immediately.

That matters beyond Air Canada. Airlines from American to Iberia have XLRs on order specifically to open up thin routes widebodies can't justify. If Air Canada can't make it work on their best route, that's a warning sign for everybody else betting on this plane.
