Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Wednesday, September 30th. Let's get into it.

So, we gotta start with the scary one. A Flydubai 737 MAX flying from Dubai to Tel Aviv this morning had what officials are calling a major disaster averted. A violent fight broke out in the cockpit — Israeli intelligence sources believe the first officer deliberately stabbed the captain and tried to crash the plane.

The jet dropped fifteen thousand feet in seconds. Fifteen thousand. Passengers jumped in to help subdue the guy, and here's the wild part — there happened to be two off-duty pilots deadheading on that flight as passengers, and they took over the controls and landed the thing safely in Tabuk, Saudi Arabia. Everyone survived.

And can we talk about the axe thing for a second? Every report out there has people speculating about how a pilot smuggled a knife or an axe past security. He didn't need to. There's already a crash axe bolted into basically every cockpit on Earth, and pilots get served meals up there with actual metal cutlery. Investigators have reportedly already ruled out the axe being smuggled aboard — it was the plane's own emergency tool. This is going to be a hijacking and airline-security story for months, but today, the headline is just: everybody lived, because a bunch of strangers and two off-duty pilots refused to let a 737 become a missile.

Okay, from terrifying to just... petty. Delta's CEO, Ed Bastian, apparently told employees at a company event why Delta skipped Starlink, and it's not about technology. It's about Elon. Quote, reportedly: "We do not want to be with Elon Musk. Trust me. It's all true what they say about him."

Which, sure, maybe he's not wrong about Musk. But here's the problem — United has already got Starlink fleetwide, American, Southwest, and Alaska are racing to install it, and Delta's plan B is Amazon's satellite internet, which doesn't even exist yet. It's targeting 2028, on less than forty percent of Delta's planes. So Delta passengers get to enjoy knowing their CEO has personal integrity while their Wi-Fi still buffers a Netflix show for ten minutes. Musk, naturally, fired back on X saying Bastian will lose his job over this. I wouldn't bet on it, but it's certainly a look for an airline that built its whole brand on being the premium option.

Next — American's doing something kind of funny with its weirdest plane. Remember the A321T? The one built just for JFK to LA and San Francisco with a tiny 102 seats and actual first-class suites? American's ripping those interiors out and turning them into regular narrowbodies with up to 196 seats.

It's not really about that one jet — it's about American walking away from having a small, special premium sub-fleet and instead spreading premium seats across the whole narrowbody operation. Basically: fewer boutique products, more standardized density. Good for American's bottom line. Less great if you loved that transcon first class experience — RIP, kind of.

Quick update on something we've been following — Alaska, a day after dropping its huge new suites-and-lounges announcement, is now confirmed to be joining American's transatlantic and transpacific joint ventures, and teasing fifteen new intercontinental routes out of Seattle by 2030. That's Alaska trying to play in the same league as Delta, United, and American overnight instead of over a decade. The catch — once you're inside a joint venture, you're not really an independent competitor on pricing anymore, you're coordinating schedules and fares with partners like British Airways and Japan Airlines. Fewer rivals, in theory more flights. We'll see if that math holds.

And last thing, because I can't not mention it — TSA is banning its document checkers from sitting down. The agency says standing makes checkpoints, quote, more welcoming. The union is furious, obviously. I don't know what's more aviation-brained than solving a hospitality problem by making federal employees stand for eight hours straight, but here we are.
