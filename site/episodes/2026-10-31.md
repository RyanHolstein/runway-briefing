Episode title: Lufthansa Tragedy in Bengaluru, the FTC Comes for Fare Pricing, and a Tiramisu That Could Get You Drug Tested
Episode Summary: A somber start today — a Lufthansa crew member died in a hotel pool electrocution in India, and we talk about why these incidents keep happening. Then: the FTC makes the personalized pricing investigation official, American pilots get warned off a boozy dessert, Amadeus admits a three-year data breach hit half the industry, and layoffs keep piling up from Anchorage to Riga.

Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Saturday, October 31st. Let's get into it.

We've got to start with something sad. A Lufthansa crew member died in India this week, and it's worth talking about why.

Alexander Theiss, 57, was on a layover in Bengaluru, staying at the Conrad Hotel. Friday evening, he and another guest — a Slovakian IBM project manager named Juraj Horvat — were in the rooftop pool when something went wrong electrically. Police are calling it a suspected electrical leakage, possibly from the underwater lighting. Two other people were hurt trying to help them, including a hotel worker who ran in to pull them out.

Here's the thing — this isn't some freak one-off. Underwater lighting faults in hotel pools have caused fatal electrocutions before, in India and elsewhere, usually because grounding and wiring inspections on these systems just don't get the scrutiny they should. The hotel's already closed the pool for, quote, "maintenance." For a five-star property that markets a rooftop pool as a selling point, that's a pretty serious safety gap. My condolences to both families — genuinely awful story to start a Friday night layover.

Okay. Sticking with something we talked about yesterday — remember the story about airlines charging solo travelers more than people booking with a friend? That theme just got a lot more official. The FTC has ordered Delta, United, and JetBlue to explain exactly how they price tickets, as part of a bigger inquiry into personalized pricing across nine major companies, not just airlines.

The idea is simple and a little unsettling: you see three hundred bucks for a flight, your buddy down the street sees two-twenty, same seat, same day, because the algorithm knows something about you it doesn't know about him. Airlines for America, the industry's lobbying group, says flatly they don't do this. Every airline always says that. Delta's already been burned once this year for using an AI pricing tool, so forgive me if I'm skeptical.

Alright, we need a palate cleanser after that. American Airlines pilots in Phoenix got an actual memo this week telling them: don't eat the tiramisu. Their catering provider put alcohol in it, uncooked, so nobody knows exactly how much booze is in a serving. The pilots' union can't tell them whether eating a dessert could get them flagged on a random alcohol test.

Think about that for a second. A crew meal nearly turned into a DOT testing incident. The union's advice is basically: take the incomplete meal, skip dessert, don't let schedule pressure talk you into eating the tiramisu. Somewhere a catering exec is having a very bad Friday.

Speaking of bad weeks — Cathay Pacific confirmed it's the latest airline caught up in a massive data breach, and this one's a doozy. The breach is inside Amadeus, the company that basically runs the booking, check-in, and operations plumbing for over 130 airlines worldwide. It ran undetected for three years — August 2023 to September this year. Cathay says 53,000 bookings were exposed. Japan Airlines said 92,000. ANA, Korean Air, and Condor have all confirmed hits too.

No payment data taken, apparently, just names, booking references, sometimes passport and frequent flyer numbers. But three years is the part that should worry everyone. When the plumbing that runs half the industry gets compromised and nobody notices for three years, that's not a one-airline problem, that's an infrastructure problem.

Couple quick ones before we go. Alaska Airlines is cutting 200 jobs, blaming fuel costs and what they're calling geopolitical challenges — fits right into that fuel-price squeeze we've been tracking all week with WestJet and others. AirBaltic's cutting around 300 positions too, pilots and cabin crew, as the bankrupt carrier keeps restructuring.

And United is pulling out of Tulum entirely come January. Remember that brand-new airport that opened with big promises back in 2024? US service there is now down more than fifty percent in two years. Turns out travelers just preferred flying into Cancun and driving. Another one for the pile of shiny new airports nobody asked for.
