Weekly Deep Dive: Boeing's Impossible Week — Winning Big While Falling Behind

Episode Summary: Boeing landed a massive Turkish Airlines order and a $36 billion week, but the 737 MAX 10 still can't get FAA sign-off, and the China mega-deal is slipping away for good. Ryan and Hope dig into why airlines keep buying a jet that isn't even certified yet, how Airbus's own quality crisis is handing Boeing a rare opening, and what losing China really costs Boeing over the next decade.

RYAN: Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing Weekly. I'm Ryan, and I've got Hope with me.

HOPE: Hey! So this week we're digging into the weirdest kind of good news Boeing's had in years — they're raking in orders, but the plane everybody actually wants still can't fly commercially. How does that even work?

RYAN: Right, this is the story of the week for me. Turkish Airlines orders up to 150 MAX jets. Boeing pulls in something like 36 billion dollars in commitments over just a few days. And at the exact same time, the FAA is sitting on a software issue that could push MAX 10 certification even further out.

HOPE: Which, just to be clear, the MAX 10 has already been waiting for certification since... what, 2019?

RYAN: The program launched in 2019, yeah. Originally Boeing was targeting certification by 2020, then it became 2023, then 2024, and now we're deep into 2026 and there's still no hard date. This is the longest a Boeing narrowbody variant has ever taken to get certified in the company's history.

HOPE: So walk me through what's actually broken right now. Is this a new problem or the same problem we've been hearing about?

RYAN: New wrinkle, same root cause in a sense. The FAA is reviewing a software issue tied to the flight control system. I don't have every technical detail, but The Air Current reported this could delay things further, and given how the MAX has gone, "further" from the FAA basically means indefinite until proven otherwise.

HOPE: And this is separate from MCAS, the thing that grounded the whole fleet back in 2019?

RYAN: Separate system, same underlying tension. The FAA is scrutinizing everything on this jet now with a level of paranoia that just didn't exist before two 737 MAX 8 crashes killed 346 people. Every software change, every system interaction, gets torn apart. That's actually appropriate. But it also means Boeing doesn't get the benefit of the doubt anymore.

HOPE: Okay, so here's my dumb question. If the plane's not certified, why is Turkish Airlines ordering 150 of them right now? Wouldn't you wait until it's actually approved to fly passengers?

RYAN: It's not a dumb question, it's actually the whole story. Airlines order years ahead of delivery. Turkish isn't expecting these jets tomorrow. They're planning fleet composition for the early 2030s. By the time these deliver, certification will presumably be sorted out.

HOPE: Presumably.

RYAN: Presumably. But also, think about what the alternative is. If you're an airline that needs narrowbody jets in the 2030 to 2035 window, your choices are basically Boeing or Airbus. There's no third option that matters at scale.

HOPE: Right, it's a duopoly. Everyone always says that.

RYAN: And Airbus's order book for the A321neo — which is the direct competitor to the MAX 10 — is out to like 2032, 2033 for some customers. If Turkish wants jets sooner than that, or wants leverage in pricing, or just doesn't want to put all their eggs in one basket, Boeing's the move.

HOPE: So it's less "we love Boeing right now" and more "we need slots, and Airbus is fully booked."

RYAN: Exactly. And I'd add — Turkish has ordered from both manufacturers historically. They're not some Boeing loyalist airline. This is pure fleet math. They're growing aggressively, they want to be a top-five global carrier by traffic, and they need airplanes. Any airplanes.

HOPE: Okay, that actually makes sense. It's like buying a house before it's finished being built, because you know the housing market's tight and if you wait, you'll be renting for three extra years.

RYAN: That's a good comparison actually. And here's the thing that makes it even more interesting — Boeing needed this win badly. Not just for the revenue, for the narrative. After years of every headline being about doors falling off and whistleblowers and production halts, having an airline commit 150 jets sends a signal to the market that says, hey, the airlines still trust us long-term.

HOPE: Does the stock market agree with that read? Because I saw the stock actually fell this week.

RYAN: Yeah, and that's the tell, right? You'd think a 36 billion dollar order week would send the stock up. Instead it dropped, because investors are looking past the bookings and straight at the delivery problem. Orders don't pay the bills. Deliveries do.

HOPE: Can you explain that part for people who aren't finance nerds? Why does a company get paid mostly on delivery instead of when the order's placed?

RYAN: So when an airline places an order, there's usually a deposit, but the bulk of the payment happens on delivery, when the airline actually takes possession of the jet. Boeing's entire cash flow model depends on getting airplanes out the door and into airline hands. An order sitting on the books for a jet that can't be certified is basically a promise with no cash behind it yet.

HOPE: So Boeing's balance sheet doesn't care how many people want the MAX 10. It cares how many they can actually hand over the keys to.

RYAN: Right. And Boeing already has a backlog of something like 6,000-plus unfilled orders across the MAX family. The problem has never been demand. It's been throughput — building the planes, certifying the planes, and getting them out the factory door without another door plug flying off mid-flight.

HOPE: Okay, so let's talk about the other half of this story, because I think this is where it gets genuinely fascinating — Airbus's own mess with the A321neo.

RYAN: Yes. This is the part that I think doesn't get enough attention, because it's such a gift to Boeing, and Boeing might not even be able to take advantage of it.

HOPE: Break it down. What's actually wrong with the A321neo?

RYAN: So Simple Flying and others have reported a fuselage coating defect — this is like a corrosion protection issue — that's potentially affecting more than 500 A321neo aircraft already delivered or in production. Airbus says the planes are safe to fly, which, fine, I believe them on immediate safety. But this is a manufacturing quality problem at a scale that's honestly shocking for a program that's supposed to be Airbus's crown jewel.

HOPE: Five hundred planes. That's not a small recall, that's like a decent chunk of the entire fleet.

RYAN: It's a meaningful percentage, yeah. And remember, the A321neo — especially the XLR variant — is the plane every airline wants right now. It's the one doing transatlantic routes with lie-flat seats, the one United just put onto that brutal Air Canada route we talked about a few days ago. Demand for this jet is through the roof.

HOPE: So if there's a coating defect that needs to be fixed, does that mean Airbus has to slow down production, pull planes out of the delivery stream, go back and rework them?

RYAN: That's exactly the fear. Airbus is having to revive dormant production capacity just to keep up, and now they've got this quality issue layered on top. It's eerily similar to what Boeing went through with the 787 fuselage shimming issues a few years back, where Boeing basically had to pause 787 deliveries for over a year while they fixed structural gaps that were too small to see but big enough to matter for long-term airframe life.

HOPE: Wait, so Airbus might be about to have its own Boeing moment.

RYAN: Potentially, yeah. And I want to be careful here, because Airbus has had a real quality reputation advantage over Boeing for the last five or six years. Every time Boeing had a headline disaster, airlines quietly shifted more of their future orders toward Airbus. If it turns out Airbus has its own systemic quality issue, that reputational gap narrows, or at least it should.

HOPE: Should. But does it actually change anything for airlines making decisions right now?

RYAN: That's the real question, and I think the honest answer is: not much, not yet. Airlines don't switch manufacturers on a dime. Pilot training, maintenance infrastructure, spare parts inventory — all of that is built around whichever jet you already fly. American Airlines isn't suddenly going to cancel A321neo orders and go all-Boeing because of a coating defect.

HOPE: So this is more of a long-term crack in the narrative than something that immediately moves market share.

RYAN: Right, but here's where it gets interesting for Boeing specifically. If Airbus has to slow A321neo production to fix these jets, that extends Airbus's already-long backlog even further. And for any airline that was on the fence, weighing "do I wait years longer for an Airbus slot or take a Boeing slot sooner," this tips the math slightly more toward Boeing.

HOPE: Which loops right back to the Turkish order.

RYAN: Exactly. Turkish looked at exactly this calculation. Airbus is slower, possibly getting slower, Boeing has capacity and is desperate for the business. It's not that Turkish suddenly trusts Boeing's engineering more. It's that the alternative got less attractive at the same moment Boeing got more available.

HOPE: Okay but here's the thing I keep coming back to — Boeing has this open window, this opportunity, but they can't actually capitalize on it if the MAX 10 isn't certified. Isn't that the whole tragedy of this story?

RYAN: That is the whole tragedy, yeah. Boeing has maybe the best competitive opening it's had against Airbus in a decade, and the one product that would let them fully exploit it — the MAX 10, which is the direct head-to-head answer to the A321neo — is stuck in FAA review purgatory.

HOPE: It's like getting handed a wide-open fast break and your shoes are untied.

RYAN: That's a great way to put it. And I'd extend that image, because the MAX 7 — the smallest variant — is stuck in the same certification queue. So Boeing's entire MAX family expansion, top and bottom of the lineup, is bottlenecked while the MAX 8 keeps chugging along as basically the only variant fully cleared.

HOPE: So practically speaking, if I'm an airline planning executive, what am I actually able to buy from Boeing today versus what am I ordering on faith?

RYAN: You can order and get delivery of the MAX 8 today, that's the workhorse and it's certified and flying everywhere. The MAX 10 and MAX 7, you're ordering on faith that certification eventually happens, with your delivery slot years out anyway so it usually works out — but every year of delay is a year Boeing's not earning revenue on jets it's already built the tooling for.

HOPE: Are there actually built MAX 10s sitting around waiting?

RYAN: There have been reports over the years of stored, undelivered MAX jets parked at places like Moses Lake in Washington, waiting on certification and customer acceptance. It's not quite as dramatic right now as it was in 2020, but the pattern of building ahead of certification is a real financial drag — you're paying to store inventory that isn't generating cash.

HOPE: This feels like the story of Boeing's whole decade honestly — brilliant sales team, followed by an engineering and regulatory apparatus that just can't keep pace.

RYAN: I'd push back slightly, because I don't think it's really the sales team being brilliant so much as it's a duopoly market where buyers have very limited choices. Boeing doesn't need brilliant salesmanship when the alternative has an eight-year backlog and its own defect scandal.

HOPE: Fair. Okay, let's get into China, because I think this is actually the biggest number in the whole story and it's the one getting the least attention.

RYAN: This is huge, and I think it's underappreciated. Boeing has reportedly been unlikely to land a Chinese mega-order — we're talking over 200 jets — and even the existing roughly 200 jet deal that was already on the books is now genuinely in jeopardy.

HOPE: Remind people why China even matters this much to Boeing specifically.

RYAN: So historically, China has been projected to be one of the largest aviation markets in the world over the next twenty years — some forecasts put it as eventually surpassing the US as the single largest domestic aviation market on Earth. Boeing built entire strategic plans around capturing a huge chunk of that growth. They even built a 737 completion center in Zhoushan specifically to finish MAX jets for Chinese airlines.

HOPE: And how'd that go?

RYAN: Well, China grounded the MAX before the US did back in 2019, and it was actually one of the last major markets to lift that grounding, well into 2023. And even after lifting it, Chinese airlines have been slow-walking new MAX deliveries and orders, partly for genuine domestic reasons, and partly, everyone assumes, for geopolitical ones.

HOPE: So is this a safety thing at this point, or is this just trade war leverage?

RYAN: It's almost entirely geopolitics at this point, I'd argue. China has its own aviation ambitions with the COMAC C919, which is basically China's answer to the A320 and MAX. Every jet China doesn't buy from Boeing is a jet that either goes to Airbus or, eventually, goes to COMAC as that program scales.

HOPE: Is COMAC actually a real threat yet, though? Or is that more of a long-term thing?

RYAN: Long-term for now, but China is subsidizing it aggressively and mandating domestic carriers take delivery of it. It's not competitive globally yet, deliveries have been slow and the aircraft still relies on a lot of Western components, but as a strategic tool, it gives China leverage. They can credibly say "we don't need Boeing" even if that's only half true today.

HOPE: So when you say the mega-order and even the existing 200-jet deal are in jeopardy, is that Boeing losing the business to Airbus, or China just not buying period?

RYAN: Bit of both, honestly. Some of that demand probably shifts to Airbus, who's been steadily growing its footprint in China with a final assembly line in Tianjin. But a good chunk of it might just not materialize as Boeing orders at all — deferred, replaced by COMAC long-term, or just held as a bargaining chip in broader US-China trade tension.

HOPE: How big a deal is this dollar-wise? Like, put a number on what Boeing's actually losing here.

RYAN: If you're talking a 200-plus jet deal at list prices, even with the usual airline discounts, you're talking tens of billions of dollars in potential revenue over the life of that order. And more importantly, it's the loss of a foothold in what was supposed to be Boeing's single biggest growth market for the next two decades.

HOPE: Is this permanent, though? Like, could this flip back if US-China relations thaw even a little?

RYAN: I think it could partially recover, but I don't think it fully comes back to where projections had it five years ago. Every year China buys Airbus instead of Boeing, Airbus builds more infrastructure, more pilot training pipelines, more maintenance relationships in China. That stickiness compounds. Even if the politics improve, Boeing's starting from further behind each year this drags on.

HOPE: So basically Boeing is trading a market they might be permanently losing for orders like Turkish Airlines that they can win right now.

RYAN: That's a really good way to frame it. Boeing's playbook this year has essentially been: can't count on China, so go max aggressive everywhere else — Middle East carriers, Turkish, whoever's expanding and needs jets sooner than Airbus can deliver them.

HOPE: Does that strategy actually work as a long-term substitute for China, though? Or is it just filling a hole with smaller buckets of sand?

RYAN: I lean toward it being a real but incomplete substitute. The Gulf carriers and Turkish and Indian carriers like IndiGo and Air India are genuinely some of the fastest-growing airlines in the world right now. It's not nothing. But none of them individually replace the scale of what a fully open Chinese market would have meant.

HOPE: Okay, so if I'm trying to sum up where this leaves Boeing heading into next year — what's the headline?

RYAN: I think the headline is: Boeing has more demand than it can currently supply, which sounds like a good problem, except the constraint isn't factory capacity anymore so much as it's regulatory trust. The FAA is not going to rush the MAX 10 or MAX 7 through just because Boeing has a strong order book. If anything, the stronger the order book, the more pressure there is on the FAA to prove it isn't rubber-stamping anything.

HOPE: So more orders almost slows down certification, in a weird way, because now there's more scrutiny.

RYAN: There's an argument for that, yeah. The FAA knows every eye in the industry is watching how they handle this after 2019. They're not going to be the ones blamed for letting political or commercial pressure push through a plane with unresolved software questions.

HOPE: If you had to bet, when does the MAX 10 actually get certified?

RYAN: I'd guess sometime in 2027, possibly later. I've been burned before betting on Boeing certification timelines, so take that with a grain of salt. But given this is now a software review on top of years of other reviews, I don't see this wrapping up cleanly before mid-2027 at the earliest.

HOPE: And what about American Airlines? I saw that headline about them being the last legacy carrier without a next-gen widebody commitment, and Boeing making a move there.

RYAN: That's actually a separate but related thread — American's been stuck without a widebody order while United and Delta have both locked in their long-haul future fleets. Boeing wants American in the 787 or 777X camp badly, partly because American's fleet decisions ripple out and influence smaller carriers, and partly because every widebody order Boeing wins is one Airbus doesn't get with the A350.

HOPE: It really does feel like every single one of these stories connects back to the same fight — Boeing trying to claw back position against Airbus on every single front at once.

RYAN: That's the whole story of this era of the industry. It's not really about any one airplane. It's Boeing trying to stabilize on five fronts simultaneously — narrowbody certification, widebody sales, China, quality control, and the FAA relationship — while Airbus deals with its own quality mess and just tries not to blow its lead.

HOPE: And the wildcard in all of it is that neither company can really lose this fight badly enough for the other one to run away with the market, because they're both too systemically important.

RYAN: Right, that's the safety net for both of them. Governments aren't going to let Boeing fail outright, and Airbus is too dominant to let a coating defect actually threaten its position. So this whole rivalry just keeps grinding forward, messy as it is.

HOPE: Which I guess is either comforting or terrifying depending on how you look at it.

RYAN: A little of both, honestly.
