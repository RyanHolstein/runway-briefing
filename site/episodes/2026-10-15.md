Episode title: American's Network Chief Exits, Saudi Arabia Evacuates $4 Billion in Jets, and Ryanair's $9 Flight Sale
Episode Summary: American's network boss is out as the airline tries to walk back years of retreat — and we talk fleet with the exec who's replacing his vision. Plus, Saudi airlines scramble to evacuate billions in aircraft from Riyadh, Southwest flirts with widebodies, and United's stuck holding hundreds of lie-flat seats with nowhere to put them.
Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Thursday, October 15th. Let's get into it.

So, American Airlines. Network chief Brian Znotins is reportedly on his way out, and normally that wouldn't be headline news. But this one's interesting because of why he's leaving.

Znotins was the guy who oversaw American's retreat from LA to Asia flying, and basically bet everything on growing domestic instead. His new boss, CCO Nat Pieper, wants the opposite — a real global network that actually competes instead of retreating. So Pieper's cleaning house.

Here's the thing though — View from the Wing made a good point today. Don't just blame Znotins. He built the network his bosses told him to build. The CEO and the board tolerated years of a weaker product and a thinner international footprint. Firing the network guy doesn't fix that if the people above him don't change direction too.

And speaking of fleet direction — Cranky Flier sat down with Pieper this week, and the guy is a total fleet nerd. Back at Northwest, he literally bought four corroded old DC-10s from Thai Airways just to hit capacity targets. Now he's telling Cranky the A330 might be the perfect Atlantic airplane. So if American's rebuilding its international network, don't be shocked if the A330 and A350 do a lot of the heavy lifting.

Okay, update on something we've been following all week — the Houthi attacks on Riyadh's airport. Saudi airlines are now racing to evacuate their planes.

The Wall Street Journal says the number of aircraft parked at King Khalid International dropped from 134 down to 38 in less than a week. That's something like four billion dollars worth of planes getting moved out, because leaving them parked at an airport that keeps getting hit is obviously not great for your insurance premium, or your fleet.

One Saudia A320 has already been destroyed, and at least six other aircraft are damaged. Remember, this is supposed to be the year Saudi Arabia becomes the next global aviation hub, with Riyadh Air launching and everybody pouring money into new routes. Instead, the priority right now isn't bringing planes in. It's getting them out.

In happier news — well, happier if you like cheap flights — Ryanair just announced 27 new routes, with fares starting at nine dollars.

This is just Ryanair being Ryanair. Nobody does network growth like these guys — blanket Europe with new routes and let the ancillary fees do the rest. You're not paying nine bucks because Ryanair loves you. You're paying nine bucks because they know you'll pay for the seat selection, the bag, and priority boarding anyway.

It works, though. Ryanair's doing something like three and a half thousand flights a day. That nine-dollar fare is basically a loss leader for an airline that makes its real money everywhere else in the booking flow.

Southwest's also doing something that would've been unthinkable a few years ago — they're reportedly eyeing the Boeing 787 for long-haul flying, out of four possible U.S. cities.

This is a company that spent fifty years flying nothing but 737s, point to point, no assigned seats, no fuss. Now they've added assigned seating, they're talking premium cabins, and maybe widebodies and international long-haul next. Southwest is turning into exactly the kind of hybrid airline it used to make fun of everyone else for being.

Whether that's smart or an identity crisis kind of depends on whether they can make long-haul economics work. That's a totally different cost structure than flying Dallas to Houston five times a day.

Last one — United has hundreds of brand-new lie-flat business class seats sitting around that it literally cannot use.

Here's why. They were built for the 737 MAX 10, which was supposed to be flying by now. Boeing's certification delays pushed that timeline out again, and it turns out you can't just bolt a MAX 10 seat onto a different airplane — the dimensions and certification don't carry over.

So United's basically sitting on a very expensive storage problem. It's a pretty perfect example of how Boeing's delays don't just hurt Boeing. They create weird downstream chaos for every airline that ordered the plane and planned a cabin around it.
