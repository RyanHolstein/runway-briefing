Episode Title: NTSB Details the Miami Crash, Delta's Scary Descent, and American Kills First Class (Sort Of)
Episode Summary: NTSB briefs reporters on the fatal Prime Air 767 crash we covered yesterday, and it's grim. A Delta 737 makes an emergency dive from 35,000 feet after a depressurization scare over the Mojave. Plus, American Airlines is killing off its actual first class cabin while still honoring $250,000 lifetime passes for it, and Delta keeps quietly abandoning JFK even as Frontier heads for the exit entirely.

Hey, welcome to Runway Briefing for Tuesday, September 8th. Let's get into it.

So, update on something we covered yesterday — that Amazon Prime Air 767 crash in Miami. NTSB chair Jennifer Homendy briefed reporters Monday, and the details are rough. The plane struck two vehicles on the ground, a van and an SUV, after running off the runway. Five people are dead. Homendy's word for the scene was "utter devastation," which, coming from the head of the NTSB, is not a phrase you hear thrown around lightly. Investigators are still working out why a cargo jet ended up off the runway and into traffic, but this is shaping up to be one of the deadlier ground incidents in recent memory involving a commercial-category aircraft. We'll keep following this one as the investigation moves forward, because right now we've got the what, not the why.

Okay, next one, and this is a wild listen if you find the audio. Delta flight 2311, LAX to Salt Lake City, Sunday night. Cruising at 35,000 feet, and the plane suddenly depressurizes. Masks drop, pilots strap into those full-face oxygen masks, and air traffic control hears them basically gasping through the radio asking to dive to 9,000 feet immediately. That's called a rapid descent, and it's exactly what it sounds like — get down fast, because at cruising altitude you've got maybe a minute of useful consciousness before hypoxia kicks in. The plane diverted safely to Las Vegas, everyone's fine, but 181 people just found out what it feels like to think about the actual atmosphere for a second. The jet is 14 years old, which isn't ancient, but it's a reminder that pressurization systems are exactly the kind of thing that fails without warning. Delta says they're investigating.

Now, American Airlines, and this one's a little funny in a corporate-nonsense way. Remember Flagship First? That's American's actual first class — above business, the good stuff. It's going away. But here's the kicker — back in the day, American sold literal lifetime passes for this cabin, some going for around 250 grand, and they're still honoring those. So there are people out there who paid a quarter million dollars for a seat that technically no longer exists. American says don't worry, you can still use it on business class and domestic first. Which, fine, but that's a little like buying lifetime access to a steakhouse and finding out they've closed the dining room and now just hand you a gift card to Chili's. Meanwhile, Cranky Flier's been asking a good question about where American's actually putting its new A321XLRs — the plan is mostly Europe out of Philly and JFK, but Latin America has always been American's real strength since they bought up those old Eastern and Braniff routes back in 1990. Seems like a weird time to chase Vienna when your backyard in Latin America is looking a little exposed.

And JFK keeps getting smaller for Delta. Four more domestic routes are getting cut this fall, including Milwaukee, and that's on top of Houston, Memphis and St. Louis disappearing earlier this year. Add in Delta walking away from Brussels after 34 years and ditching a planned Geneva return, and you've got a pattern — Delta is consolidating international flying over Atlanta and treating JFK like a boutique operation instead of a full hub. Which is interesting timing, because Frontier is packing up from JFK entirely. So you've got one airline going more selective and another going nowhere near it. JFK's future looks a lot more legacy-carrier, a lot less budget.

Quick one before we go — somebody stuffed an entire bag's worth of clothes into her leggings to dodge a carry-on fee, and it worked, she boarded no problem. Which, look, I don't love encouraging that, but also, airlines invented this problem by charging for a bag the size of a backpack. Innovation goes both ways.
